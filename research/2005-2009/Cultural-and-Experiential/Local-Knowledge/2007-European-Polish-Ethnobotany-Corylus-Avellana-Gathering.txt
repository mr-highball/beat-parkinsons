Title: European rural ethnobotanical record of wild Corylus avellana hazelnuts as staple raw food versus leaf/bark folk medicine
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1859986/
Source kind: Ethnographic review
Publication date: 2007
Date basis: Peer-reviewed ethnobotanical review published in 2007 (Luczaj & Szymanski, Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine), synthesizing 42 ethnographic sources from the late 18th century to 2007.
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Customary rural food staple (raw kernels and desserts) with no neurological or Parkinson disease treatment claim; distinguishes nut kernels from astringent hazel leaf/bark folk remedies.
Authenticity: Traceable open-access peer-reviewed ethnobotanical review (Luczaj & Szymanski, 2007, PMID:17433114, PMC1859986, DOI:10.1186/1746-4269-3-17), cross-referenced with European leaf/bark traditional uses (PMID:30096647, PMID:27319139).
Efficacy evidence: Dietary and ethnobotanical baseline context only; does not establish clinical efficacy for any Parkinson disease symptom.

Community and practice context: Rural Polish and broader Central/Northern European wild-plant gathering and folk-medicine traditions documented from the late 18th century through 2007 across 42 ethnographic and botanical sources.

Documented practices and plant-part separation: Luczaj & Szymanski (2007, PMC1859986) record Corylus avellana L. (common hazel; Polish: leszczyna pospolita / orzech laskowy) as one of the 15 enduring wild fruits and seeds gathered across all Polish regions, eaten raw or incorporated into desserts (while catkins/inflorescences were historically used only as famine-bread admixtures). By contrast, European folk herbal medicine reviews (PMID:30096647; PMID:27319139) document that traditional medicinal infusions for venous insufficiency, varicose veins, hemorrhoids, phlebitis, and edema used tannin-rich hazel LEAVES and BARK rather than edible nut kernels.

Historical attribution verification: Round 4 source checking also confirmed that modern secondary herbal blogs attributing "oil of hazelnut for cold griefs of the nerves, joints, and palsies" to Nicholas Culpeper's Complete Herbal (1653) misattribute a passage that in Culpeper's text belongs to Bay-Tree/Bay Berries rather than Hazel-nut. Preserving accurate plant-part and textual boundaries avoids spurious historical PD claims.
