Date basis: Publication year of the article that reports the interviews. Archaeological dates in the paper concern botanical specimens and do not date the individual practices in Table 3.
Passage: Table 3 and the surrounding account of interviews on sunflower names and practices.
Community/context: Interview-based accounts reported by Lentz and colleagues in Mexico. The reported practices are attributed to the communities in the table.
Exposure and reported symptom: Otomi informants described culinary sunflower seed oil, seed foods, other medicinal uses of the plant and ceremonial use of flowers. The oil extraction method was unspecified. Separate table entries describe sunflower seed preparations for stomach complaints and other plant uses for cough or rheumatism. The paper does not assign those outcomes to Otomi culinary oil use and gives no account of memory, executive function or Parkinson symptoms.
Reported direction: Unspecified for culinary sunflower oil and neurological symptoms.
Evidence and limits: The interviews identify culturally specific uses and distinguish oil, seeds, flowers and other plant material. They provide no measured executive result, comparator, treatment denominator, exposure grade or verified Parkinson diagnosis.
Authenticity: A traceable primary publication describes interviews and reports the distinct practices.
Efficacy evidence: Treatment effect and historical continuity for any specific symptom are undetermined.
Related research: research/2005-2009/Source-Text/2008-PMID18678826-MEMO-Sunflower-Executive-Function.txt and research/2025-2029/Source-Text/2025-PMID39973828-Sunflower-Recognition-Memory.txt are separate experimental sources with different exposures and outcomes.
Access and follow-up: Article and table inspected. The source does not provide individual interview transcripts or enough preparation detail to identify cold pressing. Search separately for first-person cognitive or Parkinson accounts and for preparation documentation.
