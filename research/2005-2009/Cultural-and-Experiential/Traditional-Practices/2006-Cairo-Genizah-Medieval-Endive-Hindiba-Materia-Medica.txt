Title: Endive (hindiba) among top prescribed botanicals in medieval Cairo Genizah medical prescriptions
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16893620/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2006
Date basis: Peer-reviewed historical ethnopharmacology publication year (medieval sources date to 10th-14th centuries CE).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Historically prescribed across medieval Mediterranean and Levantine humoral medicine for liver complaints, digestive/constipation purgatives, fevers, and urinary ailments; no historical link to Parkinson disease.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed archival reconstruction of 141 practical medieval medical prescriptions from the Taylor-Schechter Cairo Genizah collection at Cambridge University Library (Lev & Amar 2006, J Ethnopharmacol, PMID:16893620).
Efficacy evidence: Historical materia medica documentation only; does not establish modern clinical efficacy for Parkinson symptoms, and medieval Arabic hindiba historically spanned both Cichorium endivia and Cichorium intybus.

Archival analysis of 141 practical medical prescriptions from the medieval Jewish community of Cairo (10th-14th centuries CE, Taylor-Schechter Genizah collection) identified 242 medicinal substances, of which endive (hindiba, Cichorium spp.) ranked among the five most frequently prescribed botanicals alongside rose, myrobalan, sugar, and almonds (Lev & Amar 2006, PMID:16893620). Prescriptions in this Mediterranean-Levantine humoral tradition commonly addressed digestive complaints (including constipation/purgatives and stomach ailments), liver conditions, fevers, headaches, eye diseases, and urinary tract complaints. Botanical and historical distinction: Medieval term hindiba encompassed both cultivated endive (Cichorium endivia) and wild/cultivated chicory (Cichorium intybus), and publication date (2006) is distinct from the medieval manuscript dates (10th-14th centuries). No Parkinson-specific clinical inference can be drawn.
