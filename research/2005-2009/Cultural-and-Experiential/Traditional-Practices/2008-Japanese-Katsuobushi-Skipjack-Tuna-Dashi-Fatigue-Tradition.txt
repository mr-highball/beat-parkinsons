Title: Japanese culinary and folk-remedy tradition of katsuobushi dashi (dried skipjack tuna broth) for fatigue and circulation
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2581762/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2008-11-01
Date basis: Published open-access clinical/traditional-context report (Umeki et al., J Clin Biochem Nutr 2008; PMC2581762), corroborated by Kuroda & Nozawa 2008 (PMID:18724004) and Kadooka et al. 2020 (PMID:32009019) on traditional Japanese katsuobushi fermentation.
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional Japanese culinary and folk-remedy accounts regard katsuobushi dashi (aqueous broth brewed from boiled, smoked, Aspergillus-fermented dried skipjack tuna, Katsuwonus pelamis) as restorative against daily fatigue and poor peripheral circulation; does not address canned or plain cooked tuna fillets in Parkinson disease.
Authenticity: Traceable peer-reviewed publications describing authentic Japanese culinary fermentation and household use, inspected during Round 7 review.
Efficacy evidence: Traditional culinary record only; while small Ajinomoto-affiliated crossover trials in non-PD Japanese adults report modest POMS fatigue/tension-anxiety gains with dashi broth, preparation differs sharply from canned or cooked tuna fillets and provides no direct PD efficacy evidence.

In Japanese culinary heritage, katsuobushi is produced from skipjack tuna (Katsuwonus pelamis, the same species predominant in commercial canned light tuna) through boiling, wood-smoking, sun-drying, and surface culturing with Aspergillus molds (karebushi; documented microbiologically in Kadooka et al. 2020, PMID:32009019). Shaved katsuobushi steeped in hot water yields dashi (dried-bonito broth), a daily umami soup base traditionally regarded in Japan as a household tonic for recovery from fatigue and sluggish peripheral circulation (Umeki et al. 2008, PMC2581762; Kuroda & Nozawa 2008, PMID:18724004). Although modern Japanese crossover trials in fatigued healthy and elderly adults tested this broth against placebo or water, traditional katsuobushi processing alters aroma volatiles and concentrates water-soluble histidine and imidazole dipeptides relative to ordinary cooked or canned tuna fillets, and traditional accounts do not diagnose or evaluate Parkinson disease symptoms.
