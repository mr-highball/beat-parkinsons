Title: Caribbean ethnobotanical use of Citrus paradisi (grapefruit) for hypertension and cooling remedies in Trinidad and Tobago
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1624823/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2006
Date basis: Publication year of Lans 2006 ethnobotanical fieldwork study (PMID:17040567, PMC1624823; interviews conducted 1996-2000).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional Caribbean folk medicine lists Citrus paradisi among plants used for hypertension and hot/cold cooling regimens, without Parkinson-specific efficacy claims; modern pharmacovigilance warns of serious CYP3A4 medication interactions.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed ethnobotanical fieldwork published in Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine (Lans 2006, PMID:17040567, PMC1624823).
Efficacy evidence: Ethnobotanical survey context only; does not establish safe or effective Parkinson symptom treatment and underscores the need to warn clinicians about blood-pressure and CYP3A4 drug interactions.

Citrus x paradisi originated in Barbados as a Caribbean hybrid of sweet orange and pomelo. In ethnobotanical interviews conducted across Trinidad and Tobago (1996-2000; Lans 2006, PMC1624823), respondents listed Citrus paradisi among folk remedies used for hypertension within the regional Caribbean hot/cold cooling framework (aimed at reducing systemic heat and supporting blood-pressure balance), while separate West African ethnobotanical reports document Yoruba seed decoctions for metabolic ailments (Adeneye 2008, PMID:19391322). Neither tradition addresses Parkinson disease, and modern clinical pharmacology demonstrates that raw grapefruit furanocoumarins irreversibly inhibit intestinal CYP3A4, risking excessive blood-pressure lowering and drug toxicity when combined with antihypertensives or Parkinson medications.
