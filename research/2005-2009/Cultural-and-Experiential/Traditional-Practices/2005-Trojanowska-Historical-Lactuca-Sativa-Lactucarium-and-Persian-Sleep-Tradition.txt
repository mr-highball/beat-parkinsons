Title: Historical European, Galenic, and Traditional Persian Medicine sedative records of Lactuca sativa vs wild Lactuca virosa lactucarium and modern lettuce-water folklore
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17153150/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2005
Date basis: Peer-reviewed historical article publication year (Trojanowska 2005, Kwart Hist Nauki Tech, PMID:17153150), cross-referenced with Traditional Persian Medicine ethnobotanical records (Pour et al. 2018, PMID:30172900) and Bologna folk records (Chiocchio et al. 2024, PMC10936038).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Longstanding traditional sedative, sleep-promoting, cooling, and mild analgesic attribution across Greco-Roman, Persian, and 19th-century European medical texts, with historical taxonomic confusion between culinary Lactuca sativa and toxic wild Lactuca virosa and skeptical modern appraisal of kitchen-steeped lettuce water.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed medical history paper (Trojanowska 2005, PMID:17153150) inspected via PubMed abstract alongside peer-reviewed Persian ethnopharmacology (Pour 2018, PMID:30172900) and Bologna field ethnobotany (PMC10936038) during Round 6 review.
Efficacy evidence: Historical and traditional materia medica context only; documents why lettuce entered sleep research while underscoring that historical lactucarium (dried milky sap, often from wild L. virosa) and Persian lettuce-seed remedies differ fundamentally from raw culinary romaine salads or viral social-media steeped lettuce water.

Trojanowska (2005, Kwartalnik Historii Nauki i Techniki, PMID:17153150) traces European medical writings from classical antiquity through 19th-century Polish pharmacopoeias showing that both wild poisonous lettuce (Lactuca virosa) and cultivated garden lettuce (Lactuca sativa / L. hortensis)—especially their dried milky latex, lactucarium—were historically used as mild, opium-like sedatives and analgesics, while noting widespread historical ambiguity over which Lactuca species was cited. Parallel Traditional Persian Medicine texts similarly prescribed Lactuca sativa (kahu) preparations—predominantly lettuce seeds and seed oils—for insomnia and internal heat (Pour et al. 2018, PMID:30172900), while Northern Italian Bologna informants recorded L. sativa salads and soups for soothing stomachache and leaf decoctions for cough and toothache (Chiocchio et al. 2024, PMC10936038). In the 2020s, social-media "lettuce water" trends revived this folklore by steeping raw romaine leaves in hot water before bed, with mixed public anecdotal reports and nutritionist cautions that kitchen infusions deliver minimal lactucin compared with standardized high-lactucin cultivar extracts (Son et al. 2025, PMC12251321).
