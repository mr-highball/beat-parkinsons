Title: European, Mediterranean, and Iranian traditional practices for brewed peppermint leaf tea (Mentha x piperita)
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16767798/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2006
Date basis: Peer-reviewed pharmacognosy review publication years (McKay & Blumberg, Phytother Res 2006, PMID:16767798; Kazemi et al., Crit Rev Food Sci Nutr 2025, PMID:38168664); traditional use spans antiquity to modern household practice.
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Favorable traditional carminative, antispasmodic, mild digestive, and mental-refreshment reputation, paired with traditional/clinical cautions against gastroesophageal reflux aggravation; no traditional PD clinical claim.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed phytotherapy and food-science reviews inspected during Round 6 review (PMID:16767798; PMID:38168664).
Efficacy evidence: Traditional herbal infusion documentation only; does not establish clinical efficacy in Parkinson's disease and highlights compositional differences between brewed aqueous leaf tea and enteric-coated peppermint oil capsules.

Brewed infusions of fresh or dried Mentha x piperita L. (peppermint) leaves have been used for centuries across European herbalism, North African mint-tea culture, and Iranian traditional medicine as a soothing carminative and digestive tea for stomach cramps, flatulence, indigestion, nausea, and mild mental fatigue (McKay & Blumberg, 2006, PMID:16767798; Kazemi et al., 2025, PMID:38168664). Modern phytochemical profiling distinguishes aqueous brewed peppermint leaf tea (rich in water-soluble rosmarinic acid and flavonoid glycosides such as eriocitrin, with modest steam-volatile menthol transfer) from steam-distilled peppermint essential oil (~35-60% menthol) delivered in enteric-coated capsules. Traditional and modern clinical guidance consistently warn that oral peppermint relaxes the lower esophageal sphincter and can trigger or worsen heartburn and acid reflux, while high polyphenol content in brewed tea inhibits non-heme iron absorption (Hurrell et al., 1999, PMID:10999016). Traditional use does not verify PD efficacy.
