Bang JS, Oh DH, Choi HM, et al. Anti-inflammatory and antiarthritic effects of piperine in human interleukin 1beta-stimulated fibroblast-like synoviocytes and in rat arthritis models. Arthritis Research & Therapy. Published March 30, 2009; volume 11, issue 2, article R49. DOI 10.1186/ar2662. PMID 19327174; PMCID PMC2688199.
Original full body: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2688199/

Factual extract of Materials and methods and Results, Figure 4: In vitro studies used synoviocytes obtained from three people with rheumatoid arthritis; these were laboratory cell experiments, not treatment of those people. Rat experiments compared isolated piperine dissolved in corn oil with vehicle and drug controls in carrageenan-induced paw hyperalgesia and ankle arthritis. For acute paw pressure, 10 rats per group received oral piperine 20 or 100 mg/kg one hour before challenge; the comparison drug was celecoxib 100 mg/kg. The measured response was tolerated pressure, not a patient pain rating. Authors report increased tolerated pressure, more pronounced at 100 mg/kg.

For induced ankle arthritis, treatment began the day after induction and continued orally once daily for eight days; piperine at 20 or 100 mg/kg was compared with vehicle and prednisolone 10 mg/kg. Outcomes included ankle swelling, vocalizations during flexion and extension, and percentage of hind-limb weight borne on the affected limb. Authors report less swelling and fewer evoked vocalizations at 100 mg/kg, with vocalizations decreasing from day 5; 20 mg/kg had little effect on vocalizations. The affected-limb weight ratio recovered on days 8 and 9 in both piperine groups, although the paper describes the 20 mg/kg analgesic effect as not statistically significant. Rat movement/weight-bearing in inflamed paws is an indirect pain-related measure rather than a Parkinson gait or motor outcome. Figure 4 presents graphs without tabulated exact individual estimates.

Piperine is a single constituent, not consumed cracked black pepper; the models and doses do not provide evidence of clinical Parkinson pain outcomes or a dose correspondence for food.
