Geerligs and colleagues assessed four Parkinson patients with a history of visual hallucinations before, during and after short intravenous apomorphine exposure. During exposure, contrast sensitivity improved and attention performance worsened. Other tested visual, cognition and motor measures did not show a statistically significant change. The investigators state that the short observation and infrequent events prevented scoring the effect on visual hallucinations directly. The contrasting proxy responses therefore do not establish a change in visual episode frequency. The open self-comparison studied a medicine, with no culinary fig exposure.
