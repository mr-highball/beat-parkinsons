Title: Aging and nocturnal driving: better with coffee or a nap? A randomized study
Source: https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article-pdf/30/12/1808/13663746/sleep-30-12-1808.pdf
PMID: 18246990
PMCID: PMC2276135
DOI: 10.1093/sleep/30.12.1808
Publication date: 2007-12-01
Date basis: Sleep journal article and issue.
Checked: 2026-11-24
Review depth: Original publisher PDF, methods, results and cohort-lineage statement.

The 24-person crossover included 12 young and 12 middle-aged healthy participants. The Results section explicitly identifies the young group as already reported in Philip 2006 (PMID 16754920). Compare these reports as a shared family even though the follow-up adds a middle-aged group.

The coffee comparison used 125 mL caffeinated instant coffee (200 mg caffeine) and decaffeinated instant coffee (15 mg). On an instantaneous fatigue VAS (0 to 100), means were 50 (SD 19) after caffeinated coffee, 58 (SD 23) after decaf, 58 (SD 19) after a nap, and 35 (SD 19) in the daytime reference. The paper reports no clear difference in fatigue across the nighttime conditions and no age-by-condition interaction. It separately reports lower Karolinska sleepiness with caffeinated coffee and fewer line crossings. The favorable raw fatigue difference is imprecise and lacks a separately reported paired interval.

The coffee preparation, nighttime driving context and acute fatigue in healthy volunteers limit transfer to persistent Parkinson fatigue (claim 0121). There is no tested worsening effect in a Parkinson cohort.
