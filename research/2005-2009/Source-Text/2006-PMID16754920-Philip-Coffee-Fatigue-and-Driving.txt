Title: The effects of coffee and napping on nighttime highway driving: a randomized trial

Twelve healthy young men completed randomized crossover sessions with caffeinated instant coffee, decaffeinated instant coffee, and a separate nap. Both coffees had the same 125 mL volume. Reported caffeine contents were 200 mg and 15 mg. The participant-rated fatigue scale was scored before driving. Subjective sleepiness while driving was evaluated separately.

The three nighttime conditions did not differ detectably on the 100-mm fatigue scale (Figure 1). In contrast, sleepiness during nighttime driving was lower with caffeinated than decaffeinated coffee (p = 0.004), and video-assessed line crossings also favored coffee. These outcomes have different meanings and should remain separate in the evidence ledger.

The 2007 Sagaspe paper explicitly identifies these 12 participants as its previously published young group and adds 12 middle-aged participants. Treat these records as one family for combined conclusions and the 2010 Ker Cochrane review as a citation trail. The instant-coffee exposure and experimentally induced acute sleepiness do not resolve the culinary brewed-coffee and Parkinson fatigue comparison (claim 0121).
