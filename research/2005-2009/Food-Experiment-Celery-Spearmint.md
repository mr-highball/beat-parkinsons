# Food experiment evidence for celery leaf and spearmint

## 2005 Fresh celery leaf fractions and anesthetic sleep in mice

Bursać and colleagues extracted five fractions from fresh authenticated celery leaves. After intraperitoneal administration, all five fractions lengthened ketamine-induced sleep in BALB/c mice. Only the ethyl acetate celery fraction significantly delayed onset of induced sleep. The study addresses anesthesia in mice, with no waking sleepiness, REM electromyography or dream-enactment outcome. It provides a leaf-specific preparation lead for 0143 and 0389. Published in the 2005 volume and posted online October 7, 2008. [Primary report, Figures 1–2](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/13880200500406677)

## 2009 Brewed mint and reported smell in nasal polyposis

Goronfolah and colleagues reported 22 completers in a randomized crossover of high- and low-rosmarinic-acid mint teas, with four weeks per treatment and a four-week washout. The conference report gives no significant diary difference in ability to smell or sleep. Connelly’s doctoral thesis identifies both teas as spearmint. This supplies a brewed-species lead for 0645 and sleep context for 0140 and 0373. It studied adults with bilateral nasal polyps using diary ratings and two active mint preparations. It did not measure Parkinson olfaction or daytime sleepiness. The matching registry record NCT00465543 was posted in 2007 and has no posted results. ## 2005 Supermint oil and flatulence-related pain after cesarean section

Fazel's double-blind comparison in the Babol University journal reports lower visual pain scores for oral Supermint oil than placebo after 40, 60 and 120 minutes (p < .001). The Persian abstract lists 107 participants overall but gives 37 placebo and 60 active, which sum to 97. The trial measured acute postoperative pain after a concentrated oil preparation. The product species and denominators need verification from the full report. The journal record indexes the paper in 2005, volume 7 issue 1, pages 28–33. ## A three-extract IBS trial and abdominal pain

First published online July 26, 2006. Vejdani and colleagues randomized 32 irritable bowel syndrome patients, 14 to Carmint and 18 to placebo. Participants also received loperamide or psyllium according to bowel subtype. The original Table 2 gives baseline mean abdominal pain or discomfort severity of 1.8 in both groups on the 0–3 scale. Page 1504 reports means of 0.83 versus 1.38 at midpoint (p=.13) and 0.28 versus 1.50 at eight weeks (p=.016). In the final week there were 0.42 versus 2.25 pain days (p=.001). Page 1506 reports an inconclusive between-group comparison for overall IBS symptom relief.

The extract contains lemon balm, spearmint and coriander. Four participants withdrew early and 28 supplied follow-up questionnaires. The paper describes intention-to-treat analysis but does not establish exact denominators for each endpoint or how missing observations were handled. It separately reports two placebo discontinuations for adverse events. The mixture and cohort leave fresh spearmint and Parkinson pain unresolved.
