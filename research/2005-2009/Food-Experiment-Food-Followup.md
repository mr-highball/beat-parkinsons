# Food experiment follow-up evidence

## 2007 Colombian patient history and diet texture

Perea-Sasiaín’s essay in the June 2007 issue follows one woman with onset of finger tremor at 84 and a ten-year course. The account attributes increasing swallowing difficulty in part to reflux esophagitis and increasing constipation in part to a soft, low-residue diet. Evening levodopa/carbidopa was associated with better sleep in the account. No food, preparation, control meal or swallowing or constipation scale is specified. This is the same woman described with expanded food history in the 2014 paper. The essay’s proposed disease mechanism was a hypothesis. ## S-methyl-L-cysteine added to feed in a fly model

In November 2007 Wassef and colleagues reported that adding isolated S-methyl-L-cysteine to feed from eclosion delayed age-related climbing deterioration in flies expressing human alpha-synuclein. Figure 4 separates the supplement and control-feed curves and reports climbing and spontaneous activity. The trial did not feed the flies cabbage, turnips or garlic, foods listed beside the compound in a later citation table. The model reports no human Parkinson symptom measurements. [Primary article, Methods and Figure 4](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/jneuro/27/47/12808.full.pdf) · [Later citation table and correction](../2010-2014/Food-Experiment-Food-Followup.md#two-dietary-case-histories-from-colombia)

## Food texture and weight loss in a Swedish Parkinson’s cohort

Birgitta Lorefält’s doctoral thesis was publicly defended on May 20, 2005. Its abstract describes a prospective study beginning with 28 people with Parkinson’s and 28 matched controls. Twenty-six pairs completed follow-up, and 19 people with Parkinson’s lost weight between the two final assessments. More people with Parkinson’s had dysphagia at both assessments, and solid-food intake declined. The public thesis record supplies no patient food diaries, named ingredient data or swallowing-test thresholds. The Swedish National Library lists the 2005 dissertation in its catalog. People with Parkinson’s who lost weight had more dysphagia than their controls. They consumed less solid food and fluid at both visits. This was an observational comparison of weight-loss groups and food consistency. The abstract supplies no named food, preparation, test threshold or before-and-after baked-potato outcome for `0290`. ## Foods recovered in a fatal-choking autopsy series

Wick, Gilbert, and Byard reviewed 44 fatal food asphyxia cases from Adelaide, including one person with Parkinson’s disease. The publisher’s Results preview identifies an 11-month-old infant as the grape case. A later forensic review lists potato chips and banana among materials recovered across the series, with no case-level attribution to the person with Parkinson’s. The University of Adelaide record supplies the abstract and its public repository API lists only a license bundle. The full Results and any case-level tables remain unavailable. The endpoint was fatal obstruction, which gives no comparative swallowing-difficulty severity estimate for baked potato `0290`. ## Baked sweet potato in a Japanese swallowing questionnaire

In 2007 Hiro Ogoshi reproduced food rankings from an earlier questionnaire of 358 older adults and 243 middle-aged adults. ## Carnosine supplementation alongside levodopa

Boldyrev and colleagues presented an adjunct carnosine report in 2007 and published the controlled pilot on August 27, 2008. The author-shared full article confirms that 20 participants received 1.5 g/day of acid-resistant Sevitine carnosine tablets plus usual therapy and 16 received usual therapy alone for 30 days. The clinician rating the UPDRS was unaware of treatment. The paper does not describe random allocation. Table 1 gives a pooled initial mean of 38.9 (SE 2.5) and follow-up means of 24.9 (SE 2.1) with carnosine and 32.5 (SE 2.0) with usual therapy alone (reported between-group p = .02). In Table 2, between-group comparisons favored carnosine for rapid alternating hand movements (p = .015) and leg agility (p = .005), while hand and leg rigidity were inconclusive (p = .077 and .073). The study gives no separately measured or reported fatigue outcome. The isolated tablet exposure and endpoints leave poached chicken and fatigue `0348` unresolved. ## Date, fig, and olive diets in a mouse pain-threshold study

Zafari Zangeneh, Moezi, and Amir Zargar assigned 24 laboratory mice to four groups of six. The date group received about 1.5 g of dried Zahedi dates per mouse daily with ordinary feed for two months. The other groups received dried figs, olives, or ordinary feed alone. The Persian results report shorter hot-plate response latency at month two versus month one in the date group, a direction consistent with greater sensitivity to heat. That change and the comparisons among the three fruits and the control were statistically inconclusive. The English abstract on p. 158 also reports no clear effect on pain threshold. The ordinary mice and evoked heat response limit applicability to pain severity in people with Parkinson’s for claim `0502`. [Publisher record, DOI 10.22092/ijmapr.2009.7186](https://ijmapr.areeo.ac.ir/article_7186.html) · [Full report, pp. 151–152 and 154–155, Figure 3](https://ijmapr.areeo.ac.ir/article_7186_829d0e1fded951260df5c529fd2bc537.pdf#page=6)

## Low-protein replacement foods and levodopa off time

Barichella and colleagues analyzed 18 people with Parkinson’s and motor fluctuations in a randomized single-blind crossover comparing two months of a balanced diet with two months of a diet using specially formulated low-protein products. Mean total off time was 164 versus 271 minutes on the low-protein-product and balanced diets, respectively. Mean post-meal off time was 49 versus 79 minutes. A separate six-person study from the group found 3.5 versus 5 hours of daily off time with and without low-protein products and 6 versus 4.5 hours of on time with dyskinesia. The studies concern levodopa response under dietary regimens. They supply no treatment contrast for individual foods such as the roasted potato, raw pear, or raw apricot in `0020`, `0667`, or `0723`. [Primary crossover, PMID 16773618](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16773618/) · [Local full text of the six-person study](Source-Text/PMC2413077.txt)

## Semisolid yogurt as a gastric-emptying test meal

Thomaides and colleagues gave the same labeled semisolid yogurt meal to 12 people with Parkinson’s, 12 with multiple system atrophy, and 12 controls. Gastric half-emptying time averaged 90.6 minutes in the Parkinson’s group and 46.2 minutes in controls. This was a physiological comparison between patient groups using a common test meal. It does not identify an effect of yogurt against another food. Frozen claim `0832` asks about handwriting after strained yogurt. [Primary report, PMID 15795792](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15795792/)

## Reported chocolate consumption and depressive symptoms

Wolz and colleagues analyzed questionnaires from 274 people with Parkinson’s and 234 controls. Reported chocolate consumption was higher in the Parkinson’s group, and the authors found the difference independent of depressive symptoms measured by BDI-I. This was a food-consumption comparison by diagnosis. It provides no treatment estimate for plain dark chocolate or for anxiety, voice volume, or speech intelligibility in frozen claims `0100`, `0660`, and `0703`. ## Banana juice and levodopa exposure in rats

In a December 2005 pharmacokinetic comparison, Table 1 gives mean 0–240 minute levodopa exposure of 1,882.8 µg·min/mL with levodopa alone, 933.5 with fresh banana juice, and 1,724.8 with the commercial banana beverage. Three rats contributed to each group. The fresh-juice contrast was statistically clear, and the commercial-beverage contrast was inconclusive. The study measured drug exposure, with no symptom outcome. Its discussion cites a July–August 2005 Kyowa Hakko report of a separate compatibility test. The rat study authors report that 56.2% of the initial levodopa remained at 30 minutes and 47.0% at one hour when the dissolved granule formulation was mixed with fresh banana and water. The original manufacturer bulletin remains unavailable. ## Doparl granule compatibility tables in the 2008 product form

A manufacturer interview form dated October 2008 and preserved by Tokyo University of Pharmacy and Life Sciences gives separate rows for directly mixing plain Doparl fine granules with banana and water or banana and milk. Relative to the concentration at mixing, **56.2% and 40.0%** remained in the water mixture at 30 and 60 minutes, and **63.4% and 47.0%** remained in the milk mixture. Apple with milk and grapes showed smaller declines, to 90.8% and 83.5% at one hour. The text reports no levodopa loss with milk, tomato, grapefruit, orange, plain yogurt, or kudzu gruel in this test. The document’s first public upload date is unverified. [2008 product interview form, printed p. 11, PDF p. 14](https://www.ps.toyaku.ac.jp/~kosugi/zemi2010/iform/Levodopa.pdf#page=14)

Ogo et al. quote 56.2% and 47.0% for a water mixture from the unrecovered 2005 safety bulletin. The two values appear in different rows of the 2008 table. The product designation also differs between the descriptions, so whether they reflect the same experimental data or a reporting difference remains open. These are bench measurements. The 2008 form supplies no clinical concentration or symptom estimate. ## Furushima reports on fruit and levodopa compatibility

In poster P-062, Furushima and colleagues describe black vomit after a one-year-old with epileptic encephalopathy and dystonia took levodopa with banana and milk. They then used HPLC to measure drug remaining after direct mixing with foods. In blended banana and milk, 47% remained at one hour and 0% at 24 hours. Milk alone did not change the drug content or color. The corresponding values were 90% and 45% with apple and 84% and 69% with grape. The authors also tested orange, grapefruit, tomato, yogurt, and kudzu gruel and reported loss with banana, apple, and grape. They report similar findings with levodopa plus benserazide. The case measured neither a change in levodopa exposure nor a change in dystonia, and the poster supplies no Parkinson symptom data. The scan is dated May 2006, although the J-STAGE index gives a November issue date. The same six authors published a short report on compatibility with banana, apple and grape in March 2007. The 2007 abstract and full text require authenticated access. [Primary poster P-062, printed p. ## Extreme banana intake and circulating dopamine in an adolescent case

Tazoe and colleagues’ December 2006 online report describes an adolescent with anorexia nervosa who ate bananas almost exclusively for over two years. During the period of severe dietary restriction, whole-blood dopamine rose from 11 to 210 ng/mL, and the authors observed dysthymia. These findings improved as other foods returned to the diet. The case concerned an extreme exposure, severe nutritional restriction and peripheral dopamine. It supplies no Parkinson-specific data on levodopa, apathy, anxiety, or dyskinesia. [Primary case report, PMID 17194559](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17194559/)

## Aspiration pneumonia in two active geriatric units

Bouchard, Presse and Ferland reviewed charts for 2,238 discharges from two geriatric units during 2001–2007. Their September 2009 article reports a cumulative aspiration-pneumonia incidence of 1.1%. Dietitians assessed about 80% of the aspiration-pneumonia cases as malnourished. The abstract supplies no ingredient, preparation, Parkinson-specific effect estimate or swallowing severity change.
