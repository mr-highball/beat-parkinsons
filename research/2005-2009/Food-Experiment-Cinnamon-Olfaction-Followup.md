# Cinnamon and olfactory testing, 2005–2009

## Retronasal powder testing in Geneva

Landis and colleagues compared oral retronasal identification using ten aromatized powders with orthonasal Sniffin’ Sticks in 45 people with Parkinson’s disease. Mean identification was 60% with the oral kit and 56% with the nasal kit (p = .15). The primary abstract gives no powder list or item-level results. The University of Geneva repository restricts the article file. A separate Dresden validation paper directly lists commercially ground cinnamon in a 20-item powder kit. That larger kit cannot establish which ten powders Geneva used. Even if cinnamon was included, recognizing a stimulus during a smell test would leave the effect of dietary intake on olfactory impairment in claim `0979` unmeasured. [Primary report, PMID 19753620](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19753620/) · [Geneva repository record](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:19907) · [Dresden validation, Table 2](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaotolaryngology/fullarticle/482813)

## The word cinnamon in an olfactory brain imaging experiment

González and colleagues reported functional brain imaging in 23 participants reading odor-associated words, including cinnamon. The written-word stimuli elicited activity in olfactory regions. Neither cinnamon scent nor cinnamon ingestion was administered. The study gives no Parkinson cohort or clinical olfactory treatment comparison. Dugoua and colleagues explicitly cite this article in the olfactory subsection, Table 3 and bibliography of their later cinnamon review.
