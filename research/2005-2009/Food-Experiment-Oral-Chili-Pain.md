# Oral red pepper and gastrointestinal pain

## Acute red chili in a meal or in capsules

Gonlachanvit and colleagues compared the same standard meal with no chili, with 2 g of commercial ground red chili mixed into the food, and with 2 g chili in capsules. The 20 participants with diarrhea-predominant irritable bowel syndrome took all three conditions in randomized order one week apart. The archived project manuscript describes 1.87 mg capsaicin per 2 g of powder and matching placebo capsules for both unencapsulated meal conditions. It names a manufacturer but supplies no cayenne cultivar identification. Participant-rated peak abdominal pain and burning both increased after the two chili conditions relative to the standard meal (p<.05). The manuscript records pain in 5/20 after the standard meal, 11/20 after chili mixed into food, and 13/20 after chili capsules. The final journal abstract includes 38 healthy comparison participants, whereas the report manuscript used 20. The pain counts refer to the IBS-D sample in the earlier report. The journal reports similar symptom severity across the two chili preparations. The published result addresses acute visceral symptoms in IBS-D. ## Three-day red chili and rectal urgency thresholds

In a separate 18-person healthy-volunteer crossover, participants received 5 g/day of ground Capsicum frutescens in capsules for three days, matched placebo capsules, or the chili capsules with intravenous granisetron. The chili and oral placebo comparisons included intravenous saline. Mean first, moderate, and severe rectal urgency thresholds were 18, 24, and 38 mmHg with chili compared with 22, 31, and 45 mmHg with placebo (p<.01). The authors report that nobody reached the pain threshold in the tested range of balloon distensions. The article supports an effect on urgency in this assay.
