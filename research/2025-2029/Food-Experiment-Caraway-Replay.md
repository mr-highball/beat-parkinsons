# Caraway oil in rat movement and mouse feeding experiments

## Oral caraway oil and rat locomotor counts

Auti and Kulkarni characterized a commercial caraway oil that contained about 55% carvone. They administered it orally in an aluminum chloride rat model of neurotoxicity. Both doses increased actophotometer locomotor counts relative to the aluminum chloride-only group on days 21 and 42. Figure 5 and the Results report p < .001 for each dose at both times. Aqueous extracts, injected S-(+)-carvone and whole culinary caraway are different preparations. The test measured aggregate movement rather than tremor, dystonia, handwriting or voluntary effort, and was not a Parkinson model. ## Inhaled caraway essential oil and mouse feeding

Ogawa and colleagues obtained caraway essential oil by hydrodistilling *Carum carvi* seeds, with carvone comprising 82.82% of the separated oil by GC peak area. In a feeding task, fasted male ddY mice exposed to the oil vapor consumed more food at two tested concentrations than mice exposed to the carrier. The researchers separately tested d-carvone and d-limonene. This is a specific appetitive response with an inhaled exposure and does not establish a generalized motivation or clinical apathy effect after consuming toasted caraway. ## Caraway oil odor ratings and separate cognitive language testing

Rosa and colleagues recruited 402 adults for age-related sensory and cognitive testing. A 59-person younger and middle-aged subset rated the odor of caraway and rosemary oil for intensity, pleasantness and familiarity and supplied verbal descriptors. The cohort excluded neurodegenerative disease. The MoCA language subscore was part of separate cognitive analyses and was not reported as an effect of caraway exposure. Odor descriptions do not score either speech intelligibility or vocal loudness.
