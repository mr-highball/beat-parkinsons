# Black tea, walking and tremor

## Habitual caffeine and bradykinesia progression in a PPMI preprint

The September 2026 Research Square preprint grouped 261 people with idiopathic Parkinson disease by observed caffeine intake. It reports an association with faster bradykinesia and rigidity progression among 207 with one to four years of recorded follow-up. It used generalized estimating equations and separately reports a striatal transporter mediation analysis. The publisher body was inaccessible at review. The caffeine sources, intake definition, adjusted estimates and control for confounders remain unverified. This observational progression signal conflicts with the short randomized isolated-caffeine symptom score finding. [Original preprint](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-11054916/v1) · [Indexed original abstract](https://sciety.org/articles/activity/10.21203/rs.3.rs-11054916/v1)

## Black tea and repeated motor composite scores in LRRK2 Parkinson disease

The June 12, 2025 article by Lüth and colleagues used the Parkinson Disease Risk Factor Questionnaire. The binary black-tea item compares people who recalled drinking caffeinated black tea at least weekly for at least six months at any point in life with other respondents. It supplies no brew method, volume or confirmed postdiagnosis dosing. In the 42-person Fox Insight LRRK2 analysis, regular black tea was associated with lower log-transformed MDS-UPDRS Part II totals across visits (β=−0.51, SE 0.22, p=.028). The sex-adjusted estimate was −0.53 (SE 0.23, p=.028). The corresponding idiopathic Parkinson estimates were 0.005 (p=.872) in PPMI-Online and 0.01 (p=.898) in Fox Insight. The fixed-effects model gives a difference in composite score level across visits and specifies no tea-by-time interaction for comparing progression rates. Tea analyses were exploratory and did not adjust for multiple comparisons. These analyses provide no separate walking or tremor estimate. MDS-UPDRS II includes daily-living tremor and walking or balance items, and examination-based rest tremor is scored separately in Part III.

The discussion reports a separate PPMI Non-Online LRRK2 sample of 39 people, with a similar composite association (β=−0.41, SE 0.18, p=.026). Supplementary Table 4 describes LRRK2-PD in its caption but labels its sample row iPD, with 11 tea participants and 25 comparison participants. The counts sum to 36. The material does not clarify whether the label is erroneous or whether three participants lacked exposure data. Supplementary Table 4 and Figure S2 give composite scores. ## Lithuanian black tea intake and motor examination

Guk and colleagues recruited 59 Parkinson patients and 54 healthy controls for a 2026 case-control study. Twelve-month food-frequency recall yielded median black tea intake of 26.67 mL/day (IQR 0–200) in the Parkinson group versus 13.33 (0–80) in controls (p=.008, Supplementary Table 2). Black tea, green tea and herbal tea were separate items. The neurologist performed MDS-UPDRS Parts I–IV in the best medicated ON state. The motor analyses use total Part III and a Mediterranean diet score. The authors give no black-tea estimate for an individual motor outcome. The intake comparison distinguishes people with and without Parkinson disease and supplies no within-Parkinson symptom contrast or standard brewing method. ## Caffeine and theanine measured in black tea infusions

2025-06-30. Ayakdaş and Ağagündüz used chromatography to compare brewed teas. Their serving-form comparison found black tea cup-sized bags averaged 39 ± 9.9 mg caffeine per 200 mL and loose-leaf samples 24 ± 7.2 mg under the reported controlled brewing conditions. Brew time and format affect the measured content and black tea also contains theanine. The isolated caffeine Parkinson comparison used 200 or 400 mg daily. This supports caffeine as one possible link to the beverage and shows why the dose and mixture cannot be assumed comparable. No study participants or motor outcomes were part of the chemistry experiment.
