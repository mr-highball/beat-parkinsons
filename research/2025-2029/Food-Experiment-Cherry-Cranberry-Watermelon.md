# Cherry, cranberry and watermelon follow-up, 2025–2029

## Watermelon beverage and ambulatory blood pressure

Singh and colleagues’ September 2025 pilot used watermelon beverages made from two amounts of fresh flesh and a matched control in 39 adults with elevated blood pressure. The 24-hour systolic changes were −1.8, −1.1 and −3.2 mmHg for control, low and high watermelon doses. No clear group difference occurred for 24-hour, daytime, nighttime or morning-surge pressure. The study gives no posture-specific pressure or orthostatic symptom estimates. ## Cranberry drink and depression in CRANMOOD

Kamarunzaman and colleagues’ April 28, 2026 article reports a 12-week comparison of a polyphenol-rich cranberry drink with placebo in healthy university students. Table 2 gives an adjusted 12-week HADS depression difference of −0.404 points (95% CI −1.88 to 1.08, p = .59). The broad cognitive attention domain did not show a clear difference. Two short-term memory measures did favor the drink in exploratory analyses. The primary mood outcome was inconclusive. The Profile of Mood States depression-dejection subscale was 3.34 points higher in the cranberry arm at week 4 (95% CI 0.526 to 6.16, p = .02). Week 8 and week 12 contrasts were inconclusive. Table 2 identifies week 4 as the first follow-up and models baseline as a covariate. The treatment test was inconclusive (p = .91), with evidence of a treatment-by-visit interaction (p = .02). These outcomes were distinct from HADS depression. These results concern juice and a young healthy population. ## Watermelon juice and locomotion in an anemia model

A University of Benin undergraduate project dated November 2025 entered the repository on April 24, 2026. The author reports more open-field activity after watermelon juice in phenylhydrazine-exposed rats. The methods list 52 rats and also refer to 15 per group except the control, without explicit per-arm accounting. Several figure labels and comparisons are incomplete in the accessible version. The study measures activity in an anemia model and does not test raw watermelon or clinical bradykinesia. ## Cranberry and behavioral tasks in two non-Parkinson rat models

Abo-Saif and colleagues’ April 21, 2025 article reports shorter hidden-platform escape time in cranberry extract-treated diabetic rats. Its methods give 36 rats overall and then four groups of eight. The intervention paragraph also labels the dosed groups differently from the treatment-group definitions. These reporting gaps limit interpretation of the contrast. Tao and colleagues’ May 26, 2025 online publication (2026 issue) used oral cranberry in a quinolinic acid cognitive impairment model and reports better coordination and maze performance. The abstract gives no separate task effect estimates or clear culinary preparation. The studies used different models and neither measures clinical Parkinson attention or bradykinesia.
