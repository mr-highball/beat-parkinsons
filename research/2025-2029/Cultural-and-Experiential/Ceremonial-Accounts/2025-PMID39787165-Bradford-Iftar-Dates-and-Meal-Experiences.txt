Source kind: Ceremonial account

DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0313688
Publication date: 2025-01-09
Date basis: First publication of the study by Latif and colleagues. Recruitment took place in 2019.
Reviewed: 2026-11-20
Observed by: Participants describing iftar in semi-structured interviews and one discussion group reported by the study team.
Reported direction: One participant described dates and water as part of the routine. A different participant attributed less hunger discomfort and fatigue to smaller meals.
Authenticity: Original Bradford qualitative study. The article presents seven individual interviews and one four-person focus group.
Efficacy evidence: Two separate accounts of practice and experience. The study has no fruit-assigned symptom comparison, pain scale, specified date cultivar or Parkinson cohort.

The section about fatigue and stomach discomfort preserves separate accounts. One person described date fruit followed by water at the break of the fast. Another described lighter meals, fruits and fluids and associated smaller portions with less hunger pain and fatigue. The second person's report does not identify date fruit as the ingredient that changed those experiences. Preparation and fruit amounts are unverified.

The narrative concerns fasting routines, meal composition and anticipatory decisions. It supplies context for a possible source of confounding in clinical Ramadan studies, without providing an estimate of dried dates on Parkinson pain or apathy.
