Pecans (Carya illinoinensis) are one of the few major tree nuts indigenous to North America, naturally distributed across the Mississippi and Ohio River valleys, Texas, and northern Mexico (Sandhu et al., 2025, PMC12694537). The English word 'pecan' derives directly from Algonquian language roots (such as Miami-Illinois 'pakaani' and Shawnee 'paka:ni'), referring broadly to hard-shelled nuts requiring a stone to crack. For millennia before European colonization, Indigenous nations across the Mississippi watershed and the American South selectively harvested, stored, traded, and managed wild pecan groves, and many modern cultivars (such as Pawnee, Osage, Choctaw, Kiowa, Cheyenne, and Sioux) retain Indigenous names. Because mature kernels exceed 70% lipids, communities ate pecans raw or pounded them with mortars and pestles into storable nut meals, pastes, and boiled creamy nut 'milks' used to enrich hominy, stews, and breads through winter months. Traditional foodways valued pecans for caloric sustenance, portability, and winter nutrition rather than as a targeted treatment for tremor, bradykinesia, or other Parkinson's symptoms.
