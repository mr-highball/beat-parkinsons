Title: Brazilian abafado and cross-cultural traditional infusion uses of lemongrass (Cymbopogon citratus) for anxiety, sleep, pain, and digestive complaints
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13672496/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2026
Date basis: Peer-reviewed open-access ethnopharmacology publication year (Carrasco-Alfonso et al., Front Pharmacol 2026, PMC13672496), cross-referencing Leite et al. (1986, PMID:2429120), Carlini et al. (1986, PMID:3762195), and Grande-Terre ethnobotanical survey (2023, PMID:36771738).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Widespread folk reputation for calming anxiety, promoting sleep, and relieving digestive or febrile discomfort as an oral leaf infusion, directly contrasted with null double-blind human oral abafado testing (Leite 1986) and high-dose 30-day renal eGFR declines (Ekpenyong 2015).
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed ethnopharmacology records (PMC13672496, PMID:2429120, PMID:3762195, PMID:36771738) documenting binomial identity (Cymbopogon citratus Stapf) and traditional hot-water leaf infusion preparation.
Efficacy evidence: Ethnobotanical folk-use documentation only; classic controlled double-blind human evaluation of traditional Brazilian abafado found no hypnotic or anxiolytic effect compared with matched placebo.

Across Brazil (where boiling-water infusions of fresh or dried Cymbopogon citratus leaves are known as abafado), the Caribbean (Grande-Terre bedtime leaf infusions, PMID:36771738), Africa, and Southeast Asia, lemongrass is widely consumed both as a culinary stalk/leaf flavoring (minced in soups, curries, and stir-fries) and as a household medicinal tea for nervousness, anxiety, insomnia, stomach discomfort, fever, and mild inflammatory pain (Carrasco-Alfonso et al., 2026, PMC13672496; Carlini et al., 1986, PMID:3762195). Importantly, when Brazilian pharmacologists systematically tested traditional oral Cymbopogon citratus abafados under controlled conditions, neither rodent batteries (Carlini et al., 1986, PMID:3762195) nor double-blind placebo-controlled trials in healthy and high-trait-anxiety human volunteers (Leite et al., 1986, PMID:2429120) confirmed anxiolytic or hypnotic efficacy for the oral tea, whereas subsequent 30-day oral leaf-powder infusion trials (2-8 g/day) reported dose- and time-dependent reductions in creatinine clearance and eGFR (Ekpenyong et al., 2015, PMID:25440110). Traditional use should also be strictly separated from inhaled essential-oil aromatherapy and from Melissa officinalis (lemon balm).
