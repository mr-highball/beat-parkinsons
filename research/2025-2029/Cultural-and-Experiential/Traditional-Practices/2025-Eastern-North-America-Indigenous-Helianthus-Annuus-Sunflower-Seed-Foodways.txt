Title: Indigenous Eastern North American domestication and traditional parching, roasting, and meal foodways of Helianthus annuus sunflower seeds
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12161534/
Source kind: Archaeobotanical and ethnobotanical record
Publication date: 2025
Date basis: Publication year of the archaeological charring and domestication study in PLOS ONE (PMID:40504788, PMC12161534), cross-referenced with Adeleke & Babalola 2020 (PMC7500752) and Lentz et al. 2008 Mexican ethnography (PMC2359819); archaeological domestication age (5,000-3,800 BP) is separated from publication date.
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Contextual staple culinary heritage (parched/roasted dehulled kernels, seed meal cakes, porridges, and boiled seed oil); non-seed floral/root infusions are distinct medicinal parts with no Parkinson clinical efficacy evidence.
Authenticity: Traceable peer-reviewed archaeobotanical and ethnobotanical publications (PMC12161534; PMC7500752; PMC2359819) inspected during Round 7 review.
Efficacy evidence: Historical culinary and ethnobotanical context only; provides no controlled clinical evidence for Parkinson motor or non-motor symptom modification.

Community/context: Indigenous Eastern North American and Plains/Southwestern foodways documented in archaeobotanical and ethnobotanical literature (PLOS ONE 2025, PMID:40504788, PMC12161534; complementing Mesoamerican Otomi ethnography in Lentz et al. 2008, PMC2359819).

Exposure and culinary practices: Helianthus annuus L. was domesticated as a major seed-bearing staple in eastern North America approximately 5,000 to 3,800 years ago, evidenced by enlarged charred archaeological achenes and hulled/unhulled kernels. Traditional food preparations centered on hulling, parching or roasting kernels, grinding seeds into nutrient-dense meal/flour for flatbreads, cakes, and porridges, and boiling crushed seeds to skim edible oil. Ethnobotanical surveys also record distinct medicinal applications of non-seed plant parts (such as flower-head teas for respiratory/chest complaints or crushed root/leaf poultices for bites and swellings), which must be kept botanically separate from culinary roasted kernels.

Evidence and limits: Establishes deep culinary history and dehulling/parching traditions for sunflower kernels while confirming the absence of historical or modern controlled Parkinson symptom efficacy evidence.
