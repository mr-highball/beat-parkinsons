Title: Tibetan Manjing, Uyghur Qiamagu, Naxi A Ke, and Alpine European traditional food and ethnomedicinal uses of turnip (Brassica rapa subsp. rapa)
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13703587/
Source kind: Ethnobotanical and traditional-practice scoping synthesis
Publication date: 2026
Date basis: Publication year of Ye et al. 2026 PRISMA-ScR scoping review in Foods (PMID:42940286, PMC13703587); historical Tibetan Four Medical Tantras and Naxi Dongba sutra attributions noted separately from modern review date.
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional food-as-medicine accounts across trans-Himalayan Asia, Xinjiang, Yunnan, and Alpine Europe describe turnip root preparations for high-altitude fatigue/hypoxia (Tibetan Manjing paste), urinary/bladder complaints (Naxi dried A Ke root), cough relief (Aosta Valley sugar-treated slices; Kerman respiratory use), and winter fermented nutrition (Yi yuangen, Eastern Tyrol unsalted fermented root); modern human evidence has not confirmed clinical efficacy.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed open-access PRISMA-ScR scoping review (Ye et al., Foods 2026, PMC13703587) synthesizing 62 documented traditional-use records with voucher-backed botanical separation of Brassica rapa subsp. rapa from rutabaga (B. napus) and Peruvian maca (Lepidium meyenii).
Efficacy evidence: Traditional and ethnomedicinal context only; Ye et al. 2026 explicitly conclude that differences in preparations and null/unreplicated human trials preclude clinical efficacy claims, and no traditional Parkinson treatment exists.

Title and source: Regional culinary and ethnomedicinal practices of turnip (Brassica rapa L. subsp. rapa), synthesized in Ye et al., Foods 2026 (PMID:42940286, PMC13703587, Section 3.2.3 & Figure 5) and Chu et al., J Ethnopharmacol 2017 (PMID:27856303).
Community/context: Across high-altitude and cold-climate agrarian regions of Asia and Europe, the fleshy turnip root serves as both a storable staple vegetable and a traditional household remedy. In the Xizang (Tibet) Autonomous Region, plateau turnip (Manjing, traditionally referenced in the Tibetan medical classic Four Medical Tantras / rGyud bzhi) is decocted and concentrated into medicinal turnip paste credited in traditional texts with "tonic, anti-hypoxia, heat-clearing, and anti-fatigue" actions. In Xinjiang, Qiamagu (qamgur) is integrated into Uyghur stews and dietary therapy. Among Naxi communities in northwestern Yunnan, voucher-verified B. rapa root (Wu Jing / A Ke, linked to Dongba sutras and interviews with 104 folk healers) is eaten raw, boiled, or dried, with dried root taken orally for bladder/urinary complaints. In Liangshan (Sichuan), Ladakh (India), Eastern Tyrol (Austria), and the Aosta Valley (Italy), turnip roots are boiled, fermented without salt or in brine, or macerated with sugar as a household cough syrup.
Botanical separation and limits: Ye et al. 2026 rigorously separate true culinary turnip (Brassica rapa subsp. rapa) from rutabaga/swede (Brassica napus var. napobrassica), leafy B. rapa subspecies (pak choi, Chinese cabbage, komatsuna), and Andean maca (Lepidium meyenii, often misnamed "Peruvian turnip"). Traditional accounts reflect historical cultural beliefs rather than controlled clinical efficacy, and no source documents a verified Parkinson disease symptom outcome.
