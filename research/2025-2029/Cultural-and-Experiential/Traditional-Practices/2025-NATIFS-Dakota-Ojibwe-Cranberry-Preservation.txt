Reported direction: Culinary and preservation use. No symptom outcome is reported.

The Indigenous food organization records Dakota and Ojibwe knowledge of gathering cranberries in autumn, preserving them by sun-drying or cool storage, and eating the berries raw, dried or cooked. The sheet describes combining berries with dried meat and fat in a preserved food and identifies both small and large cranberry species. The account provides preparation context for dried cranberry. It does not distinguish a dose, compare foods, report depression relief or document intake in people with Parkinson disease.

The description comes from a public educational resource about continuing food traditions. The 1932 Ojibwe record documents small cranberry as food and separately records tea use for nausea. The two sources cover related but distinct practices and do not establish a symptom benefit from eating dried fruit.

Authenticity: Original public educational resource issued by an Indigenous food organization.
Efficacy evidence: Food use and preparation observations, with no measured symptom response.
Related research: The 1932 Ojibwe cranberry passages and CRANMOOD (PMID 42134099) document different preparations and contexts.
Access and follow-up: Further historical records could distinguish species, storage preparations and names by community.
