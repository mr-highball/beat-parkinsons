Title: British, European, Cantonese, Thai, and Pakistani raw vs cooked culinary traditions of watercress (Nasturtium officinale)
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12299072/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2025
Date basis: Peer-reviewed open-access narrative review publication year (Maluwa et al., Life 2025, PMC12299072), cross-referencing Aksornthong et al. 2019 (PMID:31353578) and Siddique et al. 2022 (PMC9677399).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Contextual culinary staple (antiscorbutic spring tonic in Europe; cooling slow-simmered broth in Cantonese cooking; stir-fries and soups in Thailand; folk herb in Pakistan) highlighting sharp preparation differences between raw chewing and thermal cooking; no Parkinson efficacy claim.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed food-science and ethnobotanical literature inspected during Round 7 review (PMC12299072; PMID:31353578; PMC9677399; PMID:32086800).
Efficacy evidence: Culinary and ethnobotanical context only; demonstrates heat inactivation of myrosinase in cooked dishes and documents vitamin K1/warfarin and CYP enzyme considerations without establishing PD symptom efficacy.

In British and Western European food heritage, fresh raw watercress served historically as a peppery spring tonic and working-class antiscorbutic green eaten with bread, in salads, or pureed into soups, whereas East and Southeast Asian cuisines frequently consume Nasturtium officinale cooked—including Cantonese slow-simmered watercress pork-bone soup traditionally regarded as cooling for respiratory dryness, and Thai Betong watercress stir-fries and hot broths (Maluwa et al., 2025, PMC12299072; Aksornthong et al., 2019, PMID:31353578). Ethnobotanical surveys in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan (Siddique et al., 2022, PMC9677399) likewise rank Nasturtium officinale among high-use-value wild culinary-medicinal plants. Two practical biomedical boundaries separate these culinary traditions: first, chewing raw watercress activates endogenous myrosinase to convert gluconasturtiin into phenethyl isothiocyanate (PEITC), whereas cooking heat inactivates myrosinase and substantially blunts PEITC-mediated bioactivity (Aksornthong et al., 2019); second, raw watercress requires safe cultivated sourcing and washing to avoid aquatic trematodes, while both raw and cooked watercress supply high vitamin K1 (~250 ug/100 g fresh) relevant to warfarin anticoagulation stability and cruciferous CYP2E1/CYP1A2 drug-metabolism interactions. Neither European nor Asian culinary traditions claim clinical relief of Parkinson disease motor or non-motor symptoms.
