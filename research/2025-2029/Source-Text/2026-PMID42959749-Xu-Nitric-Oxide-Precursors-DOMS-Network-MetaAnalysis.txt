Primary Abstract:
BACKGROUND: Delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS) commonly follows high-intensity or unaccustomed exercise. Nitric oxide (NO) precursors may reduce DOMS, but their effects remain uncertain. We conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis to evaluate NO precursor interventions across recovery time points. METHODS: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and CENTRAL were searched to February 2026 for randomized controlled trials in healthy adults. Interventions were classified into four nodes: beetroot-derived nitrate, watermelon-derived citrulline, synthetic or isolated amino acid precursors (L-arginine and citrulline malate), and watermelon plus added citrulline. Random-effects network meta-analyses were conducted separately at 24, 48, and 72 h. Effects were expressed as standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs); SUCRA rankings were exploratory. Risk of bias and evidence certainty were assessed using RoB 2 and CINeMA, with subgroup and sensitivity analyses examining robustness. RESULTS: Nineteen trials involving 356 participants were included. At 24 h, watermelon plus added citrulline (SMD = -0.97, 95% CI -1.64 to -0.31) and beetroot-derived nitrate (SMD = -0.67, 95% CI -1.14 to -0.20) significantly reduced DOMS vs. placebo. At 48 h, beetroot-derived nitrate was the only intervention with a significant benefit (SMD = -1.18, 95% CI -1.91 to -0.45). No active intervention differed significantly from placebo at 72 h, while evidence for differences between active interventions was limited, with only watermelon plus added citrulline showing a significant advantage over synthetic or isolated amino acid precursors at 24 h. Beetroot-derived nitrate significantly reduced DOMS vs. placebo in the trained subgroup but not in the untrained subgroup. Sensitivity analyses generally supported the findings, although the beetroot-derived nitrate estimate was sensitive to some restrictions related to the exercise-induced muscle damage model. CONCLUSION: NO precursor supplementation may reduce post-exercise DOMS at specific recovery time points, particularly with beetroot-derived nitrate and watermelon plus added citrulline. However, evidence for differences between active interventions remains limited, and the sparse network, substantial heterogeneity, and low certainty of evidence preclude firm clinical conclusions. [Methods] This systematic review and network meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 and the PRISMA-NMA extension to ensure clear and rigorous reporting throughout the study. To improve transparency, the review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420261373938) (26, 27). We searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) from inception to February 2026 for randomized controlled trials examining the effects of NO precursor interventions delivered either in food-based forms or as synthetic or isolated compounds on post-exercise delayed onset muscle soreness. Two reviewers independently performed the database search, screened titles and abstracts, and assessed full texts against predefined eligibility criteria. Disagreements were resolved by discussion, with input from a third reviewer when required. To minimize the risk of missing relevant studies, we also manually screened ACSM and ECSS annual meeting abstracts, searched ClinicalTrials.gov, and checked the reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews and meta-analyses. These sources were used only as supplementary checks to identify potentially eligible trials not captured by the database searches, and studies were included only when sufficient full-text data were available for extraction. The database search strategies are presented in Appendix 1. We included full-text randomized controlled trials that examined the effects of eligible NO precursor interventions on delayed onset muscle soreness after exercise. Participants were healthy adults aged ≥18 years, with no prespecified upper age limit, who completed an acute exercise protocol designed to induce EIMD and subsequent DOMS. [Results] A total of 2,548 records were identified, including 579 from PubMed, 520 from the Cochrane Library, 1,169 from Embase, and 280 from Web of Science. No additional unique potentially eligible records were identified from the supplementary sources, and none entered the deduplication or screening process. After 1,379 duplicates were removed, 1,169 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 1,086 were excluded because they were not relevant to the review question. The full texts of 83 articles were then assessed for eligibility. Sixty-four studies were excluded for the following reasons: the intervention did not meet the inclusion criteria, or the independent effect of the plant-derived nitric oxide precursor could not be isolated from a combined intervention (n = 38); the exercise-induced muscle damage model was not eligible, or the study was not conducted in a post-exercise recovery setting (n = 7); outcome data were insufficient, not relevant, or could not be extracted (n = 11); or the full text was unavailable, or the data were not extractable (n = 8). [Discussion] This network meta-analysis suggests that the effects of nitric oxide precursor interventions on post-exercise DOMS may differ across recovery time points and intervention types. Statistically significant benefits vs. placebo were observed for watermelon plus added citrulline and beetroot-derived nitrate at 24 h, and for beetroot-derived nitrate at 48 h, whereas no active intervention showed a significant benefit at 72 h. Evidence for differences between active interventions was limited, with only one statistically significant network comparison at 24 h, favoring watermelon plus added citrulline over synthetic or isolated amino acid precursors. This finding should be interpreted cautiously given the limited direct head-to-head evidence. SUCRA rankings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory rather than as evidence of superiority. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses generally supported the primary findings, although some variation was observed across participant groups and exercise models.
