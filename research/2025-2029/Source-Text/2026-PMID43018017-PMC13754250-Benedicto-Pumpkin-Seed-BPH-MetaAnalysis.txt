Primary Abstract:
PURPOSE: To compare pumpkin seed (Cucurbita pepo) with placebo and, separately, with alpha-blockers in men with benign prostatic hyperplasia-related lower urinary tract symptoms. METHODS: Five databases were searched to 30 April 2025 for randomized controlled trials (RCTs). The primary outcome was change in international prostate symptom score (IPSS), and secondary outcomes included maximum urinary flow (Qmax), prostate volume, prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and adverse events. Each comparator was analyzed separately by random-effects meta-analysis, with RoB 2 for risk of bias. RESULTS: Eight RCTs (2,170 men) were included. Versus placebo, pumpkin seed reduced IPSS at three months (mean difference -0.62, 95% confidence interval [95%CI] -1.15 to -0.09) but not at six months; versus alpha-blockers there was no difference (1.91, IC95% -0.24-4.07). Qmax improved versus placebo only; no other outcome differed from alpha-blockers. Adverse events were fewer with pumpkin seed in one head-to-head trial (odds ratio = 0.21, IC95% 0.07-0.63). CONCLUSION: Pumpkin seed offers at most a small sub-clinical IPSS benefit over placebo, not maintained at six months, and no significant difference from alpha-blockers. Certainty was low to very low; equivalence is not established.

Verified Round 6 Primary Facts & Critical Appraisal:
Systematic review and meta-analysis (Acta Cir Bras 2026; PMC13754250) of 8 RCTs (n=2,170 men with BPH/LUTS) evaluating Cucurbita pepo preparations (pooling whole seeds, pumpkin seed oil, and extracts) vs placebo and vs alpha-blockers. Versus placebo: pooled IPSS reduction at 3 months was -0.62 points (95% CI -1.15 to -0.09, I2=0%, below 3-point MCID) and was NOT maintained at 6 months (MD +0.16, 95% CI -1.98 to +2.31, I2=84.3%; very low certainty). Versus alpha-blockers: MD +1.91 (95% CI -0.24 to +4.07, low certainty; equivalence not established). Single-trial Qmax improved vs placebo (+2.80 mL/s, 95% CI 1.26 to 4.34, from Hong 2009). Critical adversarial finding: collapses Vahlensieck whole-seed and null-extract arms and highlights high heterogeneity, small trial sizes, and low certainty.

[Methods] This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted according to the Cochrane Handbook and reported per Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020. The completed PRISMA 2020 checklist and full search strategies together with the extracted dataset and the analysis code, is deposited in the Zenodo repository 10 . The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261445031; https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/display_record.php?RecordID=1445031). As the study used only published, aggregate data, institutional ethics approval and informed consent were not required. We searched PubMed, Embase, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar from inception to 30 April 2025 using the strategy: (“pumpkin seed oil” OR “pumpkin seed extract” OR “Cucurbita pepo” OR “pumpkin seeds”) AND (“benign prostatic hyperplasia” OR BPH OR “lower urinary tract symptoms” OR LUTS). Database-specific adaptations of this strategy are reported as supplementary material 10 . Reference lists of included studies and prior reviews were hand-searched; no additional eligible records were identified. Full reports were sought for every record that was not excluded at title and abstract screening. Part of the yield from the Cochrane Library (CENTRAL) and Google Scholar consists of records for which no full report exists—trial-registry entries, conference abstracts, theses, book chapters and product monographs—, and these were counted as reports not retrieved in the flow diagram. The composition of the non-retrieved set and a formal assessment of its potential impact on the findings are reported as supplementary material 10 . [Results] The search retrieved 615 records from five databases (PubMed 65, Embase 197, Scopus 89, Cochrane Library 164, Google Scholar 100). After removal of 287 duplicates, 328 records were screened at title and abstract level, and 233 were excluded. Among the 95 reports sought for retrieval, 51 could not be retrieved; 44 full texts were assessed for eligibility, and 36 were excluded (15 reviews, five non-comparative studies, 15 different interventions, one non-randomized design), leaving eight RCTs 12-19 included in the meta-analysis 10 . One study initially judged eligible, by Fusco et al. 20 , was excluded during data extraction because its intervention was a multi-ingredient product based on Cucurbita maxima—not Cucurbita pepo—combined with capsicum, polygonum, and vitamins, and therefore did not meet the pumpkin-seed intervention criterion 10 . A second study, Krakhotkin et al. 21 , was excluded because it was a prospective non-randomized cohort rather than an RCT; as this review was restricted to RCTs, it was not retained for narrative description or risk-of-bias assessment 10 (Fig. 1). In total, 1,322 men received pumpkin seed oil/extract and 848 received placebo or an alpha-blocker; mean age was 62 years old. [Discussion] In this systematic review of eight RCTs (2,170 men), pumpkin seed (C. pepo) produced only a small, sub-clinical reduction in IPSS versus placebo at three months (MD = -0.62 point, below the ~3-point MCID), which was no longer detectable at six months, and no statistically significant difference versus alpha-blockers, although the subgroup contrast with placebo was itself significant (p = 0.025), consistent with a small, non-equivalent advantage of alpha-blockers over pumpkin seed on this outcome rather than proof that the two are interchangeable. A Qmax advantage over placebo was seen in a single trial but not against alpha-blockers. For prostate volume, PVR, and quality of life, no clinically relevant differences emerged when each comparator was examined separately; the PSA estimate versus alpha-blockers was likewise non-significant but rested on only two small trials and was materially affected by an implausible control standard deviation in one of them (see section “Limitations”).
