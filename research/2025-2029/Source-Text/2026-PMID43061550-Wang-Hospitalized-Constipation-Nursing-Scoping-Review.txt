Primary Abstract:
Constipation is a common yet frequently overlooked problem among hospitalized patients and is associated with reduced quality of life, prolonged hospital stays, and increased healthcare costs. Nurses play a central role in the prevention and management of constipation through both pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions. However, evidence regarding nurse-led interventions is scattered across different clinical settings and study designs. This scoping review aimed to map and synthesize the available evidence on nursing interventions for the prevention and management of constipation in hospitalized patients. This scoping review was conducted following a five-stage methodological framework. A comprehensive search of PubMed, MEDLINE, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library was performed from January 2000 to November 2025. Grey literature sources were also searched. Studies conducted in hospital settings that examined nurse-led interventions for preventing or treating constipation were included, regardless of study design. Data were extracted and summarized descriptively, and methodological quality was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT). Twelve studies met the inclusion criteria and were conducted across various countries and inpatient clinical settings, including orthopedic, cardiac, oncology, rehabilitation, and intensive care units, involving more than one thousand patients overall. Nurse-led interventions included non-pharmacological approaches such as abdominal massage, reflexology, acupressure, auricular therapy, dietary or fiber-based strategies, and structured bowel management protocols. Overall, most included studies reported improvements in constipation-related outcomes following nurse-led interventions, although findings varied across intervention types and study settings. Adverse effects were rarely reported and no serious harms were identified. The available evidence indicates that a range of nurse-led interventions have been implemented and have been associated with favorable outcomes in individual studies; however, further high-quality research is required before conclusions regarding effectiveness can be drawn.

[Methods] This scoping review was conducted following the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley and further refined by the Joanna Briggs Institute. The review process consisted of five stages: (1) identifying the research question, (2) identifying relevant studies, (3) selecting studies based on predefined eligibility criteria, (4) charting the data, and (5) collating, summarizing, and reporting the results.24 The guiding research question was: “What nurse-led interventions have been implemented for managing constipation among hospitalized patients?” This question was formulated to capture both the range of nursing interventions examined in the literature and their reported constipation-related outcomes. The primary purpose of this scoping review was to map and characterize the available evidence, including intervention types, implementation approaches, clinical settings, and reported outcomes, rather than to establish the effectiveness or comparative superiority of individual interventions. Importantly, the investigation or implementation of an intervention does not necessarily indicate that its effectiveness has been established. Therefore, both favorable and non-significant findings were considered to provide a balanced overview of the existing evidence and identify areas requiring further investigation. Prior to initiating the review process, a comprehensive search strategy was developed, eligibility criteria were defined, standardized data-charting forms were created, and procedures for data synthesis were established. No review protocol was prospectively registered for this scoping review. [Results] A total of 12 studies met the predefined inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis. These studies were published over a broad period, ranging from 2001 to 2025, reflecting sustained research interest in nurse-led interventions for constipation management among hospitalized patients. The included studies originated from a diverse range of countries across Asia, Europe, North America, and the Middle East, demonstrating international engagement in nursing strategies for bowel care. Study designs included randomized controlled trials and quasi-experimental studies, with randomized designs representing most of the evidence base. The total number of participants across all included studies exceeded one thousand patients, with individual sample sizes ranging from small single-center trials (approximately 30 participants) to large randomized studies involving more than 300 patients. All included studies were conducted in hospital-based inpatient settings, including orthopedic postoperative wards, critical care units, oncology surgical departments, and stroke rehabilitation units, reflecting the relevance of constipation management across different inpatient specialties. All included studies involved hospitalized adult patients aged 18 years or older, including older adults, who were experiencing or at increased risk of constipation due to surgery, immobility, critical illness, or underlying medical conditions. Orthopedic postoperative patients constituted the largest subgroup,10,12,13,18,26,28,29 reflecting the high prevalence of bowel dysfunction associated with surgery, analgesic use, and reduced mobility. [Discussion] The present scoping review provides a comprehensive overview of the published literature on nurse-led interventions for constipation management among hospitalized adults. The identified evidence encompasses a broad range of interventions implemented across diverse inpatient settings, including physical, dietary, educational, and protocol-based approaches, with a predominance of non-pharmacological strategies integrated into routine nursing care. The included studies were characterized by considerable methodological and clinical heterogeneity with respect to study design, intervention delivery, and outcome assessment. Accordingly, the findings should be understood as a descriptive synthesis of the existing literature that outlines the scope and characteristics of nurse-led interventions and highlights areas where further research is needed. One of the most prominent observations emerging from this review is the predominance of non-pharmacological interventions, particularly abdominal massage, reflexology, acupressure, and related tactile therapies.
