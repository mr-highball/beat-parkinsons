Primary Abstract:
Trigonelline (TG), a major alkaloid in fenugreek seed and coffee, has anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities. We investigated whether TG ameliorates Parkinson's disease (PD)-related synucleinopathy and whether these effects are associated with inflammatory signaling and gut microbial-metabolic alterations. Two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) was used to examine the relationship between genetically predicted circulating TG and α-synuclein. In vivo, AAV-SynA53T C57BL/6J mice received oral TG (5 mg/kg/day) for 30 days, followed by assessment of motor behavior, pSer129-α-syn pathology, dopaminergic markers, inflammatory readouts, intestinal barrier proteins, 16S rRNA profiles, and colon-content metabolomics. MR supported a potential protective association between genetically predicted serum TG and reduced α-synuclein. TG improved motor performance, reduced midbrain pSer129-α-syn signals, preserved dopaminergic markers, and attenuated inflammatory responses, accompanied by modulation of PI3K/AKT and NF-κB signaling. TG also improved intestinal barrier-associated features and altered the gut microbiota and metabolomic profile. A metabolomic feature tentatively annotated as Ganolucidic acid B was increased following TG treatment and correlated with several taxa enriched in the TG group; conditional network pharmacology suggested that its predicted targets were enriched in PD-relevant inflammatory and PI3K-AKT-related pathways. Overall, TG may ameliorate synucleinopathy-associated phenotypes in AAV-SynA53T mice, accompanied by attenuated inflammation and gut microbiota-metabolism remodeling. Further targeted metabolomic validation and causal intervention studies are warranted to clarify the contributions of specific metabolites and gut-associated mechanisms.

[Materials and Methods] We performed two‐sample MR analysis using summary‐level data from large‐scale, open‐access genome‐wide association study (GWAS) databases. This approach relies on three core assumptions validated through sensitivity analyses: (i) strong association between instrumental variables (IVs) and target exposure (F‐statistic > 10); (ii) exclusion of pleiotropic pathways connecting IVs and outcome independent of exposure; (iii) absence of confounding factors influencing IV‐outcome relationships. Genetic instruments for serum trigonelline (GWAS accession ID: ebi‐a‐GCST90266095, n = 4960) and α‐synuclein levels (GWAS accession ID: GCST90240258, n = 3301) were derived from publicly available databases, including the NHGRI‐EBI GWAS catalog. Genetic analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR package (v0.6.1) in the R environment. Instrumental variables meeting genome‐wide significance (p < 5 × 10−6) were selected through phased screening: (1) LD pruning (r2 < 0.001, 10,000 kb window); (2) exclusion of palindromic SNPs (Auton et al. 2015); (3) F‐statistic > 10 threshold for strength validation (Yao et al. 2023). The specific SNPs used as instrumental variables and their corresponding F‐statistics are provided in Table S1. The IVW method served as the primary analysis, supplemented by four pleiotropy‐robust methods (WME, WM, MR‐Egger, ML). Comprehensive sensitivity validation included: Cochran's Q test for heterogeneity (p > 0.05); MR‐Egger intercept/MR‐PRESSO for horizontal pleiotropy (p > 0.05); leave‐one‐out stability assessment. Statistically significant associations (p < 0.05) were interpreted through odds ratios (OR) with 95% CIs, where OR > 1 indicates positive association and CI exclusion of 1 confirms significance. [Results] To establish a causal foundation for the potential neuroprotective effects of TG, we employed a two‐sample Mendelian randomization (MR) approach to investigate the relationship between genetically predicted serum TG levels and α‐synuclein pathology. The primary analysis using the Inverse Variance Weighted (IVW) method revealed a significant protective causal association, indicating that higher genetically predicted TG levels are associated with reduced α‐synuclein levels (OR = 0.858, 95% CI: 0.753–0.977, p = 0.021; Figure S1A). Importantly, this finding was corroborated by the Weighted Median method, which also demonstrated a significant protective effect (OR = 0.818, 95% CI: 0.696–0.962, p = 0.015), suggesting that the causal estimate is robust even if up to 50% of the genetic variants are invalid instruments. The scatter plot further visualized this consistent negative correlation across the selected instrumental variables (Figure S1B). To ensure the reliability of these estimates, we conducted comprehensive sensitivity analyses. We found no evidence of significant heterogeneity among the instrumental variables in either the IVW or MR‐Egger analyses (Cochran's Q test, p > 0.05), implying a consistent effect direction across different genetic loci. Furthermore, horizontal pleiotropy was ruled out, as indicated by the non‐significant intercept in the MR‐Egger regression and the results of the MR‐PRESSO global test (p > 0.05). Finally, the “leave‐one‐out” sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the observed causal association was not driven by any individual single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP), as the omission of individual variants did not alter the overall statistical significance (Figure S1C). [Discussion] TG improved motor performance, preserved dopaminergic markers, and reduced α‐synuclein pathology and inflammation. These changes were accompanied by reduced PI3K/AKT–NF‐κB signaling activity, improved intestinal barrier‐associated features, and altered gut microbial and metabolic profiles. Collectively, our data support TG as a modulator of both central and peripheral pathological changes in AAV‐SynA53T mice. Coffee contains multiple bioactive constituents, among which TG has comparatively stronger evidence in neurodegeneration research. For example, oral TG improved cognition‐related behaviors in Alzheimer's disease (AD) model mice and was detectable in the brain, with mechanistic work implicating processes related to neurite outgrowth (Farid et al. 2020). Recent mechanistic reviews further summarize TG as a candidate with antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and metabolic homeostasis–regulating potential (Liang et al. 2023). Our MR analysis provided supportive genetic evidence for an inverse association between genetically predicted serum TG and α‐synuclein levels, subject to the assumptions of MR (Sanderson et al. 2022).
