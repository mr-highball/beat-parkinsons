ABSTRACT

Purpose

Stroke‐associated pneumonia (SAP) is a common and serious complication of acute ischemic stroke (AIS). The neutrophil percentage‐to‐albumin ratio (NPAR) is a composite inflammatory and nutritional marker derived from routine blood tests. This study investigated the association between NPAR and SAP and evaluated its functional form and incremental discriminatory value.

Patients and Methods

This secondary analysis used a single‐center AIS registry that screened 2150 consecutive admissions between September 2016 and September 2022; 1766 patients comprised the NPAR analytic cohort. Associations between log2‐transformed NPAR and SAP were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression, generalized additive models (GAM), and two‐piecewise logistic regression.

Results

SAP developed in 376 patients (21.3%). NPAR was independently associated with SAP after adjustment for the 18 prespecified covariates in Model 3 (adjusted odds ratio [OR] per doubling of NPAR = 6.14; 95% confidence interval [CI], 3.19–11.82; p < 0.001). Compared with the lowest quartile, the adjusted ORs for SAP were 1.16 (95% CI, 0.68–1.99), 1.72 (95% CI, 1.04–2.83), and 3.48 (95% CI, 2.08–5.83) across increasing NPAR quartiles (p for trend < 0.001). Generalized additive and piecewise models indicated an exploratory non‐linear, threshold‐associated pattern, with a data‐derived inflection point at approximately NPAR = 1.758. In ROC analysis, NPAR showed good discrimination (AUC = 0.785; 95% CI, 0.756–0.814), higher than the A2DS2 score alone (AUC = 0.758; p = 0.120) and lower than CRP alone (AUC = 0.826; p = 0.019). Adding NPAR to A2DS2 increased the apparent in‐sample AUC to 0.821 (p < 0.001), whereas adding NPAR to CRP increased it to 0.842 without a statistically significant increment over CRP alone (p = 0.213); these combination models were not internally validated.

Conclusion

In this retrospective secondary analysis, NPAR was independently and non‐linearly associated with SAP after acute ischemic stroke. Multiple‐imputation analyses supported the robustness of these findings. NPAR provided complementary discriminatory information, particularly when added to A2DS2; however, independent external validation is required before clinical implementation.

1 Introduction

Globally, stroke ranks among the foremost causes of death and permanent disability, with AIS accounting for the majority of stroke cases (Banach et al. 2026; Golubnitschaja et al. 2024). Notwithstanding substantial progress in hyperacute stroke care, in‐hospital complications—most notably stroke‐associated pneumonia (SAP)—remain a major threat to patient recovery (Chaves et al. 2022). Occurring in roughly one‐in‐five to one‐in‐three AIS admissions, SAP prolongs hospitalization and substantially worsens functional prognosis and survival (de Jonge et al. 2022; Finlayson et al. 2011). Timely recognition of patients at elevated risk is accordingly indispensable for implementing targeted prophylactic measures and optimizing clinical outcomes.

A growing body of evidence highlights that post‐stroke immune dysregulation, compounded by nutritional compromise, substantially lowers the threshold for pulmonary infection by dismantling normal host‐defense homeostasis (Wang et al. 2023). Although widely deployed markers including CRP, WBC count, and the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR) capture elements of systemic inflammation, none achieves adequate discriminatory accuracy for SAP in isolation (Wang et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2024). There is therefore a need for novel composite markers that integrate both inflammatory and nutritional dimensions of post‐stroke immune dysfunction.

The neutrophil percentage‐to‐albumin ratio (NPAR), calculated by dividing the neutrophil percentage (NTC%) by serum albumin concentration (g/L), is a readily available composite index derived entirely from standard blood tests (Gong et al. 2020). NPAR integrates two key pathophysiological axes: neutrophilia, reflecting innate immune activation and pro‐inflammatory responses (Thind et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2023); and hypoalbuminemia, reflecting nutritional depletion and impaired immune competence (Lyu et al. 2026). Both components are well‐established determinants of infection susceptibility and poor outcomes in critically ill patients. Beyond stroke, NPAR has been associated with Parkinson disease (Ke et al. 2025) and with pneumonia and poor outcome after spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (Lv et al. 2023). A recent study of 1008 patients with AIS reported that higher admission NPAR was linearly associated with SAP and that the association was stronger in men (Du et al. 2026).

The available evidence remains limited with respect to between‐cohort consistency in the functional form of the NPAR–SAP association. Du et al. reported no statistical evidence of non‐linearity, whereas the present study evaluated a possible threshold‐associated pattern using GAM and two‐piecewise logistic regression (Du et al. 2026). Further evaluation in an independent cohort is warranted because cohort characteristics, biomarker sampling time, SAP event frequency, model specification, and covariate adjustment may influence the observed exposure–response form. The incremental discrimination of NPAR beyond CRP and A2DS2 also remains incompletely characterized.

We therefore designed the present study around three pre‐specified objectives: (i) to determine whether early in‐hospital NPAR is independently associated with SAP after adjustment for established clinical and laboratory confounders; (ii) to characterize the functional form of the NPAR–SAP exposure–response relationship, specifically testing for a threshold‐associated pattern using GAM and two‐piecewise logistic regression; and (iii) to quantify the incremental discriminatory value of NPAR relative to CRP and A2DS2, both individually and in combination. The log2 transformation was chosen over log10 because it produces integer‐valued unit increments that correspond to biologically meaningful doublings of NPAR, facilitating interpretation of OR estimates.

2 Materials and Methods

2.1 Study Design and Participants

This single‐center retrospective study was a secondary analysis of a previously established acute ischemic stroke (AIS) registry at Quzhou People's Hospital, China. The registry screened 2150 consecutive admissions between September 2016 and September 2022. After application of the prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria, 1766 patients comprised the present NPAR analytic cohort and were classified according to the occurrence of SAP within 7 days after stroke onset. No case–control matching was performed, and group differences were addressed using multivariable models. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2023–151), which waived informed consent because the study was retrospective and used de‐identified data.

The same institutional registry and ethics approval have supported related secondary analyses of SAP biomarkers, including the C‐reactive protein‐to‐albumin ratio (CAR), neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII), hemoglobin‐albumin‐lymphocyte‐platelet score (HALP), and platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR), as well as an analysis of CAR in relation to early neurological deterioration. These studies used overlapping or nested analytic subsets and should not be interpreted as independent validation cohorts. Published CAR‐SAP, NLR‐SAP, SII‐SAP, and HALP‐SAP reports are cited here (Duan et al. 2026; Duan et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2026; Zhang et al. 2026); the PLR‐SAP and CAR–early neurological deterioration analyses were under peer review at the time of this revision. The distinct exposure evaluated in the present study is NPAR, together with its non‐linear association with SAP and its comparative and incremental discrimination relative to CRP, SII, and A2DS2.

Inclusion criteria: (1) confirmed diagnosis of AIS within 24 h of symptom onset with admission during the same time window; (2) brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed within 48 h with radiological confirmation of acute infarction; (3) blood samples obtained within 48 h of symptom onset; and (4) complete clinical and laboratory data available for NPAR calculation.

Exclusion criteria: (1) incomplete medical records (n  =  112); (2) missing laboratory data required for NPAR calculation (n  =  69); (3) lost to follow‐up (n  =  35); yielding 1934 patients with complete data. These were further excluded if they had: (4) hemorrhagic transformation (n  =  45); (5) pre‐existing pneumonia at admission (n  =  68); (6) active malignancy (n  =  35); or (7) severe hepatic or renal dysfunction (n  =  20). The final analysis cohort comprised 1766 patients. The patient selection process is illustrated in Figure 1.

2.2 Data Collection and Definitions

Baseline data were retrieved from medical records and included demographic and clinical characteristics at admission: age, sex, smoking status, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, atrial fibrillation (AF), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Stroke severity was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) (Cheng et al. 2023; Johansen et al. 2023; Larsen et al. 2022), swallowing difficulty was evaluated using the Kubota Water Drinking Test (KWDT) (Labeit et al. 2023; Labeit et al. 2024; Luo et al. 2024), and consciousness disturbances were assessed using the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) (Teasdale and Jennett 1974). We also recorded the need for nasal feeding tubes and the presence of consciousness disorders (Bodien et al. 2026). The A2DS2 score (Age, Atrial fibrillation, Dysphagia, Sex, Stroke Severity) was calculated as a validated composite predictor of SAP (Hoffmann et al. 2012).

Fasting venous blood samples were collected by trained nurses on the second morning after admission (approximately 6:00 AM) and processed within 2 h. Among all patients subsequently classified as having SAP, sampling preceded the first documented clinical evidence of pneumonia and antibiotic administration. Laboratory tests included WBC, neutrophil percentage (NTC%), absolute neutrophil count (NTC), lymphocyte count (LTC), platelet count (PLT), albumin (ALB), CRP, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), homocysteine (HCY), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), uric acid (UA), and serum creatinine. NPAR was calculated as NTC%/albumin (g/L) (Gong et al. 2020), and the systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII) was calculated as PLT × NTC/LTC. Albumin was expressed in g/L; most published NPAR studies, including the original report (Gong et al. 2020), express albumin in g/dL, so absolute NPAR values reported here are not directly comparable with those studies. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the CKD‐EPI formula (Khandpur et al. 2022; Tarzi et al. 2025).

SAP diagnosis was determined independently by two attending neurologists following the revised criteria of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the pneumonia recommendations of the Stroke Consensus Group (Smith et al. 2015). Only pneumonia first documented within 7 days after stroke onset was classified as SAP; pneumonia first documented later during hospitalization was not included in the analyzed outcome. Diagnostic criteria required at least one of: fever (>38°C) without alternative cause, abnormal WBC count, or altered mental status in patients ≥70 years; plus at least two respiratory criteria including purulent sputum, new cough or dyspnea, auscultatory findings, or oxygen desaturation. Radiologic confirmation required new or progressive infiltrates on chest CT.

2.3 Statistical Analysis

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared with Student's t‐test; non‐normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi‐square test.

NPAR values were log2‐transformed to reduce skewness and to permit interpretation per doubling. Because HbA1c had 287 missing values among the 1766 eligible patients, 20 imputed datasets (m = 20) were generated using stochastic regression imputation with age, sex, vascular risk factors, admission laboratory indices, stroke severity, thrombolysis, and SAP status as predictors. Thrombolysis was included only in the imputation model and was not a covariate in Model 3. Fully adjusted Model 3, subgroup models, and the segment‐specific coefficients from two‐piecewise logistic regression were estimated in each completed dataset and pooled using Rubin's rules. Descriptive and ROC analyses used the full cohort of 1766 patients. Model covariates were prespecified on clinical grounds and from reported SAP risk factors. VIFs were recalculated for the exact 18‐covariate Model 3 design matrix in each completed dataset; all mean VIFs were <5, with HbA1c having the highest mean VIF (3.070; range, 2.965–3.139; Table S1).

Three logistic regression models were constructed: Model 1, unadjusted; Model 2, adjusted for age and sex; and Model 3, fully adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, atrial fibrillation, COPD, fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, triglycerides, total cholesterol, uric acid, homocysteine, CRP, albumin, eGFR, baseline NIHSS score, and KWDT results (18 covariates). Neither thrombolysis nor GCS was included in Model 3.

A generalized additive model (GAM) with a penalized cubic spline (five basis knots) was fitted in the first completed dataset to visualize the potentially non‐linear relationship between log2‐NPAR and SAP; a pointwise 95% bootstrap band was obtained from 200 resamples. For the two‐piecewise logistic analysis, a common inflection point was selected in the first completed dataset by maximizing the log‐likelihood within the fifth–90th percentile of log2‐NPAR and was then held fixed across all 20 completed datasets. Segment coefficients and standard errors were pooled using Rubin's rules. The difference between the two segment slopes was evaluated with a pooled 1‐degree‐of‐freedom Wald's test. Because the inflection point was selected from one completed dataset, no confidence interval is reported for the threshold and its interpretation is exploratory.

Predictive performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves in the full cohort. AUC confidence intervals and pairwise AUC comparisons were calculated using the DeLong method. Sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were calculated at the Youden‐index cutoff. The NPAR + CRP and NPAR + A2DS2 models were fitted and evaluated in the same cohort; their AUCs and incremental gains are apparent in‐sample estimates and were not included in the bootstrap validation. For each subgroup, a joint interaction model included log2‐NPAR, the subgroup indicator, their product term, and the Model 3 covariates except the stratifying variable. These models were fitted in all 20 completed datasets and pooled using Rubin's rules; interaction p values were obtained from pooled Wald's tests of the product‐term coefficient. No multiplicity adjustment was applied, so subgroup findings were treated as exploratory. Model 3 calibration used predictions averaged across the 20 completed datasets and was examined using a calibration plot and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test. Internal validation was confined to the fully adjusted Model 3 and used 200 bootstrap resamples in the first completed dataset to estimate optimism in the AUC.

3 Results

Among the 1766 AIS patients included in the final analysis (screened from 2150 initially identified patients), 376 (21.3%) developed SAP within 7 days after stroke onset (Table 1). Compared with the non‐SAP group, SAP patients were significantly older (74.49 ± 11.74 vs. 67.84 ± 12.26 years, p < 0.001), and more frequently had atrial fibrillation (35.1% vs. 11.5%) and COPD (15.7% vs. 4.0%, both p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in sex distribution or smoking status.

Laboratory findings revealed markedly elevated inflammatory markers in the SAP group. NPAR was significantly higher in SAP patients compared to non‐SAP patients (2.111 [1.848–2.373] vs. 1.702 [1.502–1.914], p < 0.001). CRP and WBC were also elevated, while albumin was lower in the SAP group (all p < 0.05). Additionally, fasting glucose and homocysteine levels were higher in the SAP group (all p < 0.05).

SAP patients exhibited more severe neurological impairment, as indicated by higher NIHSS scores (5.00 [2.00–13.00] vs. 2.00 [1.00–5.00], p < 0.001), higher A2DS2 scores (4.00 [3.00–7.00] vs. 2.00 [2.00–4.00], p < 0.001), and lower GCS scores (all p < 0.001).

3.2 Baseline Characteristics by NPAR Quartiles

Patient characteristics across NPAR quartiles are shown in Table 2. The incidence of SAP increased markedly across quartiles: 7.0% in Q1 (0.4607 ≤ NPAR ≤ 1.5382), 9.3% in Q2 (1.5382< NPAR ≤ 1.7648), 18.8% in Q3 (1.7648< NPAR ≤ 2.0107), and 50.0% in Q4 (2.0107< NPAR ≤ 4.6128; p < 0.001). Higher NPAR quartiles were associated with increasing prevalence of atrial fibrillation and COPD (both p < 0.001), as well as progressively higher CRP, fasting glucose, and WBC counts. Neurological scores, including NIHSS, A2DS2, and KWDT, increased with higher NPAR quartiles (all p < 0.001).

3.3 Non‐Linear Association and Threshold Effect of NPAR on SAP Risk

A penalized cubic‐spline GAM visualization supported a curved association between log2‐NPAR and SAP risk: the fitted probability was relatively flat at lower NPAR values and increased more steeply above the data‐derived inflection point of approximately 1.758 (Figure 4). Because the curve and cut‐point were derived from the first completed dataset, this pattern should be considered exploratory.

Using the common data‐derived inflection point (NPAR = 1.758), the pooled two‐piecewise model showed no statistically significant association below the inflection point (adjusted OR = 0.61; 95% CI, 0.26–1.43; p = 0.259) and a strong association above it (adjusted OR = 97.42; 95% CI, 32.07–295.91; p < 0.001). The difference between the two segment slopes was supported by a pooled Wald's test (χ 2 = 39.13, 1 df; p < 0.001; Tables 3 and S2).

3.5 Subgroup Analyses

Exploratory subgroup models were fitted in each of the 20 completed datasets and pooled using Rubin's rules (Figure 2). The positive NPAR–SAP association was statistically significant in 11 of 14 strata. Nominal interactions were observed for age (OR = 14.99 [95% CI, 4.39–51.18] for <70 years and 3.82 [1.76–8.30] for ≥70 years; p for interaction = 0.039), sex (OR = 8.93 [3.47–22.99] in men and 3.54 [1.43–8.74] in women; p for interaction = 0.010), and atrial fibrillation (OR = 7.37 [3.34–16.27] without atrial fibrillation and 2.89 [0.81–10.33] with atrial fibrillation; p for interaction = 0.044). Interactions for smoking, hypertension, diabetes, and COPD were not statistically significant. Because no multiplicity adjustment was applied and several strata had wide confidence intervals, these findings are hypothesis‐generating.

3.6 Predictive Performance of NPAR, CRP, and A2DS2 for SAP

Having established the independent association between NPAR and SAP, we next evaluated whether NPAR provided useful discriminatory information. In ROC analysis, NPAR alone achieved an AUC of 0.785 (95% CI, 0.756–0.814), with a sensitivity of 68.6% and specificity of 77.2% (Table 5 and Figure 3). CRP alone showed a higher AUC than NPAR (0.826; 95% CI, 0.800–0.851; p = 0.019), whereas the A2DS2 score alone showed a similar AUC (0.758; 95% CI, 0.729–0.787; p = 0.120). Adding NPAR to CRP increased the apparent in‐sample AUC to 0.842 (95% CI, 0.816–0.868), and adding NPAR to A2DS2 increased it to 0.821 (95% CI, 0.794–0.847). The increase over A2DS2 alone was statistically significant (p < 0.001), whereas the increase over CRP alone was not (p = 0.213). These combination‐model AUCs and incremental gains were not internally validated; bootstrap internal validation was performed only for the fully adjusted Model 3. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test did not detect significant lack of fit in the derivation cohort (χ 2 = 9.37; p = 0.312; Figure 4b and Table S2).

4 Discussion

Our principal finding is that NPAR, an integrated index of neutrophil burden and nutritional status, was independently and non‐linearly associated with SAP in patients with acute ischemic stroke. After multiple imputation of missing HbA1c values, each doubling of NPAR was associated with a 6.14‐fold increase in the odds of SAP, and the highest NPAR quartile had 3.48‐fold higher odds than the lowest quartile. The consistency of the association across linear, quartile‐based, and piecewise models supports NPAR as an informative risk marker within this cohort.

The biological rationale for NPAR may reflect complementary information carried by its two components. Neutrophil percentage may rise after AIS as part of sterile inflammatory and neuroendocrine responses, and excessive neutrophil activation could contribute to pulmonary vulnerability through oxidative and extracellular‐trap pathways (Vallés et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2023). Lower albumin may reflect reduced nutritional reserve and antioxidant capacity and could be associated with impaired host defense (Lv et al. 2023; Thind et al. 2024). These pathways were not measured directly and therefore remain plausible explanations rather than demonstrated mechanisms. Because NPAR itself was not included in the VIF analysis, the albumin VIF cannot establish weak correlation or biological independence between the components. Instead, the similar estimate after removing albumin from the adjustment set (OR = 6.97; 95% CI, 3.94–12.33) suggests that the association was not solely attributable to simultaneous adjustment for albumin.

The NPAR–SAP association appeared non‐linear. Risk increased most steeply above the data‐derived inflection point of approximately 1.758. In the piecewise model, the association below this value was not statistically significant, whereas the association above it was strong (adjusted OR = 97.42; 95% CI, 32.07–295.91; p < 0.001). The very large upper‐segment estimate and wide confidence interval may reflect numerical or model instability and should not be interpreted as a stable clinical effect size. Similar results were observed when albumin was removed from the adjustment set, reducing concern that the finding was solely a mathematical consequence of including albumin both in NPAR and as a covariate. Because the inflection point was estimated in a single‐center retrospective cohort, it should be interpreted as an exploratory threshold‐associated pattern rather than a clinically validated decision cutoff.

A recently published AIS cohort study by Du et al. (2026) also found a positive NPAR–SAP association but reported no statistical evidence of non‐linearity (p for nonlinearity = 0.80). That study further identified sex‐related effect modification, with a stronger association in men. Our exploratory subgroup analysis likewise yielded a nominal sex interaction (p for interaction = 0.010), with a stronger association in men, whereas our functional‐form analysis suggested a threshold‐associated pattern. Differences in cohort characteristics, NPAR sampling time, SAP event frequency, modeling strategy, and covariate adjustment may contribute to the differing functional forms, but these explanations cannot be established from the available data. Neither study provides independent validation of the other.

Head‐to‐head ROC comparison suggested that NPAR has complementary discriminatory value within this cohort. NPAR alone showed good discrimination (AUC = 0.785), lower than CRP alone but comparable with A2DS2. Adding NPAR to A2DS2 increased the apparent in‐sample AUC from 0.758 to 0.821, whereas the increase after adding NPAR to CRP was numerically modest and not statistically significant. Because these combination models were developed and evaluated in the same cohort and were not internally validated, the gains may be optimistic. NPAR may therefore be most useful for hypothesis generation alongside clinical risk scores rather than as a replacement for established inflammatory markers.

The exploratory subgroup analyses yielded nominal interactions with age, sex, and atrial fibrillation. The NPAR–SAP association appeared stronger among patients aged <70 years, men, and patients without atrial fibrillation. These patterns may reflect clinical or immunological differences, but they were identified across multiple comparisons, several stratum‐specific estimates were imprecise, and none has been externally validated. They should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than as evidence for subgroup‐specific clinical thresholds.

To contextualize NPAR against another inflammation‐related composite marker evaluated in the same registry, we compared it with SII. NPAR showed a higher AUC than SII (0.785 vs. 0.746; p = 0.001). This within‐cohort comparison supports the relevance of albumin‐adjusted neutrophil burden for SAP discrimination but does not represent external validation.

The present findings should also be interpreted in relation to other biomarker analyses derived from the same institutional AIS registry. Prior reports evaluated CAR, NLR, SII, and HALP in relation to SAP (Duan et al. 2026; Duan et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2026; Zhang et al. 2026), while PLR‐SAP and CAR–early neurological deterioration analyses were under review at the time of this revision. These reports represent related secondary analyses with overlapping or nested patient subsets rather than independent replications. The specific contribution of the current study is the evaluation of NPAR, which integrates neutrophil percentage with albumin, using multiple imputation, non‐linear and piecewise modeling, direct comparison with SII, CRP, and A2DS2, and assessment of incremental discrimination. Accordingly, the findings establish a biomarker‐specific association within a previously reported registry and require validation in an independent external cohort.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single‐center retrospective study. Important SAP determinants, including infarct location and volume, aspiration events, mechanical ventilation, stroke subtype, antibiotic exposure, oral hygiene, pre‐stroke functional status, and frailty, were unavailable or were not included; residual confounding by these factors therefore remains possible. The same institutional registry has also supported other biomarker analyses with overlapping or nested cohorts; consequently, those studies are related secondary analyses rather than independent replications, and their findings may be correlated. Second, SAP diagnosis was based on clinical and radiographic documentation, and microbiological confirmation was not available for all cases. Third, HbA1c had 287 missing values; multiple imputation was used to retain the full cohort in fully adjusted analyses, but this approach assumes that missingness can be explained by observed variables. Fourth, NPAR was measured only once, on the second morning after admission, and dynamic changes during hospitalization were not assessed. Although samples in all patients who developed SAP were obtained before the first documented clinical evidence of pneumonia and before antibiotic administration, this temporal ordering does not exclude the presence of an evolving subclinical infection at sampling. Therefore, part of the observed association may reflect early infection‐related changes rather than only antecedent susceptibility. Fifth, the NPAR + CRP and NPAR + A2DS2 models were developed and evaluated in the same cohort; their AUCs and incremental gains are apparent in‐sample estimates, and only the fully adjusted Model 3 underwent bootstrap internal validation. Finally, independent prospective multicenter validation is required before NPAR can be incorporated into routine SAP risk prediction.

5 Conclusion

In this retrospective secondary analysis of 1766 patients with acute ischemic stroke, NPAR was independently and non‐linearly associated with SAP. Multiple‐imputation analyses supported the robustness of the association after accounting for missing HbA1c values. NPAR provided complementary discriminatory information, with a statistically significant incremental value when added to A2DS2; the incremental improvement beyond CRP alone was not statistically significant. Independent external validation is required before clinical implementation.
