==============================
This study investigated changes in chemical composition, aroma, and color during Sichuan pepper stir-frying (0–10 min, 120 °C), identifying key contributors and a processing window. An integrated strategy combining colorimetry, electronic nose, HS-GC–MS, HPLC–UV, sensory, antioxidant, and molecular docking/dynamics analyses was applied. Color shifted from red to dark brown, with a* peaking at 5 min, while electronic-nose responses were highest at 4–6 min. Among 26 volatile compounds, (+)-limonene showed the highest ROAV (100), whereas eucalyptol declined sharply after 3 min. Molecular docking and dynamics supported stable binding of (+)-limonene to OR1A2. Total flavonoids and sanshools, as well as antioxidant activity, peaked at 5 min, while polyphenols continuously decreased. Multivariate analyses identified seven key contributors (VIP > 1), and correlation analysis showed associations of L* with bioactive constituents and a* with numbing-related constituents and antioxidant activity. 4–5 min was identified as a reference processing window for quality control.

Highlights

• Stir-frying 4–5 min balanced aroma, color, and bioactives in Sichuan pepper.

• L* value correlated with flavonoids/polyphenols, a visual quality indicator.

• (+)-Limonene (ROAV=100) is a key aroma driver and binds stably to OR1A2.

• Total flavonoids and eucalyptol (VIP>1) drive the aroma–color synergy.

• Eucalyptol declines sharply after 3 min, a potential thermal marker.

1 Introduction

Sichuan pepper is the dried mature pericarp of plants belonging to the genus Zanthoxylum in the family Rutaceae. Originating in China, it has a long history of cultivation and is widely distributed across Sichuan, Shaanxi, Gansu, Chongqing, and other regions (Gao et al., 2024). As a multifunctional economic crop that serves medicinal, culinary, and seasoning purposes, Sichuan pepper holds a prominent place in Chinese dietary culture, imparting a unique numbing and aromatic flavor to dishes (Ivane et al., 2022; J. Li et al., 2022). The quality of Sichuan pepper used in food processing is predominantly determined by its flavor and color, both of which are closely associated with its chemical composition (K. Zhang et al., 2026). The Chinese Pharmacopoeia stipulates that stir-fried Sichuan pepper should be processed until it develops a characteristic aroma (Chinese Pharmacopoeia Commission, 2025). This characteristic is primarily derived from volatile constituents such as terpenes, aldehydes, and ketones present in the essential oil, whereas the color is related to pigments, including flavonoids and polyphenols, as well as Maillard reaction and caramelization products formed during stir-frying (Qiu et al., 2025; W. Zhang et al., 2025).

Stir-frying is a critical step in Sichuan pepper processing, as it removes the raw, green odor, triggers and enhances the characteristic numbing and aromatic flavor, and simultaneously improves the color (Zhao et al., 2022). Previous studies have shown that stir-frying markedly affects the volatile profile and aroma characteristics of Sichuan pepper. Li et al. (Z. Li et al., 2019) reported that changes in cooking temperature significantly altered aroma-active compounds, particularly terpenoids such as limonene and myrcene. Liu et al. (Liu et al., 2022) further demonstrated that frying temperature and duration affected the volatile composition and sensory characteristics of Sichuan pepper oil, with changes in linalool and other aroma-related compounds contributing to differences in citrus and floral notes. More recently, Gu et al. (Gu et al., 2025) investigated aroma formation during the frying process and showed that heat treatment reduced fresh, floral, and citrus notes while enhancing roasted and fatty notes, and identified several aroma-active compounds associated with these changes. These studies demonstrate that thermal processing substantially reshapes the aroma profile of Sichuan pepper. However, existing studies on Sichuan pepper stir-frying have mainly focused on individual indicators, such as volatile compounds, numbing substances, aroma, or color, whereas systematic investigations into the coordinated changes and interrelationships among chemical composition, aroma, color, and sensory quality during stir-frying remain limited (Dai et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026).

Chemical composition, aroma, and color, as core quality indicators of Sichuan pepper, do not exist in isolation but are closely interconnected and may change synergistically interrelated (Fei et al., 2025). The volatile compounds in the essential oil not only serve as the material basis of aroma, but their oxidation and decomposition may also affect the color of Sichuan pepper. Flavonoids and polyphenols act both as antioxidant substances and as important pigments that influence color, and they may simultaneously interact with the formation or transformation of volatile constituents (He et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2025). However, systematic studies on the synergistic changes and interrelationships among chemical composition, aroma, and color during stir-frying remain limited. Therefore, this study aimed to elucidate their dynamic changes and interrelationships, identify the key components contributing to quality changes, determine an appropriate stir-frying window, and evaluate the potential of L* and a* as visual indicators of quality changes during stir-frying. By integrating chemical, sensory, and instrumental analyses, this study provides a more comprehensive understanding of quality transformations in Sichuan pepper during stir-frying.

2 Materials and methods

2.1 Materials

Sichuan pepper (the dried mature pericarp of Zanthoxylum bungeanum Maxim., Rutaceae) was purchased from Shaanxi Xingshengde Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Lot No. 20251201; Hancheng, Shaanxi, China) and authenticated by Professor Changli Wang at Shaanxi University of Chinese Medicine.

1,2-Dichlorobenzene (≥ 99.9%, W13876), Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, and DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl, 98%, V93833) were obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. Rutin (≥ 98.0%, PS1930–0100) was supplied by Chengdu Push Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. Hydroxy-α-sanshool (≥ 98.0%, STC5640120), hydroxy-β-sanshool (≥ 98.0%, STC2010110), and xanthoxylin (≥ 98.0%, ST79640120) were purchased from Shanghai Shidande Standard Technical Service Co., Ltd. All other reagents were of analytical grade and obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.

2.2 Preparation of samples with different stir-frying durations

Following the plain stir-frying procedure specified in the General Principles of Processing (0213) of the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2025 Edition), a 26 cm stainless-steel wok was placed on an electric ceramic cooktop with a heating power of 2000 W and preheated until the temperature at the center of the wok bottom reached 120 °C, as monitored by an infrared thermometer. The temperature of 120 °C was selected as a controllable temperature within the reported range for low-heat stir-frying of Sichuan pepper and was maintained using the electric ceramic cooktop. For each stir-frying time point, three independent 50 g batches of Sichuan pepper were prepared separately. Each batch was processed independently under the same stir-frying conditions. For assays involving repeated instrumental measurements, the measurements within each independent batch were averaged to obtain one representative value for that batch. The three independent batch values were then used for statistical analysis. Based on preliminary trials showing distinct changes in color, aroma, and chemical composition during the early and middle stages of stir-frying, a 1 min sampling interval was selected to capture the critical changes around the 5 min stage. For comparison, 50 g of Sichuan pepper was stir-fried in a conventional iron wok over an open flame following the traditional empirical endpoint of “stir-fried until fragrant.” After removal, the samples were naturally cooled to room temperature, ground using a grinder, passed through a 40-mesh sieve, sealed in light-proof bags, and stored at 4 °C until analysis.

2.3 Determination of color values of samples with different stir-frying durations

With reference to a previous study (Wang et al., 2026) and with slight modifications, a CS-520 spectrophotometer (Hangzhou CHNSpec Technology Co., Ltd., China) was calibrated with a standard white tile. Accurately weighed 5 g of Sichuan pepper powder was placed in a petri dish and spread evenly. Three measuring points at different positions were selected, and the L, a, and b* values were recorded; the average values were calculated. The color difference ΔE was computed using the following formula:ΔE=ΔL∗2+Δa∗2+Δb∗2

Where ΔL* = L*1 – L*0,Δa* = a*1 - a*0,Δb* = b*1 - b*0; L*0, a*0, b*0 denote the color parameters of the unstir-fried Sichuan pepper (0 min), whereas L*₁, a₁, and b₁ denote those of the samples at different stir-frying stages.

2.4 Scanning Electron microscopy (SEM) observation of surface morphology

Intact Sichuan pepper pericarps were cut into small pieces, mounted onto a specimen stub using conductive adhesive, and sputter-coated with gold (15 nm). The surface morphology was observed at 500× magnification using a VEGA3 scanning electron microscope (SEM, TESCAN Trading (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) operated at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. Additional higher-magnification images (2000×) were obtained to provide detailed information on surface micropore formation.

2.5 E-nose analysis

With reference to a previous method (Qin et al., 2026) and slight modifications, accurately weighed 1 g of Sichuan pepper powder was accurately weighed, placed into a 20 mL headspace vial, sealed, and equilibrated in a thermostatic water bath at 45 °C for 20 min. Analysis was performed using an iSensortalk N1400 electronic nose (Beijing iSensortech Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) under the following conditions: injection time, 5 s; purge time, 60 s; acquisition time, 120 s; and pump duty cycle, 20%. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and the mean value of the three technical replicates was used for subsequent analysis.

2.6 GC–MS determination of volatile compounds in samples with different stir-frying durations

The volatile compounds of Sichuan pepper were analyzed using headspace sampling coupled with a QP2020 gas chromatograph–mass spectrometer (GC–MS, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan), based on a previously reported procedure (Feng et al., 2024) with minor modifications. For sample preparation, 2 g of powder was mixed with 10 μL of 1,2-dichlorobenzene internal standard solution (10 mg/mL) in a headspace vial and sealed. The headspace parameters were as follows: vial equilibration temperature, 80 °C for 30 min, loop temperature, 140 °C; loop equilibration time, 2 min; and injection time, 3 min. GC separation was carried out on a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) with helium as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The oven temperature program was as follows: 50 °C held for 1 min, then increased to 220 °C at 30 °C/min and held for 1 min, followed by an increase to 280 °C at 5 °C/min and held for 5 min. A 1 μL aliquot was injected with a split ratio of 30:1. Mass spectrometric detection was performed using an electron ionization (EI) source at 70 eV with a solvent delay of 3 min. Qualitative identification of volatile compounds was achieved by matching the acquired mass spectra against those in the NIST 17 library. Their semi-quantitative contents were estimated using 1,2-dichlorobenzene as a single internal standard, assuming a response factor of 1.0 for all compounds. Therefore, the calculated values represent approximate contents expressed as 1,2-dichlorobenzene equivalents rather than absolute concentrations.

2.7 Relative Odor activity value (ROAV)

The aroma contribution of volatile compounds was evaluated using the relative odor activity value (ROAV) method. The compound that contributed the most to the overall aroma was assigned an ROAV of 100, and the ROAVs of the other constituents were calculated using the following equation: ROAVi ≈ 100 × (Ci/Cmax) × (Tmax/Ti), where Ci and Ti denote the relative content and odor threshold of compound i, respectively, and Cmax and Tmax represent the corresponding values for the most aroma-contributing compound. Because the volatile contents (Ci) were obtained by semi-quantitative analysis using a single internal standard, the calculated ROAVs should be relative estimates of odor contribution rather than absolute odor activity values. Volatile compounds with ROAV ≥ 1 were considered key aroma components contributing substantially to the overall odor profile, while those with 0.1 ≤ ROAV < 1 were regarded as auxiliary aroma compounds that may contribute to odor modification.

2.8 Sensory evaluation

Eight professionally trained panelists with relevant expertise participated in the sensory evaluation. The assessment was conducted with reference to the Chinese National Standards GB/T 10220–2012 and GB/T 30391–2013. Samples from different stir-frying times were randomly coded and presented in a randomized order for blinded evaluation. Each sample was evaluated three times, and the mean scores were used for subsequent analysis. Sensory attributes included color, aroma, taste, and burnt odor, with maximum scores of 20, 30, 30, and 20 points, respectively, giving a total sensory score of 100 points. Burnt odor was scored inversely, with higher scores indicating a weaker burnt odor.

2.9 Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation

The three-dimensional structures of core olfactory receptor proteins involved in aroma perception were retrieved from the AlphaFold3 database (Abramson et al., 2024), and the molecular structures of characteristic volatile compounds were obtained from the PubChem database. Seven key aroma compounds were selected based on their ROAV values (ROAV ≥1), relatively high semi-quantitative contents in the volatile profile, and their reported olfactory importance in Sichuan pepper or related spices. These compounds included (+)-limonene, β-terpineol, linalool, eucalyptol, myrcene, and two additional representative aroma compounds. Seven olfactory receptors were selected based on previous reports of interactions between terpenoid compounds and olfactory receptors. All molecular structures were converted to PDB format using Open Babel. Molecular docking was performed with AutoDock Tools, and the binding poses were generated by AutoDock Vina and subsequently visualized. The docking analysis was conducted to provide a molecular-level interpretation of the potential interactions between key aroma compounds and olfactory receptors.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation of the complex formed by (+)-limonene and the olfactory receptor OR1A2 was carried out using GROMACS 2020.3 (Abraham et al., 2015). MD simulation was further performed to evaluate the stability of the ligand–receptor complex over time and to provide additional structural evidence for the docking results. The simulation box was sized so that the distance between the protein atoms and the box edges exceeded 1.0 nm, and the system energy was minimized to eliminate steric clashes or atomic overlap. Equilibration was conducted under the NVT and NPT ensembles, followed by a 100 ns production run. Temperature and pressure were controlled using the V-rescale thermostat and the Parrinello–Rahman barostat, respectively. Van der Waals interactions were described by the Lennard-Jones potential with a non-bonded cutoff of 1.4 nm. All bond lengths were constrained using the LINCS algorithm. Electrostatic interactions were treated using the particle-mesh Ewald (PME) method with a Fourier spacing of 0.16 nm.

2.10 HPLC–UV determination of non-volatile constituents

Accurately weighed Sichuan pepper powder (0.5 g) was ultrasonically extracted with 25 mL of 60% (v/v) ethanol for 30 min and then filtered to obtain the test solution. Reference stock solutions were prepared by individually dissolving accurately weighed amounts of 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (5-HMF), gallic acid, rutin, hydroxy-β-sanshool, hydroxy-α-sanshool, and xanthoxylin in 70% (v/v) methanol to yield concentrations of 1.64, 2.18, 1.98, 0.71, 1.98, and 1.87 mg/mL, respectively.

Analysis was performed on an LC-2030 Plus high-performance liquid chromatograph (HPLC, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with an ENorMicro ENM-Miracle™ XL-C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of methanol (A) and water (B) with the following gradient program: 0–15 min, 32% A; 15–25 min, 32–55% A; 25–35 min, 55–58% A; 35–52 min, 58% A. The flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, the column temperature was maintained at 30 °C, and the detection wavelength was set at 270 nm. The method was validated by assessing precision, stability, repeatability, and recovery, and the relative standard deviations (RSDs) were calculated to evaluate the reliability of the analytical method. The linear regression equations, linearity ranges, and correlation coefficients are presented in Table S2, while the results of the precision, stability, repeatability, and recovery tests (n = 6) are summarized in Table S3.

2.11 Determination of Total flavonoids

A series of rutin standard solutions (0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, and 3.0 mL of 0.2 mg/mL rutin) were prepared accurately. The absorbance was measured at 510 nm using the NaNO2–Al(NO3)3 colorimetric method. A linear regression equation was established with rutin concentration (x) as the abscissa and absorbance (y) as the ordinate: y = 2.21× + 0.0469 (R2 = 0.9998). The absorbance of the Sichuan pepper test solution was determined under the same conditions, and the total flavonoid content was calculated using the regression equation.

2.12 Determination of Total polyphenols

Gallic acid (10 mg) was accurately weighed, dissolved in water, and diluted to 10 mL to obtain a 1 mg/mL standard stock solution. Aliquots of 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 mL were transferred to 10 mL volumetric flasks, and 1 mL of 1 mol/L Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and 2 mL of 15% (w/v) sodium carbonate solution were added sequentially. The mixtures were diluted to volume with water, mixed thoroughly, and allowed to stand for 1 h. The absorbance was measured at 765 nm against a blank. A linear regression equation was obtained with gallic acid concentration (x) as the independent variable and absorbance (y) as the dependent variable: y = 1.939× + 0.2626 (R2 = 0.9998). One milliliter of the Sichuan pepper test solution was subjected to the same procedure, and the total polyphenol content was calculated using the standard curve.

2.13 Antioxidant activity assays

DPPH radical scavenging assay: The sample solution was mixed with an equal volume of 0.2 mmol/L DPPH solution and incubated in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at 517 nm (A₁). A blank control (A₀) using 60% ethanol and a sample background control (A₂) were prepared concurrently. The scavenging rate was calculated as: scavenging rate (%) = [1 − (A₁ − A₂) / A₀] × 100%.

ABTS radical cation scavenging assay: Ten microliters of sample, Trolox standard, or water were added to the wells of a microplate, followed by the sequential addition of 20 μL peroxidase and 170 μL ABTS working solution. After 6 min reaction, the absorbance was recorded at 414 nm. Results were expressed as Trolox equivalent antioxidant capacity (TEAC) in mmol TE/g DW.

Superoxide anion radical scavenging assay: Buffer solution and the respective solutions were added to the measurement tube (sample), control tube (water), and standard tube (0.15 mg/mL ascorbic acid). Reagents 2, 3, and 4 were then added in sequence, and the mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 40 min. After incubation, the chromogenic agent was added and the tubes were left to stand for 10 min before measuring the absorbance was measured at 550 nm. The activity was calculated as: activity (U/L) = (OD_control − OD_sample) / (OD_control − OD_standard) × 0.15 × 1000 × dilution factor.

Hydroxyl radical (·OH) scavenging assay: Fifty microliters each of FeSO₄ solution, H₂O₂ solution, salicylic acid–ethanol solution, and the sample were combined, and the volume was made up to 400 μL. After incubation at 37 °C for 30 min, the absorbance was measured at 510 nm (A_sample). A blank (A_blank) and a sample background (A_background) were measured in parallel. The scavenging rate was calculated as: scavenging rate (%) = [1 − (A_sample − A_background) / A_blank] × 100%.

2.14 Statistical and correlation analysis

All results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1). Comparisons among different stir-frying durations were conducted by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Pairwise correlations were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficients and a correlation heatmap was generated (significance thresholds: P < 0.05, P < 0.01, and P < 0.001). Furthermore, partial least squares regression (PLSR) was employed to model the relationships between chemical constituents and sensory attributes (aroma and color), and the variable importance in projection (VIP) values were used to identify key contributing components.

3 Results and discussion

3.1 Changes in appearance and powder morphology of Sichuan pepper during stir-frying

With the extension of stir-frying time, the color of Sichuan pepper pericarp gradually deepened from the initial bright red (Fig. 1A). A glossy, oily sheen appeared on the surface at 1–3 min of stir-frying. After 4–6 min, the pericarp color turned reddish-brown, and partial charring appeared at the edges of some pericarps from 7 min onward. At 10 min, the samples were uniformly dark brown and emitted a burnt odor. The temperature–time profiles of the electric ceramic cooktop and traditional open-flame stir-frying are shown in Fig. S1. Compared with the open-flame method, the electric ceramic cooktop exhibited a more stable temperature profile with smaller fluctuations during stir-frying, indicating better temperature controllability and process stability. The color change trend of the powder was consistent with that of the intact pericarp: the unstir-fried sample powder was light reddish-yellow, whereas the powder became dark reddish-brown in the later stages of stir-frying. SEM (Fig. 1B) observations revealed that the surface of unstir-fried Sichuan pepper was relatively smooth with an intact waxy layer. After 3 min of stir-frying, the waxy layer started to melt and rupture, micropores appeared on the surface, and a small amount of oil was observed around the sample, corresponding to the “sweating” phenomenon observed during stir-frying. At 5 min, the surface structure visibly collapsed and the number of pores increased, which may have facilitated the release and diffusion of volatile compounds. The higher-magnification SEM images (2000×, Fig. S2) further showed the formation of surface micropores, localized oil release, and progressive tissue collapse, providing additional microscopic evidence for the structural changes observed during stir-frying. With prolonged heating, excessive tissue collapse and charring were observed, providing structural evidence of progressive tissue deterioration at later stages. At 8 min, charred fragments appeared on the surface, indicating severe tissue damage. These findings suggest that moderate stir-frying disrupts the waxy barrier on the pericarp surface and may facilitate the release of volatile compounds, providing a structural basis for the characteristic requirement that Sichuan pepper be “stir-fried until fragrant.” However, excessive stir-frying leads to pronounced tissue deterioration, accompanied by aroma loss and the generation of burnt off-odors, underscoring the importance of controlling the degree of stir-frying for quality preservation (Alim et al., 2025; Yang & Jin, 2024). 3.5 GC–MS analysis of volatile compounds

A total of 26 volatile compounds were identified by headspace GC–MS (Table 1), including monoterpenes, alcohols, esters, sesquiterpenes, ethers/oxides, and ketones. In the raw sample, sabinene, (+)-limonene, eucalyptol, myrcene, and 4-thujanol were the most abundant constituents, with monoterpenes dominating the volatile profile. During stir-frying, the majority of monoterpenes exhibited a pattern of initially increasing and subsequently decreasing. (+)-limonene, α-pinene, sabinene, and myrcene increased slightly within 0–2 min, generally declined at 3 min, increased again to peak levels at 4–5 min, and then declined progressively after 6 min. In the early stage of stir-frying, the melting and rupture of the waxy layer and the disruption of cellular structure may have promoted the desorption and release of monoterpenes from the matrix, leading to a transient increase in their content. This initial increase followed by a decline is consistent with previous reports that thermal treatment can first promote the release of aroma-active terpenoids from plant matrices and subsequently accelerate their evaporation and degradation (Z. Li et al., 2019; Mahanta et al., 2021). Eucalyptol, by contrast, exhibited a different pattern: its content dropped sharply after 3 min and remained at a low level thereafter, indicating relatively high thermal sensitivity. Although the boiling point of eucalyptol (174–177 °C) is similar to that of (+)-limonene (176 °C), its more pronounced decrease may be related to the greater susceptibility of its ether/oxide structure to thermal oxidation. Previous studies have confirmed that high-temperature heating can accelerate the oxidation of Sichuan pepper essential oil and cause flavor deterioration (Chen et al., 2024). The distinct behavior of eucalyptol further indicates that individual volatile compounds differ in thermal stability, making its sharp decline after 3 min particularly informative for monitoring thermal stress during stir-frying. Therefore, eucalyptol can be regarded as a potential chemical marker for monitoring the degree of stir-frying. Among the alcohols, linalool decreased continuously, and 4-thujanol showed a decline of 84.1%. Both linalool and 4-thujanol belong to the monoterpene alcohols, and their persistent decrease is attributable not only to thermal evaporation and oxidative losses but also potentially to acid-catalyzed hydration/dehydration and rearrangement reactions as important degradation pathway. Using isotope tracer techniques, Dong et al. systematically summarized the degradation pathways of linalool and limonene under boiling conditions, confirming that changes in the overall content of terpenoids directly affect the overall aroma characteristics of Sichuan pepper aqueous extracts (Dong et al., 2024).

3.6 ROAV analysis of key aroma components

The ROAV values were calculated according to the method described in the literature (Su et al., 2025), with ROAV ≥ 1 indicating key aroma components and 0.1 ≤ ROAV < 1 indicating auxiliary aroma components (Table 2). In the raw Sichuan pepper, 15 key aroma compounds were identified. β-Terpineol showed the highest relative odor contribution (ROAV = 100), followed by (+)-limonene (70.04), linalool (61.69), and eucalyptol (50.85), indicating that these compounds contributed substantially to the volatile profile. During the initial stage of stir-frying, β-terpineol remained the highest-contributing compound at 1 and 2 min (ROAV = 100), but its relative contribution generally decreased thereafter, reaching 71.39 at 5 min and 54.98 at 10 min, although some fluctuations were observed. From 3 min onward, (+)-limonene became the highest relative contributor (ROAV = 100). Linalool decreased from 61.69 in the raw sample to 35.13 at 5 min, followed by fluctuations at later stages. Eucalyptol showed a marked nonlinear change, increasing from 50.85 in the raw sample to 62.62 at 3 min, then decreasing sharply to 12.20 and 10.91 at 4 and 5 min, respectively, followed by a marked increase at 6 min. In contrast, myrcene consistently maintained an ROAV ≥ 1 throughout the process, with ROAV values ranging from 22.46 to 37.01, suggesting a relatively stable contribution to the volatile profile. Importantly, the non-uniform changes in ROAVs indicate that stir-frying altered the relative contribution of individual volatiles rather than simply reducing overall aroma intensity. The decline in heat-sensitive compounds together with the persistence of myrcene therefore reflects a progressive remodeling of the aroma-active profile. Overall, the ROAV results demonstrated progressive remodeling of the aroma-active composition with increasing stir-frying time, characterized by the decline of several heat-sensitive monoterpenes and terpene alcohols and the relative persistence of selected components. The number of key aroma components remained within 15–16 throughout the stir-frying process, while 4–6 compounds were classified as auxiliary modifying components. At approximately 5 min, (+)-limonene remained the dominant relative contributor, while β-terpineol, linalool, myrcene, and other key aroma compounds retained relatively high ROAVs, indicating a distinct transition stage in the aroma-active profile. 3.7 Molecular docking and dynamics simulation reveal key aroma molecules

Based on ROAV values, semi-quantitative abundance, and reported olfactory importance, seven key aroma compounds were selected for molecular docking with seven olfactory receptors. Molecular docking of the seven key aroma compounds with seven olfactory receptor proteins was performed (Fig. 5A). The binding energies ranged from −4.646 to −7.317 kcal/mol. Notably, the complex between (+)-limonene and OR1A2 exhibited the lowest binding energy of −7.317 kcal/mol, suggesting a relatively favorable binding interaction (< −7.0 kcal/mol). As shown in Fig. 5B, (+)-limonene primarily formed hydrophobic interactions with residues Ala108, Tyr276, and Phe206, while a π–σ interaction was observed between Tyr258 and limonene. Based on this favorable docking result, a 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation was conducted. Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) analyses (Fig. 5C) revealed that the complex system approached equilibrium after approximately 20 ns, with low RMSD values indicating limited structural fluctuations. The RMSF results (Fig. 5F) showed that the local conformation of the receptor underwent minor adjustments upon binding, whereas the core region maintained consistently low RMSF values, indicating a relatively rigid structural scaffold. The radius of gyration (Rg) fluctuated between 2.08 and 2.20 nm, suggesting that the overall structure of the complex remained relatively compact throughout the simulation. 3.8 Changes in the contents of non-volatile constituents

The changes in the contents of non-volatile constituents are shown in Fig. 6A, and the corresponding HPLC chromatograms are presented in Fig. 6B. The moisture content decreased progressively with increasing stir-frying time (Fig. S3), indicating substantial water loss during thermal processing. Therefore, the contents of non-volatile constituents were expressed on a dry-weight basis according to the measured moisture content at each corresponding stir-frying time point, thereby minimizing the potential influence of dehydration on apparent concentration changes. In the raw sample, the total flavonoid content, expressed as rutin equivalents (RE), and total polyphenol content, expressed as gallic acid equivalents (GAE), were 243.43 and 205.38 mg/g DW, respectively. Total flavonoids increased progressively during the early-to-middle stages of stir-frying, reaching a maximum of 297.96 mg/g DW at 5 min, and subsequently decreased to 254.30 mg/g DW at 10 min. Rutin also increased markedly, reaching a maximum of 8.05 mg/g at 5 min, followed by a gradual decrease during prolonged heating. 3.9 Antioxidant activity analysis

The changes in antioxidant activity are illustrated in Fig. 7A-D. All four indices exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease, with their peak values observed at 5 min of stir-frying. The antioxidant response was temporally consistent with the compositional changes observed in the non-volatile constituents, particularly the transient accumulation of several antioxidant-related components during the earlier stages and their subsequent decline during prolonged heating. Sustained high-temperature exposure may therefore have contributed to the decline in antioxidant capacity at later stages. The VIP values of total flavonoids and total polyphenols exceeding 1 highlight the central importance of phenolic compounds in the association between color browning and bioactivity attenuation.

Spearman correlation analysis (Fig. 8E) further delineated the relationships among the indicators, including color attributes, bioactive compounds, numbing-taste substances, and antioxidant indices. The L* value was significantly correlated with total flavonoids, total polyphenols, and gallic acid, showing a negative correlation with total flavonoids (r = −0.61, P < 0.05) but positive correlations with total polyphenols (r = 0.95, P < 0.001) and gallic acid (r = 0.96, P < 0.001), indicating that changes in lightness were closely associated with variations in these chemical constituents. In contrast, L* was significantly negatively correlated with 5-HMF (r = −0.86, P < 0.001), consistent with the progressive accumulation of Maillard reaction products during prolonged stir-frying. The a* value was significantly positively correlated with numbing-related components, including hydroxy-α-sanshool, xanthoxylin, and hydroxy-β-sanshool, and showed positive correlations with hydroxy-α-sanshool (r = 0.62, P < 0.05), hydroxy-β-sanshool (r = 0.80, P < 0.01), and xanthoxylin (r = 0.78, P < 0.01), as well as with hydroxyl radical scavenging activity (r = 0.88, P < 0.001), DPPH scavenging activity (r = 0.73, P < 0.05), superoxide anion scavenging activity (r = 0.89, P < 0.001), and ABTS capacity (r = 0.85, P < 0.01). Together, these correlations indicate that color changes were associated with changes in bioactive and numbing-related constituents as well as antioxidant status. Sensory attributes were also closely associated with these chemical and instrumental indicators. Sensory color correlated strongly with aroma (r = 0.96, P < 0.001) and taste (r = 0.95, P < 0.001), while sensory aroma correlated with xanthoxylin (r = 0.97, P < 0.001) and ABTS capacity (r = 0.98, P < 0.001). Sensory taste was strongly correlated with hydroxy-α-sanshool (r = 0.92, P < 0.001), linking compositional changes with sensory perception. Overall, the integrated analyses demonstrated coordinated transformations in chemical composition, aroma-related characteristics, color, and sensory perception during stir-frying. Considering the combined changes in these quality-related indicators, 4–5 min was identified as a reference processing window for standardized production.

4 Conclusion

This study systematically characterized the dynamic changes and interrelationships among chemical composition, aroma, and color during the stir-frying of Sichuan pepper. Stir-frying time markedly affected all three attributes, and the quality transformation showed nonlinear characteristics. The L* value was significantly positively correlated with total flavonoids and total polyphenols, supporting its potential use as a visual indicator of quality changes, while a* was positively correlated with numbing-related constituents and antioxidant activity. PLSR identified seven key synergistic contributors (VIP > 1) involved in the coordinated color–aroma changes. By integrating colorimetry, electronic-nose analysis, HS-GC–MS, HPLC–UV, antioxidant assays, sensory evaluation, and molecular docking and dynamics simulations, this study provides a multidimensional framework for characterizing the coordinated quality changes during stir-frying. Taken together, the coordinated changes across the measured indicators support 4–5 min as a reference processing window based on the integrated quality indicators, rather than defining aroma quality from any single instrumental measurement alone.
