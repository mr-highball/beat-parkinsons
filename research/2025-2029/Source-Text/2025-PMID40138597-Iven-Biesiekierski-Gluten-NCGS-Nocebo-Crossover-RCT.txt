Primary Abstract:
BACKGROUND/AIMS: Non-coeliac gluten sensitivity (NCGS) is a controversial entity, characterised by symptom improvement with gluten exclusion in the absence of coeliac disease. We primarily investigated the effects of acute and sub-acute gluten on psychological and mood profiles, with secondary outcomes examining gastrointestinal symptoms and biological markers in healthy controls (HC) and individuals with NCGS. METHODS: A randomised, single-blind, crossover study used acute (16 g gluten or whey in yoghurt) and sub-acute (gluten-containing (16 g) or gluten-free muffins per day for 5 days) challenges. (Extra)intestinal symptoms, intestinal permeability, high-sensitive C-reactive protein and cortisol awakening response were assessed. Responses over time were analysed using generalised linear mixed models. RESULTS: Twenty HCs (15% men, mean age 30 years) and 16 individuals with NCGS (31% men, mean age 33 years) participated. No significant group-by-nutrient interactions were observed. Negative affect scores were higher and positive affect scores were lower in NCGS compared to HC (p = 0.01 and p = 0.04, respectively). Participants experienced higher tension scores after gluten compared with placebo (p = 0.01 acute; p = 0.05 sub-acute) regardless of the group. After acute administration, fatigue scores increased in NCGS (p = 0.03) compared with HC regardless of nutrient intake. After sub-acute administration, abdominal pain scores (p < 0.001) and bloating (p = 0.001) increased in NCGS compared with HC regardless of nutrient intake. No differences were found for biological markers. CONCLUSIONS: These findings reveal that NCGS is characterised by baseline differences in affect, and higher acute fatigue and subacute gastrointestinal symptoms that are not gluten-specific. This may be explained by nocebo effects, warranting research into novel mechanisms and re-evaluating the NCGS definition. TRIAL REGISTRATION: ClinicalTrials.gov no: NCT03798262; NCT03798249.

[Materials and Methods] A total of 20 HC and 16 individuals with NCGS were enrolled. The study, initially powered for a medium effect size (Cohen's d = 0.64) [7] with 85% power was terminated early due to recruitment challenges and the COVID‐19 pandemic. A sensitivity analysis confirmed that the final NCGS sample size (n = 16) provides 80% power for medium effects (Cohen's d = 0.65) and small‐to‐medium group‐by‐nutrient interactions (Cohen's f = 0.24). Full power calculations are in Supporting Information S1: Supplementary Material (S1). Key inclusion criteria included: For NCGS participants—self‐reported GI and/or extraintestinal symptoms that significantly improved on a gluten‐free diet (GFD), adherence to strict GFD for ≥ 6 weeks, and confirmed absence of coeliac disease through negative serology, HLA testing, and duodenal biopsy when indicated. Healthy controls were required to have no digestive symptoms or history of gastrointestinal disorders. Major exclusion criteria for both groups included: psychiatric disorders, recent abdominal/thoracic surgery, food allergies, use of antibiotics/prebiotics/probiotics in past 3 months, abnormal eating behaviours, smoking, pregnancy, and excessive alcohol intake. NCGS participants were excluded if they had any diagnosed disorder of gut‐brain interaction (DGBI) other than IBS. While participants could meet the Rome III criteria for IBS symptoms, those with formally diagnosed DGBIs were excluded. Detailed inclusion/exclusion criteria are provided in Supporting Information S1. The study used a randomised, placebo‐controlled, single‐blind, crossover design. HC followed a GFD for 6 weeks and individuals with NCGS continued their normal GFD. All study visits were performed at the University Hospital Leuven in the morning after an overnight fast. [Discussion] This single‐blind, randomised, placebo‐controlled, crossover trial is, to the best of our knowledge, the first study that investigated the effects of acute and sub‐acute gluten administration on extraintestinal and GI symptoms in individuals with NCGS and healthy controls. Our results indicate that individuals with NCGS exhibit distinct psychological characteristics at baseline, with higher NA and lower PA scores. While these individuals experienced increased fatigue after acute challenge and heightened GI symptoms during sub‐acute exposure, these responses were not gluten‐specific. The only main effect of nutrient found was for tension where acute gluten induced a less strong decrease compared to placebo, regardless of participant group. Hypothesised potential mediators of these effects (intestinal permeability, hsCRP and CAR) did not differ between nutrients or groups. These results challenge current conceptualisations of NCGS and highlight the need for a revised diagnostic framework.
