SUPPLEMENTAL MATERIAL
A: Respiratory-Swallow Training Performance Bandwidth Rules
Performance bandwidth rules were used by treating clinicians to guide the type, timing, and frequency of augment verbal feedback and error processing questions about task performance in a way that was both standardized and patient specific. They included the following.
First set of every training session:
Provide concurrent verbal instructions and concurrent visual models during every trial
Ask error process questions after every trial
Provide prescriptive feedback of results and performance after every trial
After one set with ≥80% accuracy, then for the following set:
Ask error processing questions after every trial
Provide descriptive knowledge of results and performance after the set (not after each trial)
After two consecutive sets with ≥80% accuracy, then for the following set:
Ask error processing questions after the set (not after each trial)
Provide descriptive knowledge of results and performance after the set (not after each trial)
After one set with <80% accuracy, then for the following set:
Ask error processing questions after every trial
Provide prescriptive knowledge of results and performance after every trial
After two consecutive sets with <80% accuracy, then for the following set:
Provide concurrent verbal instructions and concurrent visual models during every trial
Ask error processing questions after every trial
Provide prescriptive knowledge of results and performance after every trial
Definitions:
Concurrent verbal instructions: verbal instructions given ask the person is doing the task.
Example (given by the clinician in real-time with the patient): “Take a sip. Now breathe in. Now breathe out a little. Swallow. Keep breathing out.”
Concurrent visual models: visual models given by the clinician as the person is doing the task.
Example (given by the clinician in real-time with the patient): (1) moving both hands outward while expanding the ribcage to indicate an inhalation; (2) moving both hands inward while deflating the ribcage to indicate exhalation; (3) swallowing saliva in an exaggerated fashion with head motion to indicate swallowing; and continue to move both hands inward while deflating the ribcage to indicate continued exhalation.
Error processing questions: questions given by the clinician to the patient to facilitate improved self-awareness of task performance. The primary error processing question asked was: “Was that right or wrong?” Additional questions may have been intermittently added following this primary question to further scaffold awareness building. Examples of additional questions, when sporadically asked, included:
“Did you breathe out before you swallowed?”
“Did you breathe out after you swallowed?”
“Did you breathe out a little or a lot before you swallowed?”
“Did you feel like you had to push your breath out after you swallowed? If so, do you think taking a bigger breath in first, and breathing only a small amount of air before you swallowed might have made it easier?”
“What made that one easier for you to do?”
“Why was that one harder for you?”
Descriptive Knowledge of Results and Performance: a type of feedback that describes what the person did, including if the task was done correctly vs incorrectly (results) and if they exhaled vs inhaled (performance). Examples include:
“That one was right. You exhaled, swallowed, and then continued to exhale”
“That one was wrong. You inhaled, swallowed, then exhaled.”
“That one was wrong. You exhaled, swallowed, then inhaled.”
“That one was wrong. You inhaled, swallowed, then inhaled some more.”
Prescriptive Knowledge of Results and Performance: a type of feedback that describes what the person did and how to change it. Examples include:
“That one was right. You exhaled, swallowed, and then continued to exhale. Keep doing that.”
“That one was wrong. You inhaled, swallowed, then exhaled. Next time, breathe out before you swallow.”
“That one was wrong. You exhaled, swallowed, then inhaled. Next time, breathe out after you swallow.”
“That one was wrong. You inhaled, swallowed, then inhaled some more. Next time, breathe out before and after you swallow.”
B: FEES Protocol
The order of presentation for the swallowing conditions was fixed, and included the following:
5 mL, thin water, via syringe (three trials)
5 mL, thin water, via a 30 mL medicine cup (three trials)
15 mL, thin water, via a 30 mL medicine cup (three trials)
Self-selected volume (SSV), thin liquid, via an 8-ounce cup three trials)
SSV, mildly thick water, via an 8-ounce medicine cup (three trials)
5 mL, natural (no food dye) vanilla pudding, via spoon (three trials)
5 mL, mixed consistency containing three pineapple tidbits with pineapple juice and green food dye, via spoon (one trial)
5 mL, blue opaque thin water, via spoon (one trial)
SSV, green opaque thin water, via straw (one trial)
50 mL, blue opaque thin water, via an 8-ounce cup (one trial)
90 mL, white-dyed thin water, via an 8-ounce cup (one trial)
Thin water was tested to International Dysphagia Diet Standardisation Initiative (IDDSI) Level 0, mildly thick water was tested to IDDSI Level 2, vanilla pudding was tested to IDDSI Level 4, and the mixed consistency was tested to IDDSI Levels 6 (pineapple tidbits) and 0 (pineapple juice). White (AmeriColor), blue (Chef-o-Van), and green (Chef-o-Van) food coloring agents were used to enhance endoscopic visualization for a liquid. Each thin liquid swallowing condition was presented three times, with one trial of each of the following: white coating water (165 mL of water and 10 mL of white food coloring); blue opaque water (175 mL water with 0.5 mL of blue food coloring and four drops of white food coloring); and green opaque water (175 mL water with 0.5 mL of green food coloring and four drops of white food coloring). Four drops of green food coloring were also added to the 4-ounce cup of pineapple tidbits and juice.
All boluses were self-administered, except for the three trials of the 5 mL thin water via syringe, the one trial of the 5 mL thin liquid via spoon, and the one trial of 5 mL mixed consistency via spoon. Participants were instructed to swallow all boluses within a single swallow as best as able, except for the mixed consistency, 50 mL thin water, and 90 mL thin water. The single swallow instruction was given before the start of each new swallowing condition to reduce the likelihood of piecemeal deglutition but were not given once self-feeding began to avoid potential effects of verbal cueing or bolus holding.

D: Changes in Subglottic Residue Across Timepoint Comparisons
Figure: For the subglottic residue, odds ratios < 1.0 represent improved swallowing safety. This includes lower odds of residue being present (yellow) and/or lower odds of a high residue rating when residue was present (blue). Figure display odds ratio point estimates (dots) and their 95% confidence intervals.  Confidence intervals that cross the 1.0 marker are considered effects that are not statistically significant. As seen above, subglottic residue did not change significantly at any timepoint comparison.
