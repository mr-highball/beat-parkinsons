Primary Abstract:
Oxylipins are bioactive lipids involved in inflammation. This study evaluated how a 2-week anti-inflammatory diet (ITIS, omega-3/omega-6 ratio of 1:1.5) affects plasma oxylipin profiles in patients with active Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA). In an open-label pilot trial, 20 RA patients (≥3 tender and ≥3 swollen joints) followed the ITIS diet. Targeted lipidomics by mass spectrometry was used to quantify oxylipins. Patients were classified as responders or non-responders based on ≥50 % pain reduction (Pain-50). Dietary intake was assessed through diet scores, and statistical analyses were performed using RStudio. Participants were predominantly female (90 %) with an average age of 57.1. At baseline, responders consumed more walnuts (p = 0.08), almond milk (p = 0.06), avocado (p = 0.04), and quinoa (p = 0.05), and fewer burgers (p = 0.02). No differences in diet adherence were observed between groups. Baseline oxylipin levels did not differ significantly. However, after the intervention, six oxylipins-5-HETE, 11,12-diHETrE, 14,15-diHETrE, 19,20-DiHDPA, 9-oxo-ODE, and 14,15-EET-differed significantly between responders and non-responders. Notably, oxylipins derived from both arachidonic acid (omega-6) and eicosapentaenoic acid (omega-3) decreased significantly after the diet (p = 0.0006 and p = 0.01, respectively). The anti-inflammatory diet modified circulating levels of both pro- and anti-inflammatory oxylipins. These changes varied by pain response, suggesting that diet can influence inflammatory pathways in RA. Further studies are warranted to clarify the mechanisms linking dietary changes, oxylipin modulation, and clinical outcomes. NCT04999683.

[PATIENTS AND METHODS] Twenty patients ≥ 18 years of age with a diagnosis of RA with active disease based on the 2010 ACR/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) criteria (at least 3 tender and 3 swollen joints) were recruited to participate in a prospective open-label pilot trial to assess the effect of the 2-week ITIS diet on multiple clinical and biological outcomes. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB # 161474) at the University of California, San Diego. Patients who had a change in their treatment regimen within the three months leading up to the study or who would require a treatment change during the study were excluded. If patients had food allergies, were following a vegan/vegetarian diet at the time of recruitment, or were pregnant or lactating, they were also excluded from the study. The ITIS diet intervention is described in detail in Bustamante et al. 2020 (26–27). The ITIS diet recommends daily consumption of multiple anti-inflammatory ingredients including enzymatic fruits and berries, extra virgin olive oil, turmeric, seeds, flaxseed oil, tahini and/or avocado, walnuts, plain yogurt, and green tea. Additionally, the diet recommends weekly consumption of fatty fish, legumes, miso, whole grains, and pseudo-cereals. Foods with documented pro-inflammatory potential in the literature (including sugary products, red meat, refined grains, cruciferous vegetables, potatoes, eggplants, tomatoes, saturated fats, milk, alcohol, coffee, and sweetened beverages) were excluded from the diet. A sample of ITIS diet is described in ST1. The pilot study was conducted between 2018-2019. [RESULTS] A total of 20 patients completed the study. Table 1 summarizes the patients’ demographic information and treatment regimens at the start of the study. The participants had an average age of 57.10 years (±12.14) and 90% identified as female. The average body mass index (BMI) was 30.77 (±8.80). 95% were noted to be seropositive and 47.30% had joint erosions noted in prior imaging. At the initiation of the study, 10% of patients were taking corticosteroids, 20% were taking NSAIDs, 60% were taking DMARDs, 40% were taking biologic drugs, and 20% were on a combination regimen of DMARDs and Biologics. The average C-Reactive protein (CRP) was 1.09 (± 0.98), while the mean DAS28 was 3.86 (± 0.73). Levels of serum oxylipins were measured in the 20 patients before and after the diet intervention. A total of 47 oxylipins derived from AA (n=24), DHA (n=10), LA (n=6), EPA (n=6), and ALA (n=1) were included in the analysis (Supplementary Figure 1). During the first study visit (D-14), we collected data on participants’ baseline diet. Based on the 2-week pre-intervention diet log, we calculated omega-3 and omega-6 diet scores for all participants. The average pre-intervention omega-3 score for study participants was 13.89 (±13.37) and the average omega-6 score was 65.28 (±29.40). [DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS] In this study, we investigated the impact of a 2-week ITIS diet trial on oxylipin concentrations. Notably, eight oxylipins changed significantly pre- to post-intervention. We calculated omega-3 and omega-6 diet scores to evaluate dietary habits amongst participants. After grouping oxylipins based on their FA precursor, we noted a significant decrease in AA and EPA pre- to post-intervention. When oxylipins were further combined to yield overall omega-3 and omega-6 scores, we noted decreases in levels of both in R and NR pre- to post-intervention. This decrease was more pronounced in NR than R. Although diet scores amongst R and NR were similar at baseline, we noted differences in the consumption of multiple foods at baseline between R and NR. Despite baseline similarities, R exhibited higher post-diet concentrations of multiple notable oxylipins. Our nutritional pilot study is the first investigation evaluating the impact of a dietary intervention on serum oxylipins.
