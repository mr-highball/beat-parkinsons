Background

The exact disease-causing mechanisms in ALS remain incompletely understood. A possible cause is inflammation of neurons. Compared to other neurodegenerative diseases, neuroinflammation is pronounced in ALS, presumably leading to disease progression and neuronal death. Patients with ALS can have neuronal autoantibodies and most notably antibody-producing cells in the cerebrospinal fluid. The CD20-binding therapeutic antibody rituximab targets these antibody-producing B-cells. Rituximab is a medication commonly used in neurology. Single patients with ALS have already been treated with rituximab, which was well tolerated. We therefore want to evaluate safety and efficacy of a treatment with rituximab in ALS patients in a double-blind placebo-controlled trial. With this trial we aim for slower disease progression and unveiling disease-causing mechanisms in ALS.

Methods

A total of 52 male and female patients older than 18 years with active sporadic ALS and disease duration of less than 24 months who are on a stable dose of riluzole (50 mg BID) should be included. Patients requiring assisted ventilation of any kind or have a known intolerance to the study medication rituximab are excluded. The study medication rituximab or placebo should be administered over a period of 52 weeks at 4 distinct time points, followed by a follow-up period of 79 weeks with 4 visits. Treatment efficacy should be evaluated using the ALS functioning scale ALSFRS-R-SE at every study visit (except safety visits). Safety will be assessed through regular laboratory analyses, adverse event (AE) and serious adverse event (SAE) monitoring, and CD20+ B-cell kinetics. The primary endpoint is the change in the ALS Functional Rating Scale-Revised (ALSFRS-R-SE) from baseline to week 79. Trial sites will be at Charité/DZNE Berlin and DZNE Bonn.

Discussion

ALS is a rapidly progressive neurodegenerative disease with limited treatment options. Recent preclinical in vivo studies suggest that B-cell-mediated autoimmunity contributes to disease progression. This trial will evaluate whether rituximab, which specifically depletes CD20+ B-cells, can slow ALS progression and provide insights into disease mechanisms.

Trial registration

The trial was registered in the German WHO-recognized primary register Deutsches Register Klinische Studien DRKS (German Clinical Trials Register) on 3/07/2023 under registration number DRKS00030948.

Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a devastating, rapidly progressive neurodegenerative disease without effective treatment options [8]. Life expectancy after symptom onset is 2–5 years. The incidence in Germany is at around 3.1/100,000 inhabitants. Although the risk for developing ALS is at 1:400, it ranks among the “orphan diseases” as prevalence is low due the short life expectancy [21].

ALS is characterized by a degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons, which rapidly leads to paralysis, spasticity and muscular atrophy. It ultimately results in death, usually due to respiratory failure. Patients rapidly become dependent in their activities of daily life. Thus, quality of life is often impaired already in early stages of the disease.

Currently, in Germany the only available neuroprotective medication riluzole has an average life-prolonging effect of 2–3 months. Despite manifold research efforts, ALS still is a fatal disease with a striking therapeutic need. Promising results—improved functioning, longer life expectancy and/or quality of life—would substantially change the therapeutic options for the individual patient and the clinical management of ALS. Besides the therapeutic aspect, our trial can markedly add to the understanding of disease development.

Presumed mechanisms

The pathophysiological processes behind the progressive motor neuron degeneration in ALS are still incompletely understood. Neuroinflammation in ALS has been recognized as a fundamental disease-promoting mechanism for a long time. At the same time, protein aggregation and the genetic background play a major role in ALS pathophysiology (A). Compared to other neurodegenerative disorders, the inflammatory response in ALS is more pronounced and progression is faster. Thus, inflammation likely contributes to disease progression, aggravates neurodegeneration and leads to the typical spreading of disease [12, 15, 22].

The pathogenic role of the immune system in ALS is supported by both clinical and basic science studies, and involves various types of immune cells (Fig. 1B). For example, two register-based studies showed increased susceptibility to autoimmunity in ALS patients ranging from myasthenia gravis, myositis, Guillain-Barré syndrome to type 1 diabetes and multiple sclerosis (MS) [5]. Cellular dysfunction in ALS involves T- and B-cells. For example, CD8+ T cells that express the ALS-causing superoxide dismutase-1 mutant protein, can kill motor neurons in vitro [18].Fig. 1 Pathophysiological processes in ALS. A Schematic overview of the proposed disease mechanisms contributing to the pathogenesis of ALS. B Depicted are immunological abnormalities in ALS covering a wide range of immune cells and inflammatory cascades including humoral autoimmunity

Regarding dysfunction of B-cells in ALS, C9orf72 knockout mice, the most common ALS mutation, show a strong autoimmune phenotype with increased activation of B-cell populations [1].

The humoral immune system provides the most direct rationale for B-cell-directed therapy in ALS. IgG deposits localize to motor neurons and to the ventral horns and pyramidal tracts affected in ALS but not to control tissue, and passive transfer of ALS-derived IgG induces motor neuron degeneration in mice, indicating that antibodies can be pathogenic rather than merely reactive [6, 18]. Consistent with an active humoral process, several intracellular-protein autoantibodies are elevated in ALS serum and rise with disease severity, anti-TDP-43 autoantibodies are consistently higher than in controls, and defined targets such as GABA-B receptors have been identified [3, 14, 19]. Because these antibody-mediated mechanisms depend on CD20 + B-cells, they define the therapeutic target this trial addresses by depleting those cells with rituximab. Further evidence for the dysfunctional humoral immune system comes from our personal clinical experience with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analyses in ALS patients. Single patients showed signs of neuroinflammation with increased CSF white blood cell counts, increased CSF protein concentrations and positive titers of anti-neuronal autoantibodies.

Rationale for selection of investigational medicinal product

Based on these immunological changes in ALS patients with a suspected role of humoral immunity, modulation of B-cell activity may be a specific disease-modifying therapy, e.g., by administration of rituximab, an anti-CD20 antibody. Rituximab effectively depletes B-cells and is safe and well-tolerated in common neuroimmunological indications, such as autoimmune encephalitis, neuromyelitis optica and myasthenia gravis [11]. However, effects are not limited to B-cell-mediated conditions, but also include MS, a primarily T-cell-mediated disease [9]. Hence, B-cell depletion with rituximab targets T-cell activity as well. Compassionate use (“individueller Heilversuch”) of rituximab in single patients with ALS resulted in B-cell depletion and proved to be safe and well tolerated.

Therefore, we conclude that rituximab should be systematically evaluated for a possible effect on disease progression in ALS. As precise targeting of inflammation and at the same time maintaining the protective immune functions is crucial, the specific B-cell depletion by rituximab is promising. This exploratory trial should help to clarify the role of the immune system in ALS. As autoimmunity certainly is only one of several disease-promoting factors, we do not expect to completely stop, but rather to slow disease progression.

We searched the electronic databases PubMed, Cochrane Central/the Cochrane Library, ClinicalTrials.gov, Deutsches Register Klinischer Studien and the International Clinical Trials Search Portal for evidence. Search terms included amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, motor neuron disease or Gehrig’s disease and immune/immunotherapy with no further limits. The latest date of search was 18.03.2024.

We found a very limited number of immune-modulating drugs to have been clinically evaluated in this indication (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifiers: NCT01884571, NCT02469896). The published NIPALS2013 trial (NCT01884571) showed no effect on disease progression using an immunosuppressant from transplantation medicine. The trial showed a dropout rate of >40%, likely because it was including patients even >24 months after disease onset. Furthermore, this study drug did not target antibody-producing cells.

A recent phase-II study (AB0015) used Masitinib, an oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor, as an add-on therapy to riluzole. ALSFRS-R progression rate was significantly slowed in the treatment group compared to the placebo group. This effect might be explained by immunomodulation through targeting of microglia, macrophages and mast cell activity in the central and peripheral nervous system [17].

Limitations of previous immunosuppressive trials in ALS

Previous attempts at immunotherapy in ALS using broad immunosuppressive agents such as corticosteroids and cyclophosphamide have failed to show clinical benefit. These failures may be attributable to lack of target specificity and substantial side effects from generalized immunosuppression. The identification of specific autoantibodies and B-cell populations in ALS provides a rationale for more targeted immunotherapy. Rituximab, which specifically depletes CD20+ B-cells while preserving other immune functions, represents a more rational therapeutic candidate based on recent mechanistic insights into B-cell-mediated autoimmunity in ALS.Hypothesis:

We hypothesize that immune mechanisms including low-level anti-neuronal autoantibodies contribute to the pathogenesis of ALS and that depletion of such autoantibodies via B-cell depletion with rituximab leads to reduced progression of ALS symptoms.

Objectives {7}

Primary objective

We hypothesize that B-cell depletion with rituximab compared to placebo will slow ALS disease progression as measured by ALSFRS-R-SE change from baseline to week 79.

Secondary and other objectives

Secondary objectives are the further evaluation of ALS-related signs and symptoms as well as of safety and tolerability after the treatment with rituximab. Secondary objectives besides the clinical evaluation of motor deficits include assessment of BMI and the evaluation of the slow vital capacity score.

Further secondary objectives are evaluation of laboratory parameters, evaluation of cognitive deficits and possible changes in the autoantibody repertoire.

Adverse events and serious adverse events are monitored throughout the whole duration of the trial.

Trial design {8}

ABCD is a multi-center, phase IIa, interventional, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel group trial with an exploratory hypothesis framework. Subjects will be randomized 1:1 either to the active substance (Rituximab) or to placebo stratified by center.

The trial will consist of a screening visit, 4 treatment, 3 safety and 3 follow-up visits with investigations of efficacy and safety.

Visits start with a screening visit that takes place a maximum of 2 weeks before the start of the treatment. The treatment period comprises 4 visits for the administration of Rituximab/placebo. As the first two administrations of Rituximab serve as induction, they need to take place within 2 weeks (± 3 days). The following two infusions will then take place every 6 months (26 weeks ± 14 days). Between the administration of rituximab and 13 weeks ± 14 days after the last IMP administration on-site safety visits are scheduled. As the expected immunosuppressive effect of Rituximab lasts for about 26 weeks, the first follow-up visit will take place 26 weeks ± 14 days after the previous treatment. This visit will constitute the main point to compare treatment effects to baseline. In order to monitor long-term effects on disease progression and possible side effects two further follow-up visits are planned, each 26 weeks ± 14 days apart.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {9}

The ABCD trial will be conducted at two academic neurological centers in Germany: the Department of Neurology at Charité Universitätsmedizin Berlin/German Center for Neurodegenerative Diseases (DZNE) Berlin and DZNE Bonn.

Eligibility criteria {10}

Inclusion criteria are broad in order to include any clinical presentation of sporadic adult-onset ALS. Patients with the diagnosis of clinically definite ALS, or clinically probable ALS based on clinical-neurological examination, electromyography and electroneurography can be included in the study when they meet the following criteria at the screening visit. This will result in a study cohort with comparable or even less comorbidity to MS or encephalitis patients—where rituximab is routinely applied—and thus in a tolerable safety profile:Disease duration of sporadic ALS is equal or less than 24 months after symptom onset and symptoms have not progressed to permanent need of assisted ventilation of any kind (including non-invasive ventilation).

Age ≥ 18 years

Written consent to participate in the study

Medication with riluzole at a stable dose of 50 mg BID for ≥ 30 days prior to the screening visit and if possible, throughout the study.

Slow vital capacity (VC) equal to or more than 75% predicted normal value for gender, height and age at the Screening Visit. The threshold was chosen to ensure adequate baseline respiratory function, thereby minimizing the risk of respiratory complications in the context of immunosuppressive treatment. Preserving sufficient pulmonary reserve is considered essential for patient safety during immunomodulatory interventions.

Recommended standard vaccination according to STIKO (incl. COVID-19 vaccination). Except for Hepatitis B, Hepatitis B serology will be analyzed at screening as to exclude an acute or underlying infection. In case of missing vaccination, it is mandatory to perform the vaccination parallel to the trial. If necessary, vaccinations should take place four weeks prior to study drug to allow for sufficient vaccination response (dose 1 at least four weeks before first infusion, dose 2 four weeks before third infusion). We will always take current national vaccination recommendations into account.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

The experimental group of 26 patients will be compared to a group of 26 placebo-controlled patients. In both groups administration of riluzole will be continued as riluzole is the only proven disease-slowing medication and no interactions with rituximab are known. The standard of care riluzole will not be paused in either group, as the study medication is an add-on medication; placebo is an acceptable control.

Intervention description {11a}

Patients will be randomly allocated to receive rituximab or placebo. The treatment group will receive a total of 4 infusions of 1000 mg rituximab (we will use the biosimilar RIXATHON®, Hexal) in solution of 0.9% sodium chloride. The placebo consists of 0.9% sterile saline solution prepared in identical infusion bags to maintain blinding. Study pharmacists prepare both rituximab and placebo infusions, and clinical staff administering infusions are blinded to treatment allocation. Placebo infusions follow the same volume and infusion rate as rituximab. To preserve blinding and minimize infusion reactions, all participants (both treatment arms) receive standardized premedication (see below).

Infusion time will be approximately 4 h. Both groups will be treated with intravenous infusions of 1000 mg paracetamol, 100 mg methylprednisolone and 4 mg dimetindene maleate as premedication 30 min prior to the study medication. All visits will be hold by study physicians. Administration of study medication/placebo can be carried out after exclusion of infection by history, physical examination and blood control of leukocytes and CRP as well as a urine sample. All safety assessments follow institutional standards. Patients will be seen in the outpatient department of the respective clinic or at the clinical trial unit of the DZNE. The treatment visits 1 and 2 will take place only two weeks apart; followed by treatment visits 4 and 6 every 26 weeks. The 26-week dosing interval is justified by the expected timing of B-cell repopulation following 1000 mg dosing, ensuring sustained B-cell depletion while minimizing unnecessary exposure. Three follow-up visits are planned starting 26 weeks after the last treatment visit and continuing every 26 weeks. Additional safety visits will take place after 15 weeks and 43 weeks during the treatment period and in week 71 during follow-up.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

Subjects are subsequently excluded if they withdraw their consent to participate in the study. The patient has the right to withdraw his/her consent to participate in the study at any time without giving reasons and without any negative consequences for further medical care. The patient is excluded if he/she meets an exclusion criterion in the screening visit or misses too many visits according to the investigator. Additionally, the investigator can decide that the treatment of a subject must be discontinued due to an adverse event or because it poses a risk to the subject or cannot be tolerated from an ethical point of view. Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

As for most of the eligible participants, there is no other disease-modifying therapy available, we expect participants highly motivated to participate throughout the study.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

Concomitant treatment with the standard of care drug riluzole at a stable dose of 50 mg BID for ≥ 30 days prior to screening is mandatory and must be kept, if possible, throughout the study. Premedication must be administered before every infusion of rituximab/placebo (for details see above). Edaravone should not have been used in the week before the baseline visit. Vaccination with live virus vaccines is prohibited during the clinical trial, since the safety of immunization with live viral vaccines following rituximab therapy has not been studied so far.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

In case of premature termination of treatment by the patient or the study physician the follow-up visits (visit 8, 9, 10) should be attended at the same interval as planned (i.e., starting 26 weeks after the last IMP administration) for safety reasons. If that is not possible or favored by the subject, at least one follow-up visit 26 weeks after the last infusion of the study medication (according to visit 8) is strongly recommended.

After completion of the trial, the subjects will continue receiving standard of care treatment according to current national guidelines.

Any decisions regarding post-trial treatment will be made independently by the treating physician based on individual patient circumstances, available evidence, and clinical judgement. Such decisions are entirely separate from the trial protocol and will not be influenced by the study team. Patients who may benefit from continued treatment will be informed about the off-label status of rituximab in ALS and referred to their treating neurologist for independent clinical decision-making. Usually, in patients with autoimmune encephalitis rituximab is discontinued after 4–5 infusions.

Outcomes {12}

The primary endpoint is the ALSFRS-R-SE change from baseline to week 79 (Maier, 2022). The ALSFRS-R-SE (Self-Evaluation) is a modified version of the ALS Functional Rating Scale–Revised in which items are completed by the patient rather than assessed by a clinician. Symptom progression should be evaluated with the ALSFRS-R-SE and its change from baseline (first treatment visit) to 79 weeks after administration of the first dose of Rituximab. The performance of the ALSFRS-R-SE is expected to be similar to the ALSFRS-R with respect to the clinical relevance.

As this is an exploratory pilot, a clinically encouraging signal is defined—independent of statistical significance—as a descriptively slowed ALSFRS-R-SE decline approaching the ALSFRS-R MCID range (~2–4 points or ~20–25% slowing), a delay in time to key milestones (loss of ambulation, initiation of NIV), or sustained stabilization over a defined interval.

Secondary endpoints will be analyzed in an exploratory manner without hierarchical testing or adjustment for multiplicity, given the pilot nature of this trial. Secondary objectives are grouped into clinical, physiological, laboratory, and patient-reported outcome measures. Clinical outcomes include the assessment of motor symptom progression using the ALSFRS-R-SE from baseline to weeks 3, 27, 53, 105, and 130 (visits 2, 4, 6, 9, and 10), as well as tracheostomy-free survival at week 79.

Physiological outcomes comprise changes in body mass index (BMI) assessed longitudinally during the study and pulmonary function measured by slow vital capacity from baseline to week 79. Symptom progression measured with the ALSFRS-R-SE as well as BMI development is the most relevant outcome measure for the individual patient.

Laboratory outcomes include serum and cerebrospinal fluid analyses of infection markers, B-cell counts, and potential changes in the autoantibody repertoire.

CD20+ B-cell depletion kinetics will be related to clinical outcomes in exploratory correlation analyses, and these biomarker analyses will also be used to probe for potential responder subgroups. Failure to achieve or maintain B-cell depletion could be interpreted as a negative mechanistic result even if clinical trends appear favorable, as any clinical signal without target engagement cannot be attributed to the intended mechanism.

Patient-reported and cognitive outcomes include the assessment of cognitive function using the Edinburgh Cognitive and Behavioural ALS Screen (ECAS) and quality-of-life questionnaires, administered at baseline and at week 79 after the first dose of rituximab. ECAS was selected because it is validated for ALS populations across multiple languages and is sensitive to frontotemporal dysfunction commonly observed in ALS patients.

All adverse events and serious adverse events are monitored throughout the entire duration of the trial.

Sample size {14}

A total of 104 patients will be assessed for eligibility, of whom 52 will be allocated to the pilot trial. Based on an anticipated drop-out rate of approximately 20%, around 40 patients are expected to be included in the final analysis.

Rationale for pilot study design

The present study is designed as a pilot trial with non-confirmatory objectives. As no preliminary data are available on the ALSFRS-R-SE score in patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) treated with rituximab, a conventional power calculation for formal hypothesis testing is not feasible. Consequently, the study is not powered to draw confirmatory conclusions regarding treatment efficacy.

The primary objectives of the pilot study are to assess feasibility and to estimate key statistical parameters required for the planning of a subsequent confirmatory trial, in particular the variability (standard deviation) and the preliminary effect size of the ALSFRS-R-SE estimand.

Sample size determination for the pilot study

The sample size of the pilot study was determined based on methodological recommendations for pilot trials, focusing on the precision of variance estimation rather than statistical power. Using the upper confidence limit approach for the standard deviation as implemented in PASS software (version 19; procedure: Sample Size of a Pilot Study by Upper Confidence Limit of SD), an overall sample size of 40 evaluable patients was estimated to provide sufficient precision for planning a future confirmatory trial under the assumptions of a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05, a target power of 80%, a mean difference of D = 2.5 points, and standard deviation value of 6.6.

Allowing for an anticipated drop-out rate of approximately 20%, this results in an envisaged overall sample size of N = 52 patients to be allocated to the pilot trial, corresponding to an estimable standard deviation of approximately 6.6.

Link to the planned confirmatory trial

The variance and effect size estimates obtained from the pilot study will be used to inform the sample size calculation and design assumptions of a subsequent randomized confirmatory trial. For illustration, assuming a two-sided t-test, a mean difference of D = 2.5 in the ALSFRS-R-SE score, and standard deviation value of 7 total sample sizes of 252 patients (1:1 allocation) would be required to achieve 80% power at a significance level of α = 0.05. Accounting for an anticipated drop-out rate of approximately 20%, this would correspond to total sample sizes of approximately 316 patients, respectively.

The assumed values for the ALSFRS-R-SE score are based on analogy with the ALSFRS-R and its performance in previous ALS clinical trials reported in the literature [4, 10, 13].

Progression to a confirmatory phase III trial will be guided by prespecified go/no-go considerations combining clinical, biomarker, and feasibility signals. A descriptively favorable clinical signal (a slowing of ALSFRS-R-SE decline approaching the ALSFRS-R MCID range, as defined above) together with evidence of target engagement (sustained CD20+ B-cell depletion) would support continuation, whereas absence of biological effect would argue against it regardless of apparent clinical trends. Feasibility metrics—recruitment rate, treatment adherence and the observed safety profile—will be treated as formal go/no-go criteria for the confirmatory trial. The variance and effect-size estimates from this pilot will concretely inform the sample size, allocation, and endpoint selection of the confirmatory study, as illustrated above.

Compliance/rate of loss to follow-up

In a recent phase-II trial, mentioned earlier already, that assessed the safety and efficacy of masitinib in a total of 394 ALS patients, only 1 patient needed to be excluded for poor compliance, and 2 patients were lost to follow-up [17]. Similar numbers were seen in a phase-III trial assessing safety and efficacy of edaravone in ALS patients: out of 137 eligible patients a total of 10 patients discontinued treatment [20]. These examples underline the strong adherence to therapy in ALS patients.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

Subjects eligible for treatment will be randomized after final check of in- and exclusion criteria before the first administration. The patients will be randomized into two equal study arms (at each of the two sites) with a total amount of 52 patients. The randomization is stratified by centre to achieve a sufficiently frequent allocation of used therapy arms in both centres. Additional stratification by baseline ALSFRS-R-SE slope, site of onset (bulbar versus limb) or sex was not implemented because the small total sample size (n = 52) would leave individual strata too sparsely populated to permit balanced allocation, and finer stratification carries a risk of incomplete or unfilled strata that can itself introduce imbalance in a trial of this size. To mitigate the resulting risk of chance imbalance, key baseline prognostic variables (baseline ALSFRS-R-SE and slope, site of onset, sex, age and disease duration) will be compared descriptively between treatment groups, and any variable found to be strongly imbalanced will be explored as a covariate in sensitivity analyses of the primary endpoint.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

After checking the in- and exclusion criteria by study physicians a randomization number will be requested by the pharmacy. The authorized staff will assign in chronological order a randomization number. The study medication will be delivered blinded so that investigators and patients will not be aware of the assignment to placebo or rituximab treatment. Preparation of the blinded infusions will be done by the manufacturing company/pharmacy. It is not possible to distinguish between verum and placebo infusions; the labeling of both infusion bags will be identical.

Implementation {16c}

The allocation sequence is generated by the SZB (Studienzentrale Bonn). The generated randomization lists will only be accessible to authorized staff (Safety Management (SZB) and Pharmacy (Medios Solution Bonn GmbH, formally known as Rheinische Compounding and Apotheke Charité Berlin).

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

The study has a double-blind design. All investigator and trial team members will be kept blind to the medication throughout the trial unless medical conditions require unblinding, e.g., in case of SUSARs (suspected unexpected serious adverse reaction). Blinding of the study medication will be carried out by manufacturing companies, the hospital pharmacy of Charité Berlin and the Medios Solution Bonn GmbH (formerly known as Rheinische Compounding). Blinding of treatment assignment is critical to the integrity of this clinical study. In the event of a medical emergency or pregnancy in an individual participant in which knowledge of the investigational product is critical to the subject’s management, the blind for that participant may be broken by the Investigator. The subject’s safety takes priority over any other considerations in determining if a treatment assignment should be unblinded.

Each site receives a total of 52 emergency envelopes to account for the possibilities in patient distribution between the centers.

Unblinding of the administered study medication can be done by the investigator with the help of these emergency envelopes in case of emergency. Before breaking the blind of an individual participant's treatment, the investigator should determine that the unblinded information is necessary, i.e., that it will alter the participant's immediate management. In many cases, particularly when the emergency is clearly not related to the investigational product, the problem may be properly managed by assuming that the participant is receiving active product. When the blind was broken, the Sponsor and project leader must be informed immediately. Unblinding must be documented properly. The investigator must document the reasons for unblinding in the subject’s source documents. The investigator is strongly advised not to divulge the subject’s treatment assignment to any individual not directly involved in managing the medical emergency, or to personnel involved with the analysis and conduct of the study. Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

A descriptive summary analysis is planned for all endpoints and will include mean, minimum, maximum, standard deviation, median and the IQR (interquartile range) for continuous variables and counts and percentages for categorical variables for all randomized patients. The number of patients who were randomized, discontinued or completed the study will be summarized by treatment groups.

Full details of the analysis will be given in the statistical analysis plan. All data will be analyzed by using SAS version 9.4 (or later).

The secondary survival-type endpoints will be analyzed by means of Cox proportional hazard model including at least the effects of treatment and center.

The primary estimand will follow a treatment policy strategy and is defined to descriptively characterize treatment effects in this pilot study using an exploratory MMRM approach.

The modified intention-to-treat (mITT) population will be used for the primary analysis and will comprise all randomized patients who receive at least one dose of double-blind study medication. The individual endpoint is the ALSFRS-R-SE score. Analyses will descriptively characterise changes in ALSFRS-R-SE from baseline to the visit in week 79 between the two treatment groups.

Treatment effects will be estimated using an exploratory mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM), based on all available post-baseline observations (assuming missingness at random (MAR) conditional on the variables included in the model) applying appropriate contrasts to estimate differences in mean change over time. The model will include fixed effects for treatment, center and visit, as well as the repeated effect of time (four visits). All model-based estimates will be interpreted descriptively and for hypothesis-generating purposes only.

As an additional exploratory analysis, a model-based estimator using an analysis of variance (ANOVA) framework will be applied to individual change scores from baseline to week 79. A further sensitivity analysis will use an exploratory MMRM approach combined with imputation methods for missing data, with the aim of descriptively assessing the impact of missingness on the estimated treatment effects. Since missing outcome values may result from intercurrent events such as death, the MAR assumption may be violated. Hence, for sensitivity analysis, no single-value imputation (e.g., last observation carried forward) will be applied. Given the potential association between functional decline, treatment discontinuation, and mortality, sensitivity analyses will assess the robustness of the primary MMRM results to deviations from the MAR assumption, including MNAR/pattern-mixture and tipping-point analyses. Additionally, the reasons for missing outcome values will be listed. Details on the handling of missing values and the deviation from the MAR assumption will be specified in the SAP.

A secondary estimand following a hypothetical strategy will be defined for a per-protocol (PP) population, comprising patients treated according to the protocol for the full study duration without relevant intercurrent events. The same exploratory estimators as for the primary estimand will be applied.

Estimated differences will be interpreted descriptively with reference to mean and median changes in ALSFRS-R-SE and discussed in relation to the Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID).

For the ALSFRS‑R, published estimates of the minimal clinically important difference (MCID/MID) range from approximately 2 to 4 points on the total score (e.g., patient‑reported MID ~3.24 points over follow‑up periods in Fournier et al. [7]) and clinician‑defined meaningful change thresholds (≈20–25% slowing of decline in Castrillo-Viguera et al. [2] in the literature. As no MCID has been formally established for the ALSFRS‑R‑SE, these values are applied by analogy. Accordingly, estimated differences in ALSFRS‑R‑SE observed in this pilot study will be interpreted descriptively in relation to this range, acknowledging the exploratory nature of this approach and the absence of a validated MCID for the SE version.

Secondary endpoints will be summarized using descriptive statistics only. Missing values will generally not be imputed.

Exploratory analyses of survival-type endpoints will be performed using Cox proportional hazards models including at least treatment and center as covariates. Results will be interpreted descriptively.

To explore the robustness of findings, results obtained from the mITT and PP populations will be descriptively compared.

The AEs will be coded using the most recent version of the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) coding. A treatment-emergent adverse event (TEAE) is defined as an event that has onset date on or after the first day of exposure to randomized treatment and no later than re-normalization of CD20+ cells has occurred. We have defined CD20+ B-cell re-normalization as return to pre-treatment baseline levels ± 20%. If the event has onset date before the first day of exposure on randomized treatment and increases in severity during the treatment period and until re-normalization of CD20+ cells, then this event should also be considered as a TEAE. TEAEs are summarized descriptively, whereas non-treatment-emergent AEs are presented in listings. TEAE data will be displayed in terms of the number of subjects with at least one event (N), the percentage of subjects with at least one event.

By sharing the results through open-access and pre-publishing them once they are available, but also by publishing the study protocol before the actual start of the trial, we aim at reaching a broad community and especially other ALS specialists. Specialists from other university hospitals could be possible partners for a following confirmatory trial.

It is planned to present the results of the clinical trial in due time and after mutual coordination with the project leader in a scientific journal and/or at German and international congresses. In principle, preference should be given to a complete publication of the clinical trial. The “Uniform requirements for manuscripts submitted to biomedical journals (International Committee of Medical Journal Editors” (ICMJE) [JAMA 1997; 277: 927-34]), CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) Statement and the FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) Data Principle are taken into account.

The clinical trial is registered in the public register DRKS (German Clinical Trials Register) in accordance with the recommendations of the ICMJE.

Discussion

ALS is a devastating disease leading to disability and death typically within 2–4 years after diagnosis. Rituximab is a medication that is frequently administered in autoimmune-mediated neurological diseases, where it proved to be efficient and safe. We anticipate a comparable safety profile in ALS, based on its tolerability in other neuroimmunological conditions, although this remains to be established in this population. Potential side effects of rituximab include infections, cytokine release syndrome (known in cancer patients), tumour lysis syndrome with acute renal failure, interstitial pneumonitis and severe infusion reactions such as drop in blood pressure or up to cardiac arrest. Many of these side effects are related to treatment trials in patients with myeloma, lymphoma and leukaemia who usually have high comorbidities and are therefore very different to neuroimmunological cohorts and patients in this study. Trials for neuroimmunological indications, such as in neuromyelitis optica (NMO), MS or encephalitis, show a much better safety profile. For example, no relevant side effects were observed in 30 patients with NMO with an average follow-up of 5 years [11]. Additionally, in patients tolerating i.v. rituximab infusions, rituximab can be regularly administered subcutaneously after individual consideration and in an outpatient clinical setting consistent with its established tolerability in these neuroimmunological indications (Mihajlović et al. [16]). ALS patients, which will be recruited for the proposed clinical trial here, will be restricted to patients with a maximal disease duration of 24 months after symptom onset. This will result in a study cohort with comparable to or with even less comorbidity than multiple sclerosis or encephalitis patients and thus in a tolerable safety profile [23]. Patients with ALS will need mechanical ventilation at some point of the disease, here, immunosuppression with rituximab might constitute a risk factor for an increased frequency of pulmonary tract infections. These infections are rare and can usually be treated with antibiotics. In order to minimize the risk of infectious complications, rituximab can only be given after exclusion of an ongoing local or systemic infection by patient history, clinical examination and broad safety laboratory, including white blood cell count, CRP and urine dipstick.

Intravenous infusion of rituximab will be performed by a study nurse and physician in order to assure correct administration and to detect and counteract potential near-term adverse effects immediately. These include infusion-related reactions (IRRs) with allergic reactions such as headache, itchiness, coughing, redness, exanthema, urticaria, hypertonia and fever. This can be controlled with reduction of infusion speed, antipyretics and antihistamines. Additionally, sodium chloride infusions, bronchodilatative medication and glucocorticoids will be administered in case of severe adverse events. If IRRs occur, infusion can be stopped immediately. Based on the trial protocol, regular clinical visits will be performed to assure patient safety and include monitoring of vital signs.

Together, the possible slight increase in infections by far exceeds the potential clinical benefit in this rapidly progressing disease. Based on the safety assessment and considered mechanism of action, the risk/benefit balance of this study is favorable.

This trial represents the first targeted evaluation of B-cell depletion therapy in ALS. Unlike previous immunosuppression trials using broad-spectrum agents (corticosteroids, cyclophosphamide, azathioprine) that lacked specificity and were associated with excessive side effects, rituximab offers specific targeting of the humoral arm of autoimmunity. Recent mechanistic evidence identifying pathogenic autoantibodies, oligoclonal B-cell expansions in CSF, and B-cell-dependent neuronal injury in preclinical models provides strong rationale for this targeted approach. By specifically depleting CD20+ B-cells while preserving other immune functions, rituximab may reduce autoimmune-mediated neuronal injury with an acceptable safety profile established in other neuroimmunological conditions.

This trial is explicitly designed as a mechanism-informed, signal-seeking phase-IIa study rather than a confirmatory efficacy trial. Its purpose is to establish biological plausibility, to assess whether B-cell depletion produces the expected biomarker modulation (sustained CD20+ B-cell depletion and changes in the autoantibody repertoire), and to evaluate feasibility, alongside exploratory clinical readouts. The clinical estimates from this pilot are therefore interpreted descriptively and for hypothesis-generating purposes, and are not powered to demonstrate a definitive disease-modifying effect. Accordingly, we caution against inferring a specific clinical effect size from this study; a favorable mechanistic and feasibility signal, even in the absence of statistically significant clinical benefit, would be considered an informative and encouraging result.

We further acknowledge that ALS is a clinically heterogeneous disease. Even within sporadic ALS of less than 24 months’ duration, disease trajectories vary substantially according to site of onset (bulbar versus limb), rate of functional decline and other prognostic factors. This heterogeneity can dilute an apparent treatment effect in a small cohort and complicate its interpretation. Site of onset and baseline progression rate are therefore recorded at baseline and, as described above, compared descriptively between groups and explored in sensitivity analyses; they are also candidate variables for the exploratory identification of potential responder subgroups that could inform stratification in a future confirmatory trial.

The eligibility criteria deliberately select a relatively preserved, early-stage ALS population (disease duration ≤ 24 months, slow vital capacity ≥ 75% predicted, no established need for assisted ventilation) in order to minimize infection and respiratory risk during immunosuppressive treatment. A consequence of this choice is that the findings may not extrapolate to later-stage ALS or to patients already requiring ventilatory support, and bulbar-onset disease was neither capped nor stratified at randomization. We regard site of onset as a potential effect modifier, and future trials may need stratified or stage-specific designs to establish efficacy across the full spectrum of ALS.
