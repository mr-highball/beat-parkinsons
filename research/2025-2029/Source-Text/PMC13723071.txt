Abstract

Background

Multiple single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) studies of the vulnerable brain regions of Parkinson’s disease (PD) have revealed alterations in brain cell populations and cell-type–specific transcriptomes. However, a systematic analysis of cell-type–resolved gene regulatory architecture in PD is lacking.

Results

Here, we develop an integrative, metacell-based multiscale network analysis (MCMNA) of snRNA-seq data from the substantia nigra to systematically uncover molecular mechanisms and identify potential therapeutic targets for PD. MCMNA overcomes the inherent sparsity of single-cell data by leveraging metacell-based aggregation, enabling construction of robust gene regulatory networks. Gene coexpression network analysis identifies cell-type–specific gene modules associated with PD. Integration of metacell-based differential gene expression and a Bayesian causal network systematically reveals putative driver genes and their hierarchies.

Conclusions

Our multiscale network models provide a framework for prioritizing candidate molecular regulators and pathways for further investigation of disease mechanisms and therapeutic targets.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, with both motor and nonmotor symptoms [1, 2]. The pathology of PD is characterized by the progressive loss of dopaminergic (DA) neurons in the substantia nigra (SN) and accumulation of Lewy bodies (LBs), which contain abnormal α-synuclein protein aggregation [3, 4]. Rare variants in over 20 genes account for 3–5% of PD cases in most populations, most of which are highly penetrant and often known to be causal genes [5]. To date, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified 90 genetic risk variants, which collectively account for 16–36% of the heritable risk of nonmonogenic PD [6]. Given that most patients with PD have no familial history or known PD mutations, there remains a critical gap in understanding how molecular signatures relate to disease mechanisms and therapeutic strategies in idiopathic PD. While the loss of DA neurons is a hallmark of the disease, microglial activation was first reported in PD brains in 1988 [7] and has been supported by in vivo and in vitro studies [8–11]. In addition, astrocyte reactivity has been prominently observed in the SN of patients with PD, with research highlighting both neuroprotective and neurotoxic roles such as production of oxidative and nitrosative stress, inflammation, and contribution to the blood–brain barrier [12–15]. Recently, studies have indicated oligodendrocyte dysfunction in PD, with changes pointing to altered inflammatory responses, protein folding stress, and myelination abnormalities [16–18].

The gene regulatory network (GRN) uncovers new pathways and gene targets in biological processes in human disease. Previously, our labs integrated a multiscale transcriptomic network analysis of PD SN samples by assembling the gene expression profiles from 8 published bulk RNA-seq studies to systematically identify intrinsic network structures and key regulators such as STMN2 in PD [19]. Bulk tissue datasets do not have cell-type–level granularity, thus limiting the understanding of complex signaling across cell–cell populations and within a population. Recently, single-cell/single-nucleus RNA sequencing (sc/snRNA-seq) has emerged as a promising tool to unveil cell-level signaling pathways associated with neurodegenerative diseases including PD. We and others have profiled single nuclei of human SN from PD and healthy controls, identifying changes in gene expression and new biological pathways at single-cell levels [20–25]. While single-cell datasets continue to expand in public repositories, little progress has been made toward cell-type–specific GRN analysis for PD.

The challenges in constructing single-cell GRN inference mainly stem from the high sparsity in gene expression profiles (large percentage of missing values or zeros) in snRNA-seq data. Many model-based imputation methods have been developed, yet the performance of these methods varies across different datasets [26]. To address this challenge, recent studies have adopted the concept of “metacells” [27] and developed several metacell algorithms [27–30] to not only compress data but also enhance the signal-to-noise ratio in expression matrices. This new approach results in higher clustering accuracy, improved annotation, and increased reliability in the identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Despite the potential, these metacell algorithms have yet to be applied to gene network analysis in PD [31].

In this study, we develop an integrative metacell-based gene coexpression and causal network analysis framework and apply it to a recently published PD snRNA-seq dataset in the human SN from 23 patients with PD and 9 healthy controls [20]. By adopting a systematic and data-driven approach in both coexpression and causal networks, we identify neuron- and glia-specific subnetworks that are dysregulated in PD. These cell-type– specific networks not only reveal putative key drivers for PD but also shed light on the molecular mechanisms of PD. Notably, the top putative drivers include known targets of PD such as UCHL1, NSF, and PREPL in neurons, as well as novel candidate genes, such as CADPS2, APBB1IP, and HSP90AA1 in neurons or glial cells. In addition, PD driver genes (STMN2 and RALYL) identified in our previously bulk RNA-seq network analysis are also key drivers in the respective neuronal single-cell networks. Altogether, our study provides a framework for integrating diverse, cell-type–specific networks to pinpoint putative drivers of disease networks.

Results

Integrative network biology framework for decoding molecular mechanisms of Parkinson’s disease at the single-cell level

To systematically model single-cell transcriptomic data in PD, we develop an integrative, metacell-based multiscale network analysis (MCMNA) framework that includes metacell construction and characterization, coexpression network analysis, causal network inference, and key driver analysis (Fig. 1). In brief, we first group single cells into metacells—a group of cells with highly similar gene expression profiles (Table 1). To preserve the cell-type annotation and phenotype information from snRNA-seq data, the metacell analysis is performed for each condition in each cell cluster. Single-cell profiles within each metacell are aggregated to create a metacell gene expression profile. Then, DEGs between the disease and control are identified, and functional enrichment analysis is performed on the DEGs to identify dysregulated pathways in PD (Fig. 1A). Next, we construct metacell-based coexpression analysis using multiscale embedded gene coexpression network analysis (MEGENA) [32] for each cell type to identify cell-type–specific coexpression modules (groups of highly coexpressed genes) (Fig. 1B). In parallel, we construct the global Bayesian probabilistic causal network (BN) [33, 34] and perform key driver analysis (KDA) [35] to predict potential key regulators, termed key drivers (KDs) of PD (Fig. 1C). A unique feature of MCMNA is that it addresses the sparsity of single-cell gene expression data and increases the robustness of the single-cell network by the generation of metacells, construction of a biologically meaningful correlation (BMC) matrix for the coexpression network, and additional pruning of the data matrix for causal network. Additionally, network models based on both PD and control allow us to capture early changes driving PD, as such signals may not change across various disease stages.

[20]. The study profiled a total of 315,867 high-quality nuclei and identified 12 distinct cell clusters.

Metacells uncover cell-type–specific gene expression enrichment between PD and control

At each cell cluster and condition (PD or control), single-cell profiles within each metacell are aggregated to generate metacell gene expression profiles, either by averaging the log-normalized single-cell expression data (average—“avg”) or by summing the raw single-cell values, followed by log normalization (sum). Additionally, the method allows for the inclusion of metacell size (the number of single cells in each metacell) as a weighting factor (weighted) or omitting it (unweighted). To explore global transcriptomic changes using the metacell method, we first examine the number of DEGs in each cell cluster (at least 1.1-fold change [FC]) and false discovery rate (adjusted P value less than 0.05) and calculate the percentage of preservation compared to the single-cell method. The average method demonstrates higher preservation compared to the sum method across most clusters (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. S1A, B). While the number of DEGs is reduced in metacells compared to the single-cell method, the number of conserved markers increases, potentially reflecting enhanced cell cluster specificity and reduced noise in metacells (Supplementary Fig. S1C). However, this observation has not been formally tested, and further studies are needed to confirm it.

While weighted aggregation can incorporate cell-level weight, it substantially increases the data size and computation time, without consistently improving downstream analyses such as differential expression and pathway enrichment (Supplementary Fig. S1A–C, Supplementary Table S1). Therefore, the unweighted method provides a good balance between efficiency and accuracy. Distinct gene signatures of 5 major brain cell types (e.g., neurons, microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and oligodendrocyte progenitor cells [OPCs]) are highlighted using the selected metacell method (average and unweighted) (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. S1E, Table 2, Supplementary Table S3). Next, we compare our DEG signatures with other published snRNA-seq data of human SN from 3 independent cohorts in PD [21, 22, 25]. We reprocess the original datasets using our own analytical pipeline to identify cell clusters (Supplementary Tables S4–S10, Supplementary Fig. S1F–G, I–J, L–M). We then assess the overlap of DEG signatures in each cell type using Fisher’s exact test (FET). The results show that, for 3 datasets, there is a decrease in corrected P value but an increase in fold enrichment, indicating reduced statistical preservation strength but greater specificity in the preservation of DEG signatures across major cell cluster between the 2 datasets in metacells (Supplementary Tables S11–S13, Supplementary Fig. S1H, K, N).

Metacells reveal common and unique cell-type–specific biological pathways between PD and control

Functional enrichment analysis is performed on the DEG signatures from both metacell and single-cell methods. Using selected conditions (average and unweighted), we identify common and unique biological pathways. We observe that 10–30% and 0–60% shared pathways in major cell types from upregulated and downregulated DEGs, respectively (Supplementary Tables S14–S16, Supplementary Fig. S2A, B). Similar to previously reported results, upregulated genes across cell types are involved in heat stress, Hsf1 activation, protein folding, and the cell migration pathway. In contrast, downregulated genes are associated with nervous system development, synaptic transmission, neurotransmitter transport across cell types, and glial cell development in glial cells (Fig. 2C). Many pathways unique to the single-cell and metacell methods are also detected (Supplementary Tables S17–S18, Supplementary Fig. S2A, B). Positive correlation of both upregulated and downregulated pathways across cell types indicates highly conserved pathways between the 2 methods. Additionally, several pathways showing a significant difference in enrichment in the single-cell and metacell-based DEG signatures are highly relevant to PD biological pathways. These include protein folding in glial cells, autophagy in astrocytes, mitochondrial processes in microglia and astrocytes, cytokine production in microglia, vasculature development, neurotransmitter release, and calcium transport in neurons (Fig. 2D, E).

Additional metacell-specific DEGs showed biologically coherent and cell-type–associated functional enrichment. In glial populations, these genes were enriched for pathways related to metal ion response and ion homeostasis, driven in part by metallothionein genes (MT1M, MT1G, MT1X, and MT1E) (Supplementary Tables S19–S20). In neuronal populations, metacell-specific DEGs were predominantly enriched for pathways related to synaptic transmission. Comparison with DEG results from two independent datasets (Columbia dataset [25] and KAIST dataset [21]) further supported the biological relevance. Recurrently identified genes such as heat-shock and stress-response genes (BAG3, DNAJA4, STIP1, and FKBP4) are consistent with the enhanced detection of stress–response pathways in the metacell analysis in the glial population (Supplementary Table S21). Synaptic genes, including GRIN2B and GAD1, also overlapped with DEGs identified in the independent Columbia dataset [25], supporting the stronger enrichment of synaptic pathways detected using the metacell approach (Supplementary Table S21).

A novel framework to address single-cell sparsity in the coexpression network

To understand the interactions among genes, we construct gene coexpression networks to identify gene modules using MEGENA under the MCMNA framework (Supplementary Fig. S3A). While metacells improve the gene–gene correlation, as noted in previous studies [27–30], gene–gene correlation still suffers from data sparsity. Therefore, zero counts are removed for each gene pair before calculating Spearman correlation coefficients (SCCs), leading to an increase in overall correlation distribution in metacells compared to single cells (Supplementary Fig. S3B). To address inherent noise in sc/snRNA-seq data observed by the drift in the mean signed SCCs across cell types, we propose a framework to create a biologically meaningful correlation (BMC) matrix at the optimal threshold of number of cells and genes (Supplementary Fig. S3A, C). We perform FET analysis between STMN2-related genes and STMN2 perturbation DEG signatures in PD mouse SN [19] across all clusters and 3 neuronal clusters. Compared to the metacell false discovery rate (FDR) only and single-cell matrix only, the BMC approach yields higher −log10 (FDR) and fold enrichment in the MC-average comparison, as well as higher fold enrichment in the MC-sum comparison (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table S22). These suggest that the BMC matrix indeed reduces noise in the weak positive correlation, resulting in a more consistent gene subset aligned with the STMN2 perturbation. Averaging log-normalized expression values indicates more statistically significant enrichment and is therefore used for downstream analysis (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table S22). The optimized BMC matrix is then subjected to MEGENA. Additionally, this method enables construction of a gene coexpression network for the small neuron cluster 9 (c9), which was not possible due to high sparsity and small sample size. No MEGENA modules are found in the fibroblast cluster 10 and the T-cell cluster 11.

To assess potential bias from excluding zero-count observations, we correlate each gene’s average expression with its MEGENA connectivity (node degree) using Spearman’s correlation. Correlations are weak across all clusters (|ρ| < 0.2; Supplementary Fig. S3D), indicating that network connectivity is largely independent of baseline expression. Only cluster 2 (astrocyte) and cluster 7 (neuron) show moderate negative correlations (ρ = −0.2 to −0.3), suggesting that lower-expressed genes tend to have slightly higher connectivity. These results demonstrate that excluding zero-count observations does not preferentially favor highly expressed genes.

Metacell-based coexpressed networks reveal cell-type–specific functional gene modules

In total, we identify 1,057 significant gene modules (FDR-adjusted P values less than 0.05) in all cell types and 833 modules in 5 major cell types (Table 3, Supplementary Table S23). To annotate biological functions associated with each module, functional enrichment analysis is performed. To prioritize gene modules associated with PD, a summary score is calculated based on the following FET enrichment tests: (i) enrichment for the DEG signatures between PD and control (upregulated [UP] and downregulated [DN] DEGs) and (ii) enrichment for the PD genetic risk variants (PD GWAS catalog) (Supplementary Table S24) [36]. Given that the central nervous system consists of various cell types, each with distinct roles in PD pathogenesis and progression, we focus on the major brain cell types: neurons (cluster 6 [c6], cluster 7 [c7], and cluster 9 [c9]), astrocytes (cluster 2 [c2]), microglia (cluster 1 [c1]), OPCs (cluster 5 [c5]), and oligodendrocytes (cluster 0 [c0] and cluster 3 [c3]). The findings of MEGENA modules reveal cell-type–specific functional modules characterized by highly coordinated molecular dysregulations in PD (Supplementary Tables S25–S27). The top 10 PD-associated gene modules within each major cell type are shown in Fig. 3B. Among the top 50 ranked modules, there are 17 neuronal modules, of which 14 originate from the neuron cluster c9. The number of top-ranked modules from other glial cells is as follows: oligodendrocytes (c0: 5, c3: 13), microglia (c1: 4), astrocytes (c2: 7), and OPC (c5: 4).

We validate the biological coherence of our network modules in another snRNA-seq dataset from the SN of patients with PD and healthy participants (termed the Ma dataset) at the cell-type–specific level [25]. As shown in Supplementary Tables S28–S29, based on a global approach across all identified modules, module preservation is highest in oligodendrocytes (~30–50%), followed by microglia/astrocytes (~20–30%) and neurons (~5–20%) (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Additionally, we examine the conservation of the PD-related modules (i.e., modules enriched for upregulated, downregulated, and/or PD GWAS genes) in the 2 datasets. PD-related modules show higher and more significant preservation across all cell clusters (Supplementary Tables S30–S31). Similar to the global approach, module preservation is strongest in oligodendrocytes (35–90%), followed by astrocytes/microglia (~60–90%) and neurons (~0–30%). Notably, in our dataset, the PD-related modules in neuron cluster C9 show greater preservation in the corresponding neuron cluster C4 from the Ma dataset than the PD-related modules in neuron cluster C6 of our dataset show in C4 of the Ma dataset (Supplementary Fig. S4B).

We additionally performed a network-based module preservation analysis using weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) [37]. This approach evaluates preservation of the modules from our dataset in the Ma dataset. Overall, the module preservation results are consistent with those from the FET analysis. Moderate-to-high module preservation is observed across major cell types, with the highest preservation in oligodendrocytes, followed by microglia and astrocytes, while module preservation is more variable across neuronal clusters. PD-associated modules generally show greater preservation than total global modules (Supplementary Tables S32–S33). These results are broadly consistent with those from the FET analysis and provide complementary evidence for the cross-cohort reproducibility of PD-associated gene modules.

Neuronal gene modules are most dysregulated in PD

Among all the modules, the module M3 in the coexpression network from c9 is ranked as the top for its most significant enrichment of the downregulated DEGs using FET (multiple testing-corrected P or FDR = 1.7E-200, 2.3-fold enrichment [FE]) and GWAS genes (FDR = 8.1E-04, 2-FE) (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table S25). The global network of c9 shows a highly hierarchical structure, suggesting strong modular organization and multiscale biological processes (Fig. 3C). Seven child modules of the module M3 in c9’s coexpression network are highly enriched for downregulated DEGs such as M27─▸M70─▸M100 (M27: FDR = 5.8E-18, 2.3 FE), M28─▸M74 (M28: FDR = 1.1E-20, 2.4 FE), M29─▸M79 (M29: FDR = 9.3E-24, 2.2 FE), M32─▸M82─▸M81 (M32: FDR = 2.2 E-16, 2.4 FE), M30 (FDR = 9.5 E-11, 2.8 FE), M31 (FDR = 5.8E-18, 2.5 FE), and M33 (FDR = 1.2 E-11, 2.7 FE) (Fig. 3E, Supplementary Table S26). M3 is associated with synapse organization (FDR P = 7.9E-24). All other child modules of M3 are also enriched for downregulated DEGs with synapse-related functions such as synapse assembly, regulation of neuron projection development, regulation of transsynaptic signaling, neuronal system, receptor localization to synapses, and dendrite development (Fig. 3F, Supplementary Table S25, Supplementary Table S27). These child modules of M3 are also among the top 50 modules from all cell clusters and among the top 20 modules from c9 (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Table S25). Interestingly, other branches of c9 separated from the M3 module are not enriched for the downregulated DEGs. These modules are associated with pathways such as ion transport, metabolic processes, and transcription, suggesting that only a small subset of c9 neurons appears to adopt the disease phenotype.

Apart from the neuron cluster c9, the modules in cluster c6 are less enriched for DEGs. In c6, a few top-ranked modules are associated with upregulated DEGs, such as M5 (FDR = 1.7E-08, 4.3 FE), M13 (FDR = 1.7E-08, 10 FE), M126 (FDR = 1.7E-08, 45 FE), and M8 (FDR = 3.73E-05, 4.6 FE), followed by modules associated with downregulated DEGs such as M92 (FDR = 1.7E-05, 16.9 FE) andM124 (FDR = 1.7, 36.1 FE) (Supplementary Fig. S5A, Supplementary Table S26). These are also linked to pathways involved in synapse organization and axon development (Figure S5B, Supplementary Table S25, Supplementary Table S27). Interestingly, no module in the network from the neuron cluster c7 is enriched for DEGs (Supplementary Table S25), though many modules have meaningful biological functions (Supplementary Fig. S5C). The global network of c7 also shows a more compact structure (Supplementary Fig. S5D). This implies that the dysregulated genes in c7 are not well coordinated.

Glial gene modules associated with PD are involved in the development process, metabolic processes, and immune responses

In addition to neuronal modules, we also identify numerous glial modules in oligodendrocytes, microglia, astrocytes, and OPCs. Oligodendrocytes represent the largest population in the dataset, and consequently, the single-cell coexpression networks of cluster 0 (c0) and cluster 3 (c3) are among the largest, containing many genes and gene interactions. Among the 50 top-ranked modules overall of c3, M10─▸M41─▸M164 emerged as 1 branch of particular interest. M10 is a parent module of M41 and M42, with its members enriched with both upregulated (FDR = 2.9E-05, 4.3 FE) and downregulated DEGs (FDR = 5.9E-34, 10.3 FE) and involved in cell polarity (FDR = 0.005), mitochondria organization (FDR P = 0.05), and vesicle organization (FDR = 0.05) (Fig. 4A, B, Supplementary Fig. S6A). M10 has 2 branches, M41─▸M164 and M42─▸M166. One is the M41─▸M164 branch, in which both modules are enriched with downregulated DEGs (M41: FDR = 1.4E-43, 16 FE; M164: FDR = 1E-44, 17 FE), and their members are associated with autophagy of mitochondria (M41: FDR = 0.04; M164: FDR = 0.04) (Supplementary Fig. S6B). Another is M42─▸M166, with both modules enriched with upregulated DEGs (M42: FDR = 3.7E-11, 12 FE; M166: FDR = 1.7E-06, 11 FE) and associated with the apoptosis pathway (M42: FDR = 0.006; M166: FDR = 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. S6C). Some branches in the network of c3 are also associated with protein folding (M8─▸M38─▸M159) and oligodendrocyte differentiation (M5─▸M20─▸M111), highlighting the diverse pathways underlying oligodendrocyte functions. The network from the cell cluster c0 has a slightly smaller number of modules ranked among the top 50 modules from all cell clusters; the most significant module M3 is enriched for both upregulated (FDR = 2.2E-04, 2.1 FE) and downregulated DEGs (FDR = 1E-15, 2.3 FE), and it is associated with actin filament organization (FDR = 1.5E-04). Several child modules of M3 from c0 are also enriched for DEGs, including M6─▸M67 (involved in nervous system development), M8 (involved in metabolic processes), and M17 (involved in nervous system development) (Supplementary Fig. S6E, F).

We also identify several significant modules in the astrocyte cluster c2. Among those, the most significant module M3 is enriched for the downregulated DEGs (FDR = 2.1E-38, 3.6 FE) and the GWAS genes (FDR = 4E-04, 1.9 FE) (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. S7A). Some of M3’s child modules are functionally important and enriched for downregulated genes. The M3─▸M59 branch is highly associated with regulation of small GTPase mediate signaling transduction (M3: FDR = 2.1E-10; M59: FDR = 0.004) (Fig. 4D), while the M3─▸M64 branch is associated with synapse assembly (M64: FDR = 5.1E-03) (Fig. 4E). On the other hand, the modules M6 and M72 are highly enriched for upregulated DEGs (M6: FDR = 5.8E-14, 21.3 FE; M72: FDR = 2.35E-08, 18 FE) (Supplementary Fig. S7B) and linked to inclusion body assembly (M6: FDR = 1.7E-04) and regulation of endothelial cell chemotaxis (FDR = 2.5E-03) (Supplementary Fig. S7C). These findings demonstrate the role of astrocytes in modulating and supporting neuronal and synaptic functions.

Furthermore, we identify several microglial modules highly associated with PD. The top microglial module M15, which is associated with mononuclear cell differentiation, is enriched for downregulated genes (FDR = 4.8E-09, 6.6 FE) (Fig. 4F, G, Supplementary Fig. S7C). Additionally, we also observe a few branches associated with GTPase activity. While the branch of M16─▸M70─▸M156 is associated with downregulated DEGs (M16: FDR = 3.9E-08, 6.1 FE; M70: FDR = 1.7E-07, 6.3 FE; M156: FDR = 9.7E-08, 6.7 FE) (Supplementary Fig. S7D), the branch of M12─▸M55 is associated with upregulated DEGs (M12: FDR = 3.6E-04, 8.7 FE; M55: FDR = 5.4–04, 13.8 FE) (Supplementary Fig. S7E). The presence of coexpressed genes with roles in innate immune signaling and GTPase activity might suggest the role of certain gene modules in translating or facilitating myonuclear cells to microglia into more reactive states.

Last but not least, although the modules in the network of OPCs are less enriched for DEGs compared to other cell types, several modules are enriched for downregulated DEGs (Supplementary Fig. S7F) and associated with glial cell development, nervous system development, and synaptic transmission (Supplementary Fig. S7G). Interestingly, these downregulated modules are the daughter modules of M2 (FDR = 0.01, 1.3 FE) with diverse functions, including the branch M6─▸M57 (M6: FDR = 2.9E-07, 6.4 FE; M57–cFET = 5.3E-07, 7.3 FE) involved in regulation of transsynaptic signaling (M6: FDR = 6.1E-03, M57: FDR = 1.4E-03) and the branch M8─▸M67 (M8: FDR = 5.3E-03, 5.3 FE; M67: FDR = 3.8E-04, 8.1 FE) involved in glial cell differentiation (M8: FDR = 1.8E-04; M67: FDR = 1.4E-06). These findings suggest that certain components of OPCs support both neuronal and glial functions, which have been understudied in the field.

Metacell-based Bayesian networks predict well-known and novel key drivers

While the gene coexpression network analysis identifies coherent gene modules associated with PD in each major brain cell type, the findings remain inherently associative and do not establish causal relationships among genes. To move beyond correlation and uncover potential regulatory mechanisms, we construct Bayesian probabilistic causal networks (BNs) to identify gene–gene causal relationships. Given the sparsity existing in the datasets, we further remove additional genes and metacells in a process of pruning to find the optimal cutoff (Fig. 1C). To evaluate whether expression-based pruning preferentially excluded transcriptional regulators, we examined transcription factors (TFs) before and after applying the Tg = 0.4 threshold. Across all major cell types, around 38–77% of TFs were removed, compared with 54–74% of genes overall. Thus, TFs were not preferentially depleted by the filtering procedure (Supplementary Table S34).

After that, we construct a global BN for each cell cluster, followed by KDA, to predict key regulators for each cell cluster [19, 38] (Fig. 5A, B, Supplementary Fig. S8A, Fig. 6A, B, Supplementary Fig. S9A, C, Supplementary Fig. S9E). We identify a total of 151 key drivers (138 unique genes), and interestingly, several drivers are detected in multiple cell clusters (Table 4, Supplementary Table S35). We also estimate the relevance of each gene to PD as the co-occurrence of a given gene and PD in PubMed. Among 138 unique key drivers, 43 (~30%) have occurrence above or equal to 5 (Fig. 5C). Then, we classify the key drivers into 2 categories: (i) well-studied key drivers (co-occurrence ≥5) and (ii) novel key drivers (co-occurrence <5) (Table 4, Supplementary Table S36).

Novel and known key drivers of neuronal clusters converge on synaptic regulation

The key drivers of neuronal clusters c7 and c9 are among the most significant drivers across all cell clusters. The most significant driver is CADPS2 in c9 (KDA FDR = 1.8E-39; 1.8 FE), which is the most downregulated in c9 (FC = 0.48, FDR = 5.2E-35). In neurons, CADPS2 encodes a calcium-binding protein that regulates synaptic exocytosis and dense core vesicles [39, 40] and has been previously studied in dopamine release [41]. Also, CADPS2 is a novel small neuronal cell marker in idiopathic PD [42]. CADPS2 is also a member of the gene modules, including M3, M32, and M81, which are associated with synapse organization and neural system pathways (Supplementary Table S23, Supplementary Table S23). The BN in c9 also indicates CADPS2 as an upstream driver of 4 downregulated key drivers such as CDH18, RYR2, UNC13C, and FSTL5, 3 of which (CDH18, RYR2, and UNC13C) play roles in calcium-dependent synaptic cell–cell adhesion [43, 44], release of calcium ions [45], and exocytosis [46], respectively (Fig. 5D, Supplementary Table S36). Interestingly, the 2-layer subnetwork centered on CAPDS2 (111 genes) is also involved in the modulation of chemical synaptic transmission (FDR = 2E-04), regulation of transsynaptic signaling (FDR = 2.1E-03), and synaptic vesicle docking (FDR = 3.2E-03) (Supplementary Table S37). These genes form a coordinated subnetwork to modulate a calcium-mediated process, which is essential for neuronal communication and synaptic function.

RALYL, previously identified as a downregulated key regulator from the network analysis of bulk transcriptomic data in PD [19], also emerges as a key driver in c9 (KDA: FDR = 2.1E-09; 2.2 FE; expression: 0.6 FC, FDR = 9.8E-23) (Supplementary Fig. S8B, Supplementary Table S36). RALYL is a member of M3, M29, and M74 (Supplementary Table S23). The subnetwork around RALYL is associated with metabolic pathways such as cell surface receptor protein tyrosine kinase activity (FDR P = 3.7E-03), cellular response to histamine (FDR = 1E-02), and hypoxia (FDR = 1E-02) (Supplementary Table S37). Thirty of the 36 drivers in c9, which are downregulated in PD, are novel as only 6 genes have been associated with PD or studied in PD (Table 4, Supplementary Table S35).

In contrast to c9, 24 of the 54 drivers in c7 have well-known functions associated with PD (24/54 genes). For example, a major driver, UCHL1 (ubiquitin C-terminal hydroxylase), is implicated in the early onset of family and sporadic PD [5], influencing α-synuclein degradation and contributing to PD susceptibility [47]. In cluster c7, UCHL1 is significantly downregulated and enriched in cluster 7 (KDA: FDR = 8E-06, FE = 2; expression: FC = 0.8, FDR = 5.4E-22). BN results indicate UCHL1 is the downstream effector of 2 key drivers, STMN2 (previously identified as a key driver in bulk transcriptomic data) and OAT (Fig. 5E, Supplementary Table S36). The subnetwork around UCHL1 is involved in regulation of high-voltage-gated calcium channel activity (FDR = 6.3E-02), insulin secretion (FDR = 6.3E-02), and membrane invagination (FDR P = 6.3E-02) (Supplementary Table S37).

Besides UCHL1, we also identify another GWAS-associated gene, NSF, as a downregulated key driver in c7 (KDA: FDR = 5.2E-34, FE = 1.9; expression: FC = 0.7, FDR = 1.8E-56). NSF encodes N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive fusion protein, which plays an essential role in intracellular vesicle transport, synaptic transmission, and membrane fusion [48], interacting with α-synuclein via the SNARE complex protein [49], with a diminishing expression level in an MPTP mouse model of PD [50] and lower blood and brain levels in PD [51]. Additionally, NSF also directly regulates other key drivers such as ATP1B1, SV2B, and STXBP1, which play a role in regulating the ATPase Na+/K+ pump [52], vesicle trafficking and exocytosis [53], and neurotransmitter release [54], respectively (Supplementary Fig. S8F, Supplementary Table S36). The subnetwork centered on NSF is also involved in protein localization to the plasma membrane (FDR = 9.4E-06), neuron projection development (FDR = 1.6E-04), and synaptic vesicle membrane organization (FDR = 5.02E-03) (Supplementary Table S37), highlighting the functional context of the PD GWAS gene.

STMN2, previously identified as a regulator [19], is also a key driver in c7 (FDR = 8E-09, 1.9 FE) and downregulated (FC = 0.8, FDR = 7.5E-15) (Fig. 5F, Supplementary Table S36). STMN2 is known to regulate microtubule dynamics, axon formation, and neurite outgrowth during development [55–57]. The subnetwork around STMN2 also indicates the enrichment of pathways such as regulation of microtubule depolymerization (FDR = 3.4E-02), regulation of macroautophagy (FDR = 3.9E-02), and polymeric cytoskeletal fiber (FDR P = 9.5E-02) (Supplementary Table S37). The subnetworks around STMN2 are enriched for the knockdown signature of STMN2 in mouse SN [19], validating the accuracy of our predicted metacell-based causal network (Fig. 5G).

Additionally, we also identify PREPL as a key driver (KDA: FDR = 1.7E-18, FE = 2.2; expression: FC = 0.8, FDR = 3.0E-23). PREPL encodes a propyl endopeptidase-like enzyme and has recently been implicated in neuronal functions, including protein trafficking, endocytosis, synaptic activity [58, 59], and the facilitation of α-synuclein aggregation seeding [60], highlighting its relevance as a candidate for further investigation. The subnetwork around PREPL (161 genes) suggests PREPL as downstream of ATP1B1 and upstream of many other key drivers such as STXBP1, SLC9A6, RTN1, NPTN, TSPYL4, SV2A, and SERINC1 (Fig. 5H, Supplementary Table S36). This network is involved in positive regulation of synaptic transmission (FDR = 2.6E-03), negative regulation of amyloid beta formation (FDR = 2.6E-03), and intracellular monoatomic cation homeostasis (FDR = 2.6E-03) (Supplementary Table S37).

In cluster c6, we identify the PD GWAS gene, MDGA2, as an upregulated key driver (KDA: FDR = 2.1E-08, FE = 3.3; expression: FC = 1.2, FDR = 2.1E-05). MDGA2, which encodes a regulator of axonal growth [61], contains a single-nucleotide polymorphism that has been potentially linked to earlier onset of familial PD [62]. The subnetwork around MDGA2 (68 genes), which is involved in chemical synaptic transmission (FDR = 2.7E-02) and synapse organization (FDR P = 3.5E-02), shows that MDGA2 directly regulates another key driver, NPAS3 (Supplementary Fig. S6E, Supplementary Tables S36–S37).

Emerging key drivers in glial cells suggest novel causal roles in homeostasis, metabolic responses, immunity, and heat shock response

In oligodendrocytes, MOBP (myelin-associated oligodendrocyte basic protein) is identified as a key driver. MOBP plays a role in myelination and is a structural constituent of the myelin sheath [63, 64]. This gene is the top driver with the highest network significance and is downregulated in c3 (KDA: FDR P = 9.2E-42, 5.5 FE; 0.8 FC, FDR = 3E-59) and c0 (KDA: FDR = 2.7E-15, 6.05 FE; expression: 0.9 FC, FDR P = 6.3E-100). The subnetworks around MOBP in c3 and c0 indicate MOBP as an upstream regulator of key drivers such as NEAT1, COL4A5, CARNS1, and LINC01505 in c3 (Fig. 6B) and CARNS1 in c0 (Supplementary Fig. S9B). In c3, the subnetwork around MOBP (307 genes) is associated with regulation of dendritic spine development (P = 2.7E-04), myelination (P = 9.9E-04), neuronal cell–cell adhesion (P = 1.1E-03), and protein localization to synapse (P = 2.1E-03), while in c0, the subnetwork (61 genes) is associated with transition metal ion transport (P = 1.1E-04), substantia nigra development (P = 3.3E-04), and regulation of neuron apoptotic process (P = 6.5E-04) in c0 (Supplementary Table S36). Although MOBP’s role is not novel, the specific pathway coenrichment identified in our study highlights its dual function in both structural maintenance and cellular response. In c3, the subnetwork (307 genes in c3, 61 genes in c0) is associated with regulation of dendritic spine development (P = 2.7E-04), myelination (P = 9.9E-04), neuronal cell–cell adhesion (P = 1.1E-03), and protein localization to synapse (P = 2.1E-03) in c3 and associated with transition metal ion transport (P = 1.1E-04), substantia nigra development (P = 3.3E-04), and regulation of neuron apoptotic processes (P = 6.5E-04) in c0 (Supplementary Table S37).

We also identify a few key drivers in astrocytes, including WDR49 and heat shock protein family genes such as HSPA1B, HSPB1, HSP90AA1, and HSPH1. WDR49 is downregulated in PD and highly ranked as a key driver in cluster 2 astrocytes (KDA: FDR = 7.6E-12, 5.53 FE; expression: 0.9 FC, FDR P = 3.4E-05). Interestingly, WDR49 encodes a protein with unknown function, but a recent study suggests WDR49 as the disease-associated subpopulation in frontotemporal dementia carrying the GRN mutation and Alzheimer’s disease [65]. The subnetwork around WDR49 (100 genes) is involved in the phosphatidic acid process (FDR P = 9.0E-03) (Supplementary Fig. S8D, Supplementary Tables S36). Interestingly, a recent study shows that α-Synuclein (α-Syn), a protein causally linked to PD, binds to vesicles containing phosphatidic acid (PA), a plasma membrane phospholipid [66]. The identification of WDR49 as a key driver opens a new avenue for research into its potential role in PD pathogenesis.

We also identify key drivers in microglial clusters. One driver is APBB1IP, which is prominently associated with activation and modulation of the innate immune response. APBB1IP is a regulator of leukocyte recruitment and pathogen clearance through complement-mediated phagocytosis [67, 68]. In our analysis, APBB1IP is moderately downregulated (KDA: FDR = 8.5E-10, FE = 5.45; expression: FC = 0.85, FDR = 1.1E-09), and the subnetwork around APBB1IP is involved in synapse pruning (FDR P = 6.7E-04), microglial cell migration (FDR P = 3.2E-02), and macrophage migration (FDR P = 4.7E-02) (Fig. 6E, Supplementary Tables S36–S37). The observed association between APBB1IP and pathways related to microglial activation underscores its importance in disease-related glial responses. Interestingly, the direct effectors of APBB1IP, such as C3 and CX3CR1, are also downregulated and involved in immune responses and inflammation (Fig. 6E). Moreover, ST6GAL1, an upstream gene of APBB1IP, encodes a member of the glycosyltransferase family, ST6Gal-I sialyltransferase, which is known to regulate several immunological processes such as immunoglobulin production in B cells [69] or inflammatory pathways [70]. Therefore, our findings further underscore the importance of APBB1IP and its surrounding effectors in driving immune responses in microglia.

Last but not least, HSP90AA1 is identified as a key driver and is consistently upregulated across multiple glial cells, including OPCs (KDA: FDR = 3.6E-11, 10.4 FE; expression: 1.3 FC, FDR = 9.9E-13), oligodendrocytes in c3 (KDA: FDR = 1.2E-07, 3.69 FE; expression: 1.1 FC, FDR = 2.7E-08), oligodendrocytes in c0 (KDA: FDR = 1.7E-05, 3.52 FE; expression: 1.2 FC, FDR = 0.005), microglia (KDA: FDR = 3.2E-06, 4.02 FE; expression: 1.2 FC, FDR P = 9.7E-11), and astrocytes (KDA: FDR = 0.02, 4.3 FE; expression: 1.1 FC, FDR = 1.2E-068) (Fig. 6E, Supplementary Fig. S9F–I). In the OPC cluster c5, the subnetwork around HSP90AA1 is involved in responses to unfolded protein (FDR = 1.9E-10), protein ubiquitination (FDR = 7.8E-06), and apoptosis (FDR = 6.4E-05) (Fig. 6E, Supplementary Table S36). The subnetworks around HSP90AA1 in other glial cells are enriched for similar pathways (Supplementary Tables S37–S38). The consistent regulatory pattern of HSP90AA1 in glial cells suggests its potential involvement in maintaining glial homeostasis and contributing to the underlying neurodegenerative mechanisms.

Discussion

In this study, we develop an MCMNA framework for analyzing snRNA-seq data and apply it to PD snRNA-seq data to identify cell-type–specific gene modules and key drivers for PD. Use of metacells rather than single cells addresses the challenge of dealing with the intrinsic sparsity in snRNA-seq data. The application of MCMNA to PD systematically identifies gene modules associated with PD and putative key network drivers for PD in the major brain cell types. Notably, 30% of the predicted drivers in neurons have been studied in PD, including UCHL1, NSF, SNAP25, STMN2, and PREPL, while the rest (70%) are novel or understudied, suggesting that our method can capture previously unrecognized regulators with potential significance in the disease.

Disease-associated networks often exhibit sparse connectivity, reflecting the complexity and heterogeneity of underlying biological processes. Especially in snRNA-seq, some signals can be hidden by sparsity in gene expression profiles. One key advantage of the metacell approach in snRNA-seq is the ability to reduce data sparsity, thereby significantly enhancing downstream biological signals [27–29]. Aggregation of similar cells into metacells allows us to improve the detection of conserved markers specific to each cell type and reduce false-positive differentially expressed genes. Metacell-specific DEGs captured biologically relevant signals, consistent with some previous PD studies. In glial populations, the enrichment of metal ion homeostasis pathways was driven by metallothionein genes, whose dysregulation has also been reported in PD [71]. Additionally, we observed that several canonical dopaminergic neurons (SLC6A3, TH, SLC18A2) were downregulated in microglia and astrocytes in PD. Although unexpected, dopaminergic-associated transcriptional signals in glial cells have been reported in a previous dataset [22], suggesting that such signals are not unique to our dataset. Although dopaminergic-associated transcripts detected in glial populations may reflect biologically relevant expression, we cannot fully exclude a contribution from ambient RNA. Future studies incorporating an ambient RNA correction algorithm, such as SoupX [72] or CellBender [73], will help further distinguish cell-intrinsic transcriptional signals from potential background contamination. Similarly, heat shock and proteostasis-related genes were recurrently identified in independent datasets [21, 25], consistent with the involvement of cellular stress responses in PD. Together, these findings support the ability of metacell analysis to detect subtle but biologically coherent disease-associated changes.

Importantly, it strengthens gene–gene correlation analysis, which serves as a foundation for constructing more robust networks. We examine different metacell construction methods and parameter choices (e.g., graining level—the ratio between the number of cells and the number of metacells, and expression representation—average vs. summing up, unweighted vs. weighted) on our dataset to accelerate and enhance substantial downstream analyses. Additionally, the denser and more accurate correlation structure stemmed from biologically meaningful matrix (BMC) results in more meaningful gene coexpression networks. Additionally, the process of pruning enables more accurate construction of causal Bayesian networks.

Our metacell-based MEGENA results show that different modules capture distinct biological pathways and reveal cell-type–specific regulatory patterns. For example, the top-ranked modules in neurons are involved in synaptic transmission and nervous system development, while the top-ranked modules in glial cells are enriched with diverse functions such as developmental processes, metabolic signaling, and immune responses. We identify 3 distinct neuronal clusters, each displaying unique network architecture that reflects the cellular heterogeneity in PD. The typical neuronal cluster c7 with the tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) marker exhibits a more compact and highly connected network structure, while the neuronal cluster c9 with the novel RIT2 marker has a sparser structure. Cluster c7 has no hierarchical modules that are enriched with PD signatures, indicating that many dysregulated genes in this neuron are rather highly coordinated. Interestingly, in c9 neurons prone to be most degenerated in PD, only a small subset of cells demonstrates the most pronounced network changes (c9_M3), highlighting the selective vulnerability within this cluster. Previous studies using midbrain datasets and mouse models have implicated SOX6+/AGTR1+ dopaminergic neuronal populations as particularly vulnerable in PD [74–76]. In our human substantia nigra dataset, neither SOX6 nor AGTR1 was identified as a defining feature of c9 or within its dysregulated M3 module (Supplementary Table S23). Given that the substantia nigra is the primary site of dopaminergic neuronal degeneration in PD, our analysis provides a complementary, anatomic, and disease-relevant context for investigating selective neuronal vulnerability. The RIT2-enriched c9 signature therefore represents an additional axis of vulnerability in the human substantia nigra that is not fully captured by the previously described SOX6+/AGTR1+ signature. However, differences in species, anatomic sampling, and analytical resolution may also contribute to this apparent distinction, and further investigation is required to determine their relationship.

In addition to neurons, our metacell-based MEGENA allows us to uncover key features within glial cells, highlighting their distinct and potentially underappreciated roles in disease pathologies. Beyond neurons, oligodendrocyte clusters c0 and c3 have gene modules highly associated with PD, revealing broader functional roles than previously described. Several modules in c0 and c3 are enriched for mitochondrial autophagy, apoptosis, oligodendrocyte differentiation, and protein folding, revealing a more dynamic and multifaceted contribution to PD. Additionally, we observe an increased abundance of astrocytes (Supplementary Fig. S1D), yet their functional role in PD remains poorly understood. Our MEGENA pinpoints additional astrocyte involvement, highlighting modules enriched for signaling transduction and synapse assembly, suggesting a possible contribution to maintaining or altering the synaptic environment during disease progression. Microglia, in contrast, have fewer high-ranked modules, which are strongly associated with the immune response pathway. This agrees with well-established knowledge of their roles in neuroinflammation. Additionally, our study also indicates that several OPC modules associated with neural functions and glial differentiation are dysregulated, further highlighting the critical roles of OPCs in PD.

Our module preservation analyses show that module preservation varies substantially across cell types, with the highest score in oligodendrocytes, followed by astrocytes, microglia, and neurons (Supplementary Fig. S4A, B). This indicates that gene coexpression networks are more stable and conserved in glial populations, while neuronal networks are comparatively more dynamic and reflect greater heterogeneity. PD-related modules also have higher preservation overall, suggesting that disease-associated transcriptional programs are more robust and reproducible.

Our analysis identifies both known and novel gene targets that are strongly related to PD-related pathways, allowing us to propose a dynamic mechanistic model of neurodegeneration and susceptibility. Several key drivers in neuronal clusters, such as CADPS2, NSF, and PREPL, are associated with calcium signaling, synaptic transmission, and protein trafficking and have been studied in PD to some extent [42, 50, 51, 60, 77, 78], highlighting their central role in neuronal functions. We also observe distinct neuronal subpopulations with unique key driver profiles and transcriptomic alterations, suggesting heterogeneity in the degree of the regulatory pattern in degeneration and vulnerability. This observation aligns with previous studies reporting selective neuronal vulnerability in neurodegenerative diseases, including PD [31, 79]. Our findings suggest that multiple pathogenic processes may operate in parallel or sequentially. For example, in novel cluster 9, which shows degeneration in PD, CADPS2, enriched for calcium signaling, can point to a novel mechanism of calcium dysregulation, contributing to stress or glial–neuronal signaling imbalance. In contrast, cluster c7 is dominated by key drivers in synaptic transmission, exocytosis, and protein trafficking such as NSF or PREPL.

Oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system (CNS) are essential for forming myelin sheaths—multilayered lipid membranes that insulate axons [80, 81]. Among the top key drivers we identify in oligodendrocytes, MOBP stands out as the highest-ranked gene across both oligodendrocyte clusters c0 and c3. It is the third most abundant protein in CNS myelin and is known to contribute to myelin integrity. Moreover, a recent study suggests a possible association between MOBP and PD [82]. Our findings highlight MOBP as a potential therapeutic target, given its role in maintaining structural stability and mediating cellular responses. Additionally, key drivers identified in microglia cells point toward compelling hypotheses involving innate immune activation. Consistent with the important role of APBB1IP suggested by our network, APBB1IP has been identified as a modulator of immune gene coexpression in a specific microglial subtype in a recent aged mouse hippocampus study [83]. It is also recognized as a core component of microglial expression signatures [84, 85]. Furthermore, human GWAS has linked APBB1IP to both Alzheimer’s disease and schizophrenia [86, 87]. Our findings contribute to this growing body of evidence by highlighting the relevance of APBB1IP in PD.

Heat shock proteins (HSPs) are molecular chaperones that manage protein folding and prevent misfolding by regulating protein aggregation and disaggregation in both intracellular and extracellular environments, playing a critical role in maintaining proteostasis [88]. In our study, we observe consistent upregulation of HSPs across multiple glial cell types (Fig. 2B). Pathway analysis further highlights the activation of processes such as protein folding and Hsf1 activation (Fig. 2C). In particular, in PD, HSP60, HSP70, and HSP90 interact with α-synuclein, becoming incorporated into α-synuclein aggregates as they attempt to prevent or repair protein misfolding and accumulation [89–92]. Among these, we nominate HSP90AA1 as a key candidate, as it is significantly upregulated in 5 glial cell types and ranks highly in our network analysis. Notably, HSP90AA1 has been identified as a marker of microglial activation in recent PD omics studies [42, 93]. Altogether, these findings support emerging evidence that nonneuronal cells significantly contribute to disease progression through immune signaling, stress response, or other metabolic and cellular responses.

Our results also reveal strong concordance between bulk and snRNA-seq analyses, providing mutual validation of key molecular drivers implicated in PD. Several key drivers previously identified in bulk transcriptomic studies, such as STMN2 and RALYL, are also drivers in our snRNA-seq network. This overlap underscores the robustness of our network-based approach and demonstrates that strong, biologically relevant signals identified in bulk are not only preserved but also refined at the single-cell level. The replication of these findings confirms the reliability of bulk-based insights while offering higher-resolution interpretation of cell-type–specific dynamics. By disentangling the contributions of distinct cell populations, we uncover cell-specific regulatory responses, enabling increased specificity in identifying therapeutic targets.

We apply our pipeline to a previously published snRNA-seq dataset to identify key drivers [25]. In this dataset, we identify 121 candidate key driver genes (Supplementary Table S38); however, there is minimal overlap with our results. Only a small number of genes are shared between the 2 analyses, including HSP90AA1 in glial cells and CADPS2, CPLX1, andSERINC1 in neurons (Supplementary Table S39). The limited concordance likely reflects the smaller sample size of the external dataset, which reduces the robustness of network construction and, consequently, the reliability of key driver identification.

We also highlight the functional contexts of PD GWAS genes using cell-type–specific Bayesian causal networks. Our causal networks in 5 major cell types across 8 cell clusters harbor a total of 509 PD GWAS genes, but only 2 (NSF in c7 and MDGA2 in c6) are identified as key drivers (Supplementary Table S39). Additionally, we predict UCHL1, a known PD causal gene, as a putative driver. Moreover, our network reveals a substantial number of novel key driver genes. These understudied genes may represent critical yet unrecognized components involved in disease progression, particularly within specific cell types, enabling a broader and more comprehensive understanding of PD mechanisms.

We also compare our DEG signatures with previously published bulk proteomics datasets of human SN [94]. We retrieve the identified differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) from 2 published cohorts (Supplementary Tables S41–S43) and assess the overlap with cell-type–specific DEGs using FET. The results show that the shared DEPs are predominantly enriched in neuronal clusters, with consistent directionality (Supplementary Fig. S10A, B, Supplementary Tables S44–S45). While bulk proteomics capture an overall signal of the molecular dysregulation in PD, these findings indicate that the greatest and most robust changes are driven by the neuronal population. This underscores the importance of cell-type–resolved analyses for accurately interpreting disease-associated signatures.

In summary, our study identifies a set of molecular subnetworks and putative key driver genes that illuminate the molecular architecture of PD at single-cell resolution. By applying MCMNA to address sparsity in snRNA-seq, we uncover well-known genes and novel regulatory candidates that may have important roles in PD. These findings offer a better understanding of the cellular heterogeneity and regulatory dynamics underlying neuronal and glial cell populations.

Limitations of the study

Although this cohort is the largest dataset of SN to date, the sample size is still relatively small. Furthermore, the current study lacks experimental validation to confirm computational predictions. Even though we observe that previous perturbation signatures overlapped with our predicted STMN2 network in c7 neurons, more validation is needed for the novel targets. Future work will focus on expanding the analysis to larger and more diverse datasets and implementing different confounding factors, such as sex and Braak stages in the study, to get more insights into the dynamic regulatory machinery. Functional perturbation of prioritized regulators in relevant in vitro and in vivo models to determine whether these predicted drivers directly contribute to the disease pathogenesis and represent tractable therapeutic targets. Our approach can also be improved with comprehensive longitudinal studies, extended studies beyond sporadic cases and specific brain regions, and incorporation of other omics-based networks such as proteomics and epigenomics. More importantly, this integrative approach establishes a foundation to guide future efforts toward generating increasingly high-quality and informative coexpression and causal inference, paving the way for deeper mechanistic insights and the discovery of novel therapeutic targets.

Methods

Postmortem substantia nigra pars compacta samples used

The framework we develop in this article is created using a discovery cohort from our previous study of human 23 PD cases and 9 controls that contain 30,038 genes across 315,867 nuclei [20]. To validate some of our findings, we used 3 publicly available snRNA-seq datasets of human substantia nigra: the Lee et al. dataset [21] of 6 PD and 6 control cases, the Martirosyan et al. dataset [22] of 15 PD and 14 control cases, and the Ma et al. [25] dataset of 11 PD and 13 control cases.

SnRNA-seq data preprocessing and clustering analysis

Three publicly available snRNA-seq datasets were downloaded and subjected to the same pipeline we already established in the previous study [20]. Briefly, from Cell Ranger–derived unique molecular identifier (UMI) count matrices from all sequencing libraries, we performed quality control (QC) by removing low-quality nuclei with too few genes (<200) or an excessive number (>2,500) of genes detected. Then we removed insufficiently detected genes and discarded mitochondrial genes. We then performed a preclustering analysis using an scRNA-seq data integration workflow based on R packages Harmony [95] (v.1.2.3) and Seurat (v.4.3.0.1) [96, 97]. A total of 2,000 most variable gene features were identified, scaled, and centered after regressing out covariates sex, age, and post-mortem interval (PMI). Next, dimensional reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA) based on the 2,000 most variable genes. Preclustering analysis, cluster stability analysis, and double prediction analysis were performed as previously described [20]. As a result, we obtained 28,339 genes across 44,450 high-quality nuclei in the KAIST dataset, 33,537 genes across 69,147 high-quality nuclei in the VIB dataset, and 31,280 genes across 194,905 high-quality nuclei in the Columbia dataset.

Cluster cell-type annotation

For each major cluster of the 3 external datasets, we interrogated the expression patterns of known gene markers to annotate clusters into major cell types: neurons (SLC17A6, RBFOX3, GAD1), astrocytes (AQP4), oligodendrocytes (MOG), microglia (C3, CSF1R), oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (VCAN), endothelial cells (FLT1), pericytes (PDGFRB), and T cells (SKAP1). Additionally, we used sets of marker genes we identified in our study, such as typical dopaminergic markers in c7 (SLC6A3, TH, SLC18A2), enriched markers in c6 (DCC and GALNTL6), and novel enriched markers in c9 (RIT2).

Construction of metacells

Metacells were built based on a log-normalized gene expression matrix using the R package SuperCell (v.1.0.1) [29]. The set of features (genes) used for the construction of metacells was defined by the set of the 2,000 most variable genes. The top principal components (top 10 by default) are used to build a single-cell network with a k-nearest neighbors algorithm built in the R package (k-NN, k = 5 by default). To preserve the cell-type annotation and phenotype information from snRNA-seq data, metacells were constructed by merging single cells (nodes) at a graining level γ = 15 in a cell cluster– and condition-specific manner. A gene expression profile of metacells was computed by averaging gene expression within metacells, or summing up the single-cell raw values, followed by log normalization.

Identification of differentially expressed genes

DEGs between PD and control and DEGs between PD and control in each cluster were identified using the R package MAST (v.1.16.0) implemented in Seurat [96, 97], with correction for PMI, age, and sex. DEGs were identified at the cutoff of a Bonferroni-corrected P value ≤0.05 and fold change ≥10%.

Identification of conserved markers

A conserved marker is defined as a cell-type–specific gene that distinguishes a given cell type from others and shows consistent expression across 2 groups (control and PD). Conserved markers in each cluster were identified using the FindConservedMarkers function in the R Seurat package [96, 97]. Genes that reached the average expression levels across the cluster with logFC >0 were identified as conserved markers.

Overlap and functional enrichment analysis

To understand biological processes underlying the DEGs and coexpressed modules, we tested these signatures for enrichment of Gene Ontology (GO) and canonical functional pathway gene sets. We used the functional gene sets collection from the Molecular Signature Database MSigDb that was downloaded from the R package called msigdb (v.25.1.1). Functional enrichment of DEGs with MSigDB gene annotation collections was examined using the R package clusterProfiler (v.4.14.6) [98]. To test the pathways of the 2-layer network neighborhood of candidate genes, we loaded the gene members onto the EnrichR server and identified the top pathways associated with these genes [99–101]. Functional enrichment analysis between 2 gene sets was determined by FET. FE was calculated as the ratio between observed overlap and expected overlap size. We employed the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) approach to correct for FDR.

Generation of the BMC matrix

We implemented a novel framework to generate a BMC matrix. First, we filtered out unexpressed genes using a range of minimum cell cutoffs (i.e., requiring that each gene be expressed in at least x% of cells), selected genes that were conserved markers, and removed noisy cells (those expressing fewer than 500 genes). For each gene pair, we excluded zero counts before calculating all possible signed SCCs (FDR = 1) using the calculate.rho.signed function in MEGENA (v.1.6.0) [32]. To capture all signed correlations, we set direction as “positive” or “negative,” then combined results from both directions.

Next, we evaluated whether the distribution of the correlation coefficient (ρ) in each cell cluster followed a truncated normal distribution. For each cell cluster at each minimum cell cutoff, we first calculated the mean and standard deviation of the observed ρ values and used them to define a truncated normal distribution bounded by the minimum and maximum observed ρ. The truncated normal density was generated at 100 equally spaced points across this range using the truncnorm function in the R package (v 1.0–9). We applied linear interpolation using the approx function in R to estimate the histogram densities at 100 equally spaced points across the observed range of correlations. We then plotted MSE values against the minimum cell cutoff to identify the optimal cutoff. The mean (µ) of all gene pair correlations was calculated. Finally, at the optimal minimum cell cutoff, all significant gene pair correlations (FDR < 0.05) were identified. However, even after applying FDR < 0.05, a substantial fraction of gene pairs fell within the (0, µ) interval across clusters ranging from 0% in c9 to 47% in C5 (Supplementary Table S24). Therefore, at the optimal mincell cutoff, we further subtracted gene pairs in the (0, µ) interval. This new set of significant correlations, named the BMC matrix, potentially removed weak and suspicious associations prior to network construction.

MEGENA coexpression network analysis

For each cell cluster, the optimized BMC matrix was then subjected to the MEGENA pipeline [32] (v.1.6.0). Briefly, significant SCCs were ranked and iteratively tested for planarity to grow a planar filtered network (PFN) by using the PMFG algorithm. Multiscale clustering analysis (MCA) was conducted with the resulting PFN to identify coexpression modules at different network-scale topologies. We identified a total of 1,057 modules in all cell clusters (Table 3). To annotate the biological functions associated with the modules, we performed MSigDB gene set enrichment analysis using FET as described above. Most of these modules (42.2–73.3%) were enriched for MSigDB GO Pathway gene sets (adjusted P < 0.05; Table 3), indicating that MEGENA can capture biologically functional signals. For simplicity, modules were annotated by the top enriched pathway and then grouped into a functional category. The MEGENA modules are formed in a hierarchy with parent–child relationships and illustrated by a sunburst plot using the R package ggsunburst.

To prioritize the gene modules with respect to the PD phenotypes, we applied a ranking metric across multiple features such as enrichment with (i) the DEG signatures identified and (ii) PD GWAS signatures. PD GWAS hits were downloaded from the GWAS catalog and filtered to remove redundance. The ranking of the top modules was based on the average of −log10 (Padj value) of all features using this equation.

The circular track plot to visualize the gene coexpression network modules and their enrichment was generated using the R package NetWeaver (v.0.0.6) [102]. The global network of the c9 neuron was visualized using Gephi (version 0.10.1). The global network of other cell clusters and the subnetwork of selected gene modules were visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.10.3).

MEGENA coexpression module preservation analysis

We assessed module preservation in the MEGENA coexpression network between our MSSM snRNA-seq data and Columbia’s snRNA-seq data using the FET, with genes from the corresponding global network in our dataset as the background. We performed pairwise comparisons of modules within the same cell type (e.g., microglia modules vs. microglia modules).

Additionally, we used module preservation analysis in WGCNA (v.1.73) [37]. For each cluster, MEGENA modules identified in the MSSM snRNA-seq dataset were used as reference modules, and module preservation was evaluated in the Columbia dataset. Expression matrices were restricted to genes available in both datasets. Because MEGENA allows a single gene to belong to multiple modules, each gene–module membership was treated as a separate feature. Prior to the preservation analysis, the expression datasets were evaluated using the goodSamplesGenes function, and the genes or the samples with zero variance or excessive missing values were removed. Module preservation was then evaluated using the modulePreservation function, which took the MSSM gene coexpression networks as the reference networks and the Columbia dataset as the testing dataset. The composite module preservation statistics, Zsummary.pres, was calculated for each module. Modules with Zsummary.pres ≥ 10 were classified as highly preserved, modules with 2 < Zsummary.pres < 10 were considered moderately preserved, and modules with Zsummary.pres ≤ 2 were considered not preserved.

Bayesian probabilistic causal network inference

We constructed the global BN based on Monte Carlo Markov chain simulation-based procedures using RIMBANet software [33, 34]. We used established TF–target relationships for Homo sapiens acquired from the TFLink database [103] to infer regulatory relationships among genes. In the causal network construction, the TFs are allowed to be parent nodes of their target genes, but targets are inhibited from being parent nodes of their TFs.

First, we began by removing genes expressed in fewer than 20% of metacells. To resolve the issue of dropouts in snRNA-seq, we applied an iterative pruning procedure to remove genes and nuclei in a data matrix. For this, we defined 2 thresholds: Tg, representing the maximum allowable gene missing rate, and Tm, representing the maximum allowable metacell missing rate. For each iteration, genes exceeding the Tg threshold and metacells exceeding the Tm threshold were removed. This pruning process was repeated iteratively until the number of remaining genes and metacells stabilized, with no changes observed between iterations. After 2 iterations, the numbers converged. We then selected the cutoff Tg = 0.4 and Tm = 0.4 for all cell clusters.

We employed a network averaging approach where 1,000 networks were generated starting with a different random structure. Links that appear in more than 30% of the networks were used as a final consensus network. An iterative de-loop procedure was executed to ensure that the consensus network was a directed acyclic graph, removing the most weakly supported link of all links involved in any loop. The global network of cell clusters was visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.10.3).

KDA

For each major cell type cluster, we performed KDA [35] on the consensus Bayesian network to identify key hub genes that are DEGs and regulate a large number of downstream nodes. Here we loaded all the BN nodes and DEGs from the corresponding cluster as input for KDA, making the resulting key drivers representative of the global network. The algorithm defined a background subnetwork by identifying nodes within an L-layer neighborhood of each DEG in the network. For each node in this subnetwork, it assessed enrichment within its L-layer downstream neighborhood for DEGs. In our analysis, we tested a combination of 1-layer (1 L), 2-layer (2 L), and 3-layer (3 L) neighborhoods, considering both directed and undirected edges to account for potential missing directionality in the causal BN. Then key drivers were compiled based on those identified from the 3 L directed analysis and those shared by both 2 L and 1 L undirected analyses. To ensure that key drivers had sufficient downstream targets, we also excluded drivers with a degree lower than the average degree of all nodes in the network. KDA was performed by the R package KDA (version 0.02).

PubMed literature mining

The function EUtilsSummary (type = “esearch,” db=“pubmed,” datetype=“pdat”) from the R package RISmed (version 2.3.0) was used to query and download content from PubMed. The query words were built as “KDG and Parkinson’s disease,” where the word KDG was replaced by the gene symbol of a specific key driver gene. For example, the query of “CADPS2 and Parkinson’s disease” would pull out all the papers that have the occurrence of CADPS2 and Parkinson’s disease.

Statistical analysis

FET was used to examine the significance of the overlap between 2 gene sets. An overlap with BH-corrected P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless stated otherwise.
