ABSTRACT

Freezing of gait (FOG) is one of the most debilitating motor symptoms of Parkinson's disease (PD). Prior studies have suggested a potential right‐hemispheric preference in the pathophysiology of FOG. Yet, there is limited evidence regarding which brain regions are most consistently associated with FOG and whether these alterations indeed show right‐hemispheric lateralization. We aimed to identify brain regions associated with FOG based on multimodal neuroimaging findings and to examine their hemispheric lateralization. A systematic review across multiple neuroimaging modalities was performed, followed by an activation likelihood estimation (ALE) coordinate‐based meta‐analysis to assess convergent brain alterations in PD patients with FOG (freezers) versus those without FOG (nonfreezers). Hemispheric lateralization was assessed using the distribution of reported stereotactic coordinates. The systematic review included 108 articles, of which 33 were included in the primary ALE meta‐analysis. We identified a significant convergent cluster in the right inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) pars opercularis/dorsolateral prefrontal cortex in PD freezers compared with nonfreezers. No evidence of overall hemispheric lateralization was observed based on the distribution of reported coordinates. Our findings identify the right IFG as a consistent neural correlate of FOG, potentially reflecting heightened frontal recruitment, with no clear evidence of hemispheric lateralization. The lack of further consistency in brain regions could reflect the known heterogeneity in the pathophysiology of FOG.

Our systematic review included 108 neuroimaging articles examining freezing of gait in Parkinson's disease, with 33 included in the primary activation likelihood estimation meta‐analysis. We assessed spatial convergence and hemispheric lateralization of reported abnormalities, identifying the right inferior frontal gyrus as the main convergent region, with no evidence of overall hemispheric lateralization.

1 Introduction

Freezing of gait (FOG) is one of the most disabling motor symptoms in people with Parkinson's disease (PD) (Giladi et al. 1997), yet its underlying pathophysiology remains incompletely understood. Beyond the fundamental PD pathology—characterized by a reduction in striatal dopamine levels due to the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta—several reviews summarize the regional and circuit alterations in PD patients with FOG (i.e., freezers) compared to those without FOG (i.e., nonfreezers). Using different imaging modalities (e.g., magnetic resonance imaging [MRI], positron emission tomography [PET], and single‐photon emission computed tomography [SPECT]), these studies highlighted widespread functional and structural abnormalities across both cortical and subcortical regions (Snijders et al. 2016; Bardakan et al. 2022; Weiss et al. 2020). A review by Bardakan et al. highlighted that resting‐state fMRI studies mainly reveal cortico‐striatal‐brainstem abnormalities (e.g., supplementary motor area [SMA], striatum, and mesencephalic locomotor region [MLR]) and impaired dorsal visuomotor processing, while task‐based fMRI studies demonstrate a disruption in cortico‐subcortical connectivity and an increased recruitment of parieto‐occipital regions (Bardakan et al. 2022). A previous coordinate‐based meta‐analysis (i.e., activation likelihood estimation [ALE] analysis) by Gilat et al. revealed that the cerebellar locomotor region (CLR) showed the most consistent gait‐related activation in PD versus age‐matched healthy adults. While that prior review provides an understanding of the functional neural signatures of Parkinsonian gait during gait‐related neuroimaging tasks (Gilat et al. 2019), it neither explicitly focused on freezers versus nonfreezers nor addressed structural alterations that have also been identified in PD freezers (D'Cruz et al. 2021; Fling et al. 2014; Fling et al. 2013). Consequently, there is still no convincing evidence regarding exactly “where” the most consistent neural alterations occur in freezers compared with nonfreezers. Specifically, to date, whether left‐ or right‐hemispheric alterations are more dominant (i.e., hemispheric lateralization) in FOG remains controversial. Clarifying this issue may provide additional insight into whether FOG‐related neural alterations preferentially involve one hemisphere or are more broadly bilateral. This could help inform future therapeutic strategies, such as neuromodulation techniques. Some neuroimaging studies have suggested a possible right‐hemispheric vulnerability in FOG (Fling et al. 2013; Craig et al. 2026). For example, Fling et al. reported reduced structural connectivity of the pedunculopontine nucleus with the cerebellum, thalamus, and frontal/prefrontal cortices specifically in the right hemisphere of freezers (Fling et al. 2013). Craig et al. extended these findings by showing right‐asymmetric atrophy of the nucleus basalis of Meynert (Craig et al. 2026). However, this right‐hemispheric hypothesis has since been questioned (Hall et al. 2015). Conversely, Rubino et al. localized parietal gray‐matter atrophy in the left posterior parietal cortex in freezers (Rubino et al. 2014), while our previous study showed effective connectivity from the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex to the bilateral MLR in freezers (Taniguchi et al. 2024). These inconsistencies highlight the need for a systematic evaluation of the brain regions most consistently associated with FOG, as well as their hemispheric lateralization. Here, we therefore systematically reviewed and performed an ALE meta‐analysis to reveal which brain regions have been most consistently associated with FOG in the literature. Specifically, the current study's primary aims were to (1) systematically summarize FOG‐specific findings of available neuroimaging experiments using various modalities (e.g., resting‐state/task‐based fMRI, structural MRI, PET/SPECT studies) and subsequently (2) identify spatial convergence across the results of the available neuroimaging studies on PD patients with and without FOG. The secondary objective was to evaluate whether FOG‐related neuroimaging findings show right‐hemispheric predominance by examining the left–right distribution of reported stereotactic coordinates.

2 Materials and Methods

2.1 Preregistration

This systematic review was preregistered at the international prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO registration number: CRD420251172569).

2.2 Literature Searches

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. A systematic search was conducted in the electronic databases of PubMed and Web of Science Core Collection to identify relevant studies published up to August 28, 2025, using the terms “Parkinson disease”, “freezing of gait”, as well as the imaging keywords of “MRI”, “PET”, and “SPECT” (the full search strategies are provided in Table S1). We also used the AI‐assisted systematic review tool “Elicit” (Whitfield and Hofmann 2023) to retrieve publications using the research question “Which brain regions are associated with FOG in PD?”. The first step was to remove duplicates using Elicit. Next, titles, abstracts, and full texts were independently screened based on a priori defined eligibility criteria by a human reviewer (H.D.) and Elicit (default score threshold 2.5). Any disagreements were resolved by a third human reviewer (S.T.).

2.3 Eligibility Criteria for the Screening Process

The inclusion criteria for screening were English‐language, peer‐reviewed studies that included PD freezers and used MRI, PET, or SPECT neuroimaging. For the ALE meta‐analysis, studies were eligible if they reported peak coordinates in a standard stereotactic space (Talairach or Montreal Neurological Institute [MNI]), where the x‐coordinate indexed the left–right axis and the initial voxelwise search space was whole‐brain. This included subsequent group comparisons conducted within data‐driven masks derived from whole‐brain analyses. Eligible contrasts compared freezers with nonfreezers or healthy older adults. Coordinates were classified according to the direction of the reported between‐group difference and entered separately into the freezers > nonfreezers and nonfreezers > freezers ALE analyses. When multiple time points were available (e.g., longitudinal or intervention studies), only baseline outcomes were extracted. We excluded studies that did not report stereotactic coordinates, studies comparing PD to healthy older adults without specifying FOG, or studies solely reporting prespecified regions of interest (ROIs) or those that were limited to unilateral coverage. In cases where a between‐group comparison was not performed, studies reporting correlations between neuroimaging measures and FOG severity within PD freezers were retained for the descriptive summary but were not included in the ALE analyses. Based on simulation‐based recommendations for cluster‐level family‐wise error (FWE) inference, direction‐specific ALE analyses including at least 17 independent experiments were considered to meet the recommended minimum for limiting excessive influence from individual experiments, although approximately 20 experiments are desirable for adequate statistical power (Eickhoff et al. 2016). Therefore, when fewer than 17 independent experiments were available for a direction‐specific comparison, coordinates from the corresponding direct between‐group contrasts were pooled irrespective of direction in an exploratory direction‐independent ALE analysis. If significant convergence was identified in this exploratory analysis, additional direction‐specific ALE analyses were performed to characterize the direction of the observed effect. The primary ALE analyses comprised the direction‐specific freezers–nonfreezers comparisons (freezers > nonfreezers, and nonfreezers > freezers), whereas comparisons involving healthy controls were considered exploratory secondary analyses.

Based on these criteria, review articles, nonhuman studies, and non‐English publications were excluded at the title screening stage. At the abstract screening stage, nonneuroimaging studies (e.g., only behavioral/clinical data), conference abstracts, and reviews of the literature with or without meta‐analysis, nonhuman studies, non‐English, and non–peer‐reviewed articles were excluded. Studies that did not include people with PD or that explicitly did not include PD freezers, were also excluded. At the full‐text screening stage, studies were excluded for insufficient neuroimaging details, no FOG assessment, no PD freezer specifications after full‐text confirmation, no reported hemispheric right/left lateralization or stereotactic labels of brain regions/networks, or a focus on symptoms other than FOG, such as tremor, bradykinesia, and freezing of the upper limbs.

2.4 Data Extraction

Data were independently extracted from included studies by Elicit and a human rater (H.D.), with any discrepancies resolved by a third human reviewer (S.T.). For the descriptive summary, extracted data included study characteristics (authors, year), imaging techniques and modalities, participant demographics (sample size, age, sex), clinical characteristics (FOG severity, medication status during scan), and key brain regions in freezers. For the purpose of the ALE meta‐analysis, we extracted stereotactic coordinates (x, y, z) representing differences between freezers and reference groups (nonfreezers or healthy older adults). If studies reported results both with and without adjustment for confounding variables (e.g., disease duration), the adjusted coordinates were extracted. Exact duplicate foci for each experiment were removed before ALE analysis to avoid redundant weighting of nonindependent coordinates. Potentially overlapping participant samples across publications were assessed, and publications or contrasts clearly derived from the same cohort were combined and treated as a single experiment.

2.5 ALE Meta‐Analysis

We conducted a standard coordinate‐based meta‐analysis using the GingerALE software (version 3.0.2; www.brainmap.org/ale) to determine the above‐chance convergence of neuroimaging differences across three group comparisons: freezers versus nonfreezers, freezers versus healthy older adults, and nonfreezers versus healthy older adults. Although the primary focus was the comparison between PD freezers and nonfreezers, nonfreezers versus healthy older adults contrasts were included as exploratory secondary analyses when they were reported within the same eligible studies, to help the interpretation of the comparisons between PD freezers and nonfreezers by providing a reference for PD‐related neuroimaging differences independent of FOG. The ALE algorithm modelled coordinates as 3D Gaussian probability distributions to account for spatial uncertainty, replacing point foci with probability distributions to compute ALE scores. To reduce bias, input sample sizes were set to the lower value between the two compared groups in each contrast. Thresholding was applied following standard recommendations, utilizing a cluster‐forming threshold of uncorrected p < 0.001 with 5000 permutations, a cluster‐extent threshold of FWE‐corrected p < 0.05, and a minimum cluster extent of 200 mm3. This cluster‐forming and cluster‐level FWE thresholding approach is consistent with that used in previous ALE meta‐analyses (Mauchand and Zhang 2023; Heard and Lee 2020; Stefaniak et al. 2021). Studies that reported Talairach coordinates were converted to MNI space using GingerALE's conversion tool.

2.6 Hemispheric Lateralization

Hemispheric lateralization was evaluated based on the extracted MNI x‐coordinates (left: x < 0; right: x > 0; midline: x = 0) in the two direction‐specific freezer–nonfreezer datasets, with an alpha of 0.05. Hemispheric lateralization was primarily evaluated using a mixed‐effects logistic regression, with hemisphere (right vs. left) as the binary outcome and study/cohort unit included as a random intercept to account for within‐unit clustering of multiple foci. Midline coordinates (x = 0) were excluded from the binary analysis and reported descriptively. As a study‐level sensitivity analysis, a laterality index was calculated for each study/cohort unit as (right − left)/(right + left), and its deviation from zero was assessed using a two‐sided Wilcoxon signed‐rank test.

2.7 Risk of Bias Assessment

We developed a neuroimaging‐specific quality assessment checklist, informed by the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist (Barker et al. 2026), to evaluate reporting quality and key methodological safeguards in the included studies (Table S2). Ratings were performed by one human reviewer (H.D.) and Elicit, and any disagreements were resolved by a third human reviewer (S.T.).

The freezers > nonfreezers contrast included 18 independent experiments with 75 foci, while the nonfreezers > freezers contrast included 26 independent experiments with 118 foci. 3.2 Quality Assessment

Table S2 summarizes the quality assessment of the 33 studies included in the ALE meta‐analysis. Freezer/nonfreezer classification and neuroimaging methods were clearly described in all studies, and FOG severity was appropriately described in 32 studies (97.0%). PD diagnostic criteria and disease severity were reported in 29 studies (87.9%), key confounders were addressed in 30 studies (90.9%), and preprocessing procedures were described in sufficient detail in 30 studies (90.9%). In contrast, head‐motion reporting was less consistent, with 21 studies (63.6%) quantitatively reporting head motion and 21 studies (63.6%) describing motion‐correcting strategies. Medication status during scanning was clearly described in 30 studies (90.9%). Among the 22 studies that examined correlations between neuroimaging findings and FOG severity in PD freezers, 16 used FOG severity measures independent of the freezer/nonfreezer classification metric, whereas 6 did not. The remaining 11 of 33 studies did not examine such clinical correlations.

3.4.1 Primary ALE Analyses Between Freezers Versus Nonfreezers

3.4.1.1 Freezers > Nonfreezers

The freezers > nonreezers analysis included 75 extracted foci from 18 independent experiments comprising 846 participants and was performed using cluster‐level FWE correction at p < 0.05. The cluster‐level FWE procedure yielded a cluster‐extent threshold of 584 mm3, and one cluster survived this threshold in the right inferior frontal gyrus, pars opercularis (IFG; predominantly within Brodmann area 9 by functional label). The peak MNI coordinates were x = 57, y = 10, z = 27 (cluster size = 736 mm3; ALE value = 0.0171, Z = 4.62; Figure 2, panel 1). Two of the 18 independent experiments contributed foci to this cluster (Li et al. 2020; Li et al. 2025). Both were resting‐state fMRI studies and reported greater right IFG involvement in freezers than in nonfreezers. The medication states differed between the two studies: Li et al. (2020) assessed patients in the ON‐medication state (Li et al. 2020), whereas Li et al. (2025) assessed patients in the OFF‐medication state.

3.4.1.2 Nonfreezers > Freezers

The nonfreezers > freezers analysis included 118 extracted foci from 26 independent experiments comprising 1593 participants and was performed using cluster‐level FWE correction at p < 0.05; no significant clusters were identified.

3.4.2 Exploratory ALE Analyses Between Nonfreezers and Healthy Older Adults

This exploratory analysis included 10 studies (504 subjects, 70 foci) and revealed two significant clusters in the bilateral lentiform nuclei, mainly putamen (cluster‐level FWE‐corrected p < 0.05, 5000 permutations). The peak MNI coordinates were located in the right lentiform nucleus (x = 20, y = 8, z = 0; ALE value = 0.0158, Z = 4.39) and the left lentiform nucleus (x = −22, y = 8, z = 0; ALE value = 0.0245, Z = 5.77). Four studies primarily contributed to these clusters (Li et al. 2025; Mi et al. 2017; Hu et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2022). All were resting‐state fMRI studies conducted in the OFF‐medication state and consistently reported reduced involvement of these regions in PD nonfreezers compared with healthy older adults.

In the direction‐specific follow‐up analysis, the bilateral lentiform nucleus clusters identified in the direction‐independent analysis were also observed in the healthy older adults > nonfreezers contrast, indicating greater involvement in healthy older adults than in nonfreezers. This analysis included 9 independent experiments (448 participants, 43 foci) and revealed two significant clusters. The right cluster (1408 mm3) peaked at MNI coordinates x = 20, y = 8, z = 0 (ALE = 0.0158, Z = 4.61) and predominantly involved the lentiform nucleus, particularly the putamen, with additional caudate involvement. The left cluster (816 mm3) peaked at x = −22, y = 8, z = 0 (ALE = 0.0243, Z = 6.02) and was localized to the left putamen. These findings are shown in Figure 2, panel 2. Four studies primarily contributed to these clusters (Li et al. 2025; Mi et al. 2017; Hu et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2022). All were resting‐state fMRI studies conducted in the OFF‐medication state and consistently reported reduced involvement of these regions in PD nonfreezers compared with healthy older adults.

3.4.3 Exploratory ALE Analyses Between Freezers and Healthy Older Adults

This exploratory analysis included 11 studies (495 subjects, 110 foci) and was performed using cluster‐level FWE correction at p < 0.05. No significant clusters were identified.

4 Discussion

This systematic review and ALE meta‐analysis synthesized coordinate‐based neuroimaging evidence to identify convergent brain regions related to FOG in PD and examine possible hemispheric lateralization underlying FOG.

In relation to our primary objective of identifying brain regions most consistently associated with FOG, the descriptive synthesis of the included studies showed that FOG‐related alterations were reported across a widely distributed set of cortical, subcortical, cerebellar, and brainstem regions. These findings involved frontal control regions, parietal and occipital visuospatial–attentional areas, basal ganglia–thalamic circuits, cerebellar regions, and brainstem locomotor‐related structures, reflecting substantial heterogeneity across study settings, imaging modalities, analytical approaches, and task conditions. This distributed pattern is consistent with the current network‐based conception of FOG (Weiss et al. 2020).

The ALE analysis further revealed the right IFG as the region most consistently associated with FOG in PD. Both contributing studies reported greater right IFG involvement in freezers than in nonfreezers at rest (Li et al. 2020; Li et al. 2025). Hypothetically, greater right prefrontal activity in freezers may reflect an increased reliance on top–down cognitive control as gait becomes less automatic (Vitorio et al. 2020; Pu et al. 2023; Vandenbossche et al. 2013); whether this recruitment is compensatory, maladaptive, or an unsuccessful compensation remains unresolved. However, this interpretation is consistent with evidence at the broader prefrontal level rather than specifically localizing it to the right IFG. Despite the localized convergence in the right IFG, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence of a focal abnormality in freezers. Rather, the right IFG might also act as a frontal control hub within a broader cortico‐subcortical network, through which cognitive control, visuospatial attentional, and locomotor processes interact. This interpretation is supported by the current network‐based view of FOG, in which freezing arises from integration failure across distributed cortical and subcortical nodes rather than from dysfunction of a single isolated region (Weiss et al. 2020).

In our additional nonfreezers versus healthy older adults comparison, bilateral lentiform nucleus alterations were observed, consistent with core basal ganglia dysfunction related to PD motor symptoms (Bhatia and Marsden 1994). These findings suggest that lentiform nucleus alterations may reflect broader PD motor‐related pathology, rather than a FOG‐specific differentiating feature. Together with the right IFG findings that were identified in the freezer–nonfreezer comparison, the absence of striatal differences emphasizes that basal ganglia circuit changes alone do not fully explain FOG, as previously suggested (Bohnen et al. 2014). Unlike the nonfreezer–healthy older adult comparison, the freezer–healthy older adult comparison revealed no significant convergence. This lack of convergence is consistent with the spatially heterogeneous pattern reported across individual studies directly comparing freezers with healthy older adults, which have implicated distributed cortical, subcortical, cerebellar, and brainstem regions rather than a common focal region (Kostić et al. 2012; Droby et al. 2021; Bharti et al. 2020). Further studies are therefore required to clarify the neural alterations distinguishing freezers from healthy older adults.

As summarized in Table 2, we found no evidence of hemispheric lateralization in the distribution of extracted foci in either direction‐specific dataset. This finding does not provide evidence for overall hemispheric lateralization, consistent with previous reports questioning a strongly unilateral account of FOG (Bardakan et al. 2022; Hall et al. 2015; Fasano et al. 2015). Nevertheless, when accounting for sample size and spatial uncertainty using the rigorous ALE meta‐analysis, a significant convergent cluster emerged in the right IFG (pars opercularis). Although the right IFG was the most consistently identified region, our broader descriptive evaluation and synthesis did not reveal clear hemispheric lateralization. Therefore, the findings of this review do not support a strongly predominant right‐sided hemispheric lateralization underlying FOG in PD.

4.1 Strengths and Limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first ALE meta‐analysis to identify consistent brain regions differentiating freezers from nonfreezers in people with PD. We could include a reasonably large number of studies in the ALE analysis. Nevertheless, the included studies were heterogeneous in terms of imaging modality, task paradigm, medication state, FOG severity, sample size, and analytical covariates. Therefore, further research is needed to clarify the neurophysiological interpretation and modality‐specific nature of the convergent right IFG finding. In particular, medication state varied across the studies contributing to the main ALE cluster, which may have influenced both neural activity and FOG severity and thereby contributed to between‐study heterogeneity. Future studies should therefore consider medication state more systematically, including separate analyses of ON‐ and OFF‐medication conditions.

5 Conclusion

This systematic review and ALE meta‐analysis identified a significant convergence of regional alterations in the right IFG in freezers compared with nonfreezers. Although we found no evidence for overall hemispheric lateralization, the right IFG may represent a key frontal control hub within broader networks implicated in FOG.
