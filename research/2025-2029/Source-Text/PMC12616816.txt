ABSTRACT

Human citrate synthase (hCS) is a mitochondrial enzyme that catalyzes the aldol condensation of acetyl coenzyme A (AcCoA) to oxaloacetate to form citrate in the TCA cycle. CS activity is important for aerobic exercise performance and basic metabolic function as a housekeeping enzyme. It has been shown through several mass spectrometry‐based physiological studies that CS is post‐translationally modified (PTM) on numerous residues via acetylation, phosphorylation, and methylation reactions. Few follow‐up studies have been reported on the impact of PTMs on CS activity. Thus, we kinetically characterized several hCS PTM mimics near and distant from the active site by site‐directed mutagenesis coupled with steady‐state kinetics. Most modifications had a negative impact on AcCoA kcat/Km but to a much lesser extent on oxaloacetate kcat/Km. Most notably, the K393 acetylation mimic, K393Q displays an increase in Km for AcCoA relative to WT by about 30‐fold, with no significant change in kcat. To complement our kinetic analyses, we performed molecular dynamics simulations on 26 PTM and mutant CS‐substrate complexes, providing a combined kinetic and MD simulation approach. Among the MD results, CS K393AcK showed the greatest reduction in AcCoA/CoA binding.

1 Introduction

Citrate synthase (CS) catalyzes the aldol condensation of oxaloacetate and acetyl‐CoA forming citrate and CoA in the first step of the TCA cycle. CS has been described as the rate‐limiting step in the TCA cycle, and its activity is used as a metric for mitochondrial function. CS structure is divided into two types: Type I being dominant in mitochondria and lacking NADH regulation while NADH‐dependent [1, 2] Type 2 CS are found generally in gram‐negative bacteria. Type I CS are composed of homodimers whereas Type 2 CS are composed of hexamers [3]. Despite discrepancies in oligomeric state and NADH regulation both CS types obey an ordered bi‐bi induced fit [3, 4, 5] mechanism [6].

There is mounting evidence that metabolic pathways [7, 8] are modified by post‐translational modifications (PTM), such as acetylation [9, 10, 11, 12] and methylation [13, 14, 15], and these modifications have been connected to heart disease [16], diabetes [17], obesity [18], cancer [19], and aerobic exercise capacity [20, 21]. These modifications are lesser characterized; however at the individual protein level, our understanding of their impact on enzyme specific function is sparse [22]. PTMs may either have a negative, neutral, or positive effect on protein function, but conclusions are difficult to ascertain from physiological studies among a background of in vivo metabolic effects that include multiple PTM sites per protein.

E. coli citrate synthase (EcCS) PTMs have been identified by acetylome studies [23] and a subsequent detailed site‐specific characterization demonstrated kinetically relevant acetylation sites that either inhibited or enhanced EcCS activity [24]. For example, EcCS K283 acetylation (K283AcK) improved AcCoA binding while K295AcK impaired AcCoA binding [24]. Such studies elucidate the inherent ambiguity of acetylation even when structures are available.

Human citrate synthase (hCS) has been shown to undergo methylation at K395, near the substrate binding pocket, by a mitochondrial lysine‐specific methyltransferase [25, 26] (METTL12 or CSKMT). Previous studies reported that methylation of K395 has a minor impact on the catalysis of hCS [23], as well as on the steady‐state kinetic parameters of pig heart CS [27]. Interestingly, a neighboring residue in hCS, K393, was shown not to be methylated [25] but yet there is evidence that this residue is acetylated [28]. After performing a structural alignment between hCS and pig heart CS, K393 and K395 in the hCS are found near the AcCoA binding site. In pig heart CS [5], K366 (which aligns with K393 in hCS) is 2.9 Å from the CoA phosphoanhydride suggesting its importance in AcCoA/CoA binding affinity. In addition to those residues mentioned, the Phosphositeplus database [29] reports numerous other PTM sites on CS that remain to be tested. Here, we use site‐directed mutagenesis, steady‐state kinetics, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, to explore the impact of acetylation, methylation, and phosphorylation on hCS.

2 Material and Methods

2.1 Protein Preparation

Purified hCS was purchased through a customized protein purification project commissioned through Genscript (Piscataway, NJ), as described previously [6]. Initially, the wildtype CS amino acid sequence was obtained from Uniprot ID: O75390 and used to generate the wildtype CS gene, minus the mitochondrial localization sequence, in an expression vector (pET30a) with a C‐term 6X histag but was found to be insoluble upon expression test. Therefore, the N‐term 6X histag was generated in the pET30a vector between NdeI and HindIII restriction sites. The WT and mutant CS gene sequences were generated through a proprietary gene synthesis method, with sequences confirmed through DNA sequencing. The plasmid was expressed in BL21 Star (DE3) cells; when the OD600 reached about 1.2 cell culture was induced with 0.4 mM IPTG at 15°C for 16 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer for sonication.

After centrifugation, the sonicated solution was applied to a High Affinity Ni‐NTA column with a 10 min incubation period protein and then was eluted using an imidazole gradient from 20 to 500 mM. Proteins were analyzed for purity using SDS‐PAGE and identified by Western blot and mass spectrometry. Protein concentration was determined by the Bradford protein assay and the final storage buffer was 50 mM Tris–HCl, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol at pH 8. hCS mutant proteins were generated by gene synthesis and purified by the same procedure. All proteins were stored at −80°C. SDS‐PAGE images are provided in Figures S38–S50.

2.2 Enzyme Kinetic Assays

All kinetic assays were performed at ambient temperature in 50 mM Tris at a pH of 7.8 and 0.25 μM CS with 0.22 μm filtered solutions. Chemicals were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich and Fisher Scientific, except acetyl‐CoA, which was purchased from COALA Biosciences (Austin, TX). Kinetic UV absorption assays were performed on a KinTek SF‐300X stopped‐flow mixing device utilizing a deuterium lamp and followed using a multiwavelength CCD array detector. For AcCoA steady‐state kinetics, the CS reaction was followed by observing the absorbance change at 232 nm between acetyl‐CoA and CoA, which has a molar extinction difference of approximately 2 mM−1 cm−1 [30]. Initial rates were determined by fitting the first few seconds of the progress curve. When varying AcCoA, the oxaloacetate concentration was fixed at 20 μM. For oxaloacetate steady‐state kinetics, the CS reaction was followed by adding DPS (4,4′‐dithiodipyridine), 1.5 mM after mixing, where progress curves were fitted at 325 nm6 and the AcCoA concentration was fixed at 20 μM. Apparent kcat and Km were determined by fitting the standard single substrate, single product form of the Michaelis–Menten equation using MATLAB software.

2.3 Molecular Dynamics Simulations

MD simulations were performed using the Desmond program in the Schrodinger software suite (2021–2 to 2023–2) on a Dell 7920 Workstation (dual Intel Xeon Platinum 8260@2.40GHz) utilizing three Nvidia Quadro RTX 5000 GPUs. The pig heart CS structure, in dimeric form, in the so‐called closed conformation (PDB: 2CTS) was used in all cases as a starting point. To generate mutants, the ‘Mutate residue’ function was used within the Schrodinger Maestro GUI to mutate each of the monomers within the full dimer structure. All complexes were prepared using the ‘Schrodinger protein preparation workflow’ which adds hydrogens, optimizes hydrogen bonds, and minimizes the protein. Subsequently, complexes were prepared for simulation using Schrodinger's ‘System Builder program’ using the parameters discussed below.

All complexes were prepared using the OPLS4 force field, SPC explicit solvent model, orthorhombic buffered boundary conditions, and were neutralized by adding Na+ ions. All MD simulations were relaxed prior to simulation and ran for 200 ns using the NPT ensemble at a temperature of 300 K and pressure of 1.01 bar. Each of the MD simulations contained approximately 95 000 atoms. The MD simulations were then analyzed using the ‘Simulation interaction analysis’ program within the Schrodinger suite. The free energy of binding of each CoA ligand over the course of the MD trajectory was calculated using the Schrodinger thermal_mmgbsa.py python script, which calculates the molecular mechanics with generalized Born Surface Area (MM‐GBSA) scoring function for all 1001 frames of the 200 ns MD simulation.

3 Results and Discussion

A wide variety of CS PTMs have been observed including acetylation [28, 29], methylation [25, 26, 27], and phosphorylation [29]. The Phosphositeplus database [29] conveniently tabulates PTMs accounting for the number of references for each modification and the flanking sequence to avoid sequence numbering ambiguity. Thus, the amino acid residues that flank the PTM of interest were used to search for the PTM site in the mature protein sequence lacking the mitochondrial localization sequence. PTM sites of interest are shown on the pig heart citrate synthase structure (Figure 1), which includes bound substrates to show proximity to the active site. Amino acid residue numbering between pig heart and human enzymes is shown.

FIGURE 1 Citrate synthase post‐translational modifications. Pig heart citrate synthase crystal structure [5] in closed (PDB: 2CTS) conformation. Residues with known PTMs are highlighted in CPK coloring with carbons in yellow. Table shows PTM type with corresponding human CS numbering. Structural figure made using PyMOL [31].

CS are known to undergo an induced fit binding mechanism [5, 32], oxaloacetate binding before AcCoA [6], with the AcCoA binding site clamping down on the substrate upon binding (Figure 2). The structures of the open (PDB: 1CTS) and closed conformations (PDB: 2CTS) for the pig heart CS have been determined [5] (Figure 2A). A crystal structure of hCS has only been determined [33] in the open conformation so we aligned the open hCS to pig heart CS and determined that pig heart CS residues K366 and 368 align with hCS K393 and K395. The structural alignment also demonstrates that K393 and K395, which undergo acetylation [28, 29] and methylation [25, 29] respectively, are highly dynamic between the open and closed structures in the substrate binding pocket. The closed conformation of pig heart CS shows that K366 forms an ionic/H‐bonding interaction (~3 Å) with the phosphoanhydride of CoA, whereas K368 appears to orient toward the solvent (Figure 2).

Bound citrate (green) coincides with the open conformation and CoA (yellow) coincides with the closed conformation. (B) hCS (PDB: 5UZQ, green), open conformation [33], aligned with pig heart CS (PDB: 1CTS, blue), open conformation (RMSD = 0.409 Å). The corresponding lysine residues in pig heart (K366 and K368) are highlighted in hCS (K393 and K395). Figure and structural alignments made using PyMOL [31].

To guide experimental mutagenesis, and because of the difficulty of site specifically generating PTMs experimentally, we chose to first simulate the PTM impact through molecular dynamics simulations of the protein/ligand complex. To better understand the impact of PTMs on the binding of CoA/AcCoA, we used the available “closed” structure of pig heart CS (PDB: 2CTS) [5] to assess CoA binding affinity since no “closed” conformation is available for the hCS. As shown above (Figure 2B), the pig heart and hCS “open” structures align very closely (RMSD = 0.409 Å), but it is the closed structure that contains CoA and citrate. Therefore, we chose the “closed” conformation of pig heart CS to assess the binding affinity of CoA to WT and several variants. We want to emphasize that our approach using MD simulations of the pig heart CS as a guide for human CS, is an approximation that does not account for potential sequence differences that could impact the results.

The pig heart CS “closed”, dimeric structure with citrate and CoA bound was prepared, minimized, and then simulated using a 200 ns explicit water molecular dynamics simulation. This simulation demonstrates that substrates, such as CoA (Figure 3B), remain bound during the simulation, where K366 is one of the most important residues interacting with CoA throughout 77% of the simulation. K366 interacts with CoA via H‐bonding and ionic interactions as well as water bridges throughout the MD simulation (Figure 3A,C; red asterisk).

We then generated the K366AcK mutant from the WT citrate/CoA bound structure (PDB: 2CTS), minimized it, and simulated it for 200 ns (Figure 4B). We observed that citrate/CoA remained bound during the simulation (Figure 4B) but AcK366‐to‐CoA interaction is greatly minimized relative to the WT K366 residue even through water bridge interactions (Figure 4A,C, red asterisk).

Since a glutamine substitution is a common choice to mimic N‐acetyllysine [22], we conducted the same MD simulation on pig heart K366Q. We found that although this mutation reduces ionic and H‐bond interactions it allows more water mediated bridging interactions (Figure 5; red asterisk) with CoA relative to lysine (Figure 3). Therefore, the MD simulations predict that a K366Q mutation is less deleterious than the K366AcK mutation, which is further corroborated by calculating the energy of the CS/CoA interaction, discussed below.

We conducted similar MD simulations as shown in Figures 3, 4, 5 with numerous mutations targeting residues shown in Figure 1, that either mimic mutations that can be generated through standard site‐directed mutagenesis or explicit PTMs such as acetylation, (mono/di/tri) methylation, or phosphorylation. The RMSD for all pig heart CS MD simulations with α‐Carbons and CoA over the course of the MD trajectory are shown Figure S1, demonstrating that CoA remained bound throughout all variant MD simulations.

To estimate the interaction energy of the CS/CoA complexes from the MD trajectory for all conducted MD simulations (Figure S2), we ran a Schrodinger‐provided Python script that determines the molecular mechanics generalized Born surface area (MM‐GBSA) scoring function for each frame of the simulation (1001 frames). The fluctuation in binding affinity of WT and various CS variants in complex with CoA as a function of time is shown in Figure S2. Averages and standard deviations of the MM‐GBSA scoring function for each CS variant over the course of the MD trajectory are given in Table S1. Histograms of the MM‐GBSA scoring function along the MD trajectory for WT CS, K366AcK, and K366Q are shown (Figure 6A) demonstrating the predicted decrease in binding affinity for CoA/AcCoA.

To explore the effects of methylation of K368 (pig heart), which aligns to K395 in humans, we also made mono, di, and trimethylation variants in silico. We then ran the same MD simulation as in WT and calculated the MM‐GBSA scoring function (Figure 6B) for the K368 methylation variants. These calculations predict a decrease in binding affinity for methylation mutants (Figure 6B). A K395M mutant, that could be more easily generated experimentally going forward, was also generated and showed similar results as the monomethylated (K395MMK) mutant.

From here, a more comprehensive set of MD simulations were conducted with various PTM mimics and explicit acetylation and phosphorylation variants corresponding to the table of observed PTMs in Figure 1. We also used glutamine, methionine, and glutamate substitutions to mimic N‐acetyllysine, N‐methylation, and phosphorylation, respectively as is common in mutagenesis studies [22].

The average and standard deviation of the MM‐GBSA scoring function along the MD trajectory were calculated to estimate CoA/AcCoA binding affinity for all variants and shown in a sorted bar plot in Figure 7. As expected, WT has the greatest binding affinity for CoA. Acetylation was the most deleterious for a single PTM (K366AcK), whereas the trimethylation (K368TMK) variant was predicted to have the least binding affinity for CoA. We also observed that a glutamate substitution to mimic a phosphorylation variant gave similar predicted binding affinities to phosphorylation such as with S198 and S205 residues, but many of the variant simulations demonstrated overlapping binding affinity (Figure 7). CS amino acid residue interactions with CoA throughout the MD simulations are provided in Figure S3–S11. We have also provided root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) plots in Figure S12–S37 to demonstrate residue‐level dynamics throughout the MD trajectory. Plots indicate very similar RMSF per residue across most variants compared to WT.

Next, we sought to use computational insights to generate PTM mimics through site‐directed mutagenesis. Bacterial strains have been engineered to incorporate unconventional amino acid residues [34], such as N‐acetyllysine or N‐methyllysine using a UAG stop codon, but a truncated version of the protein can still be produced. A C‐term histag ensures that the purified protein is full length. However, we were unable to successfully purify a C‐term histag version of hCS due to insolubility. Therefore, we generated the N‐term histag WT protein along with traditional mutants, guided by the above MD simulations, that mimic the proposed acetylation of K393 [28, 29], methylation of K395 [25, 26], and others discussed above [29]. As in the simulations, we used a glutamine, methionine, and glutamate substitution to mimic an N‐acetyllysine, N‐methylation, and phosphorylation, respectively [22].

We analyzed the impact of the PTM mimics using steady‐state kinetics for both AcCoA and oxaloacetate (Figure 8). We found that hCS K393Q had approximately a 30‐fold increase in apparent Km for AcCoA relative to WT (Figure 8C), with no significant change in kcat (Figure 8A,B), or oxaloacetate apparent steady‐state kinetic parameters (Figure 8B,D,F). In the least, the data suggest that K393 is critical for AcCoA binding, not oxaloacetate binding, or the catalytic step. Furthermore, it can be inferred that N‐acetylation of K393 would disrupt hCS AcCoA binding affinity.

Given the proximity of K395 to K393, we also generated K395Q to compare its impact on substrate binding. hCS K395Q had a similar kcat (Figure 8A) to WT but a 3.7‐fold increase in AcCoA Km (Figure 8C), with about a 5‐fold decrease in kcat/Km for AcCoA (Figure 8E). Since hCS K395 has been shown to be methylated [25, 29], we generated hCS K395M to mimic a monomethylated variant, which had a similar kcat to WT but showed a 4‐fold increase in AcCoA Km (Figure 8C) and about a 5‐fold decrease in kcat/Km for AcCoA (Figure 8E). It was previously shown that the primary methylation variant of K395 is trimethylated (TMK) [35], where it was inferred through steady‐state kinetics that this modification has little impact on pig heart CS synthase kinetics. Thus, the hCS K395M mutant generated here shows a greater impact relative to the pig heart CS K395TMK studied previously [35]. Interestingly, MD simulations of the K395TMK variant predict that this modification has the greatest impact out of all those tested here.

Since hCS K393Q displayed the greatest change relative to WT we further explored this residue with K393R and K393A mutants. We found that the K393A mutant was also disrupted relative to WT with the apparent AcCoA kcat/Km decreasing by about 10‐fold (Figure 8E). The K393R mutant demonstrated a 2‐fold decrease in AcCoA kcat/Km. Alanine and arginine mutations were also generated at K395, with K395A and K395R demonstrating a similar impact as K395M. We found that there was much less negative impact on oxaloacetate steady‐state kinetic parameters for these mutants relative to AcCoA (Figure 8B,D,F).

Next, we decided to investigate previously observed phosphorylation sites at S225, S232, Y80, and Y381, by generating mutants S225E, S232E, Y80E, and Y381E followed by steady‐state kinetic analysis of AcCoA and oxaloacetate (Figure 8). Of these, Y80E had the greatest impact, demonstrating a 10‐fold decrease in kcat, where the Km for AcCoA was unable to be determined due to poor kinetics. Given the location of Y80, which appears to orient its side chain toward the internal cavity of CS (Figure 1), it is possible that the Y80E mutant is destabilized by this mutation and therefore also most likely impacted by phosphorylation at this site. The MD simulation of the pig heart CS PTM Y53pY, corresponding to Y80 in hCS, produced a binding affinity for CoA that was not significantly different from WT (Figure 7), and therefore the 200 ns MD simulation of this PTM deviates from experiment. This discrepancy may reflect the limited sampling inherent in a 200 ns trajectory compared to a second timescale in the kinetic experiment. We should also point out that Y80E was the least pure mutant, being only 40% pure (Figure S50). Although additional bands are better observed on the Y80E gel (Figure S50), they appear to some degree in the other mutant gels including WT. Notably, the WT preparation used here was previously subjected to one of the most rigorous enzyme kinetic evaluations ever published on citrate synthase, where kinetic constants and conclusions fell in line with the literature consensus mechanism [6]. Therefore, we believe the observed impurities have little to no effect on kinetic behavior.

Y381E AcCoA steady‐state kinetics were also attenuated with a 10‐fold decrease in kcat/Km but oxaloacetate kinetics were like WT suggesting that this mutant is stable. In relation to the MD simulations, Y381E corresponding to Y354E in pig heart CS or the explicitly phosphorylated variant Y354pY gives a moderate decrease in CoA binding (Figure 7). Kinetic impacts from S232E and S225E were relatively minor. We also generated the mutant K459Q to mimic reported acetylation at this site, which is outside the range of the available crystal structure and showed a relatively minor impact on steady‐state kinetics (Figure 8). Overall, differences in WT and hCS mutants with oxaloacetate steady‐state kinetics were not significantly different, except for Y80E.

4 Conclusion

PTM of enzymes involved in central metabolism has been suggested to regulate metabolism on a global scale [7, 20, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. Although observations of PTM are certainly ubiquitous, detailed analysis of their effects is not. At the protein level, generating the actual PTM through genetic code expansion is available but it is recommended to purify the modified protein using a c‐terminal affinity tag to ensure purification of full‐length proteins [34]. This avoids the possibility of purification of truncated proteins generated from the nonsense‐codon‐based molecular biology approach. Given our solubility issues with c‐terminal affinity tag purification of hCS, we chose to generate traditional site‐directed mutants using PTM mimics. This experimental approach, although common, does not always mimic the same effect created by the actual PTM [41, 42]. Therefore, our conclusions should be considered in light of published discrepancies between PTMs and their mimics [41, 42].

Our MD simulations of explicit PTMs in pig heart CS predict that previously observed PTMs, mostly near the CS active site, will attenuate AcCoA/CoA binding and this prediction is corroborated by the steady‐state kinetics of PTM mimics. We demonstrated that K393, Y80, and Y381 in hCS are critical residues for catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) with AcCoA and will most likely impact hCS kinetics when post‐translationally modified. The most impactful of these is the K393Q acetylation mimic, which increases the Km for AcCoA by 30‐fold, with no significant effect on the kcat, suggesting that acetylation of K393 will greatly impact AcCoA/CoA binding but not the catalytic step. MD simulations also support acetylation of this residue as the most attenuating for AcCoA/CoA binding. In support of K393 and K395's impact on CS activity, these residues have also been shown to be succinylated [43], where desuccinylation by SIRT5 increased CS activity and promoted colon cancer cell proliferation and migration [43].

The monomethylation mimic hCS K395M yields a minor perturbation in AcCoA Km whereas the explicit MD simulated mono, di, and trimethylation K395 variants in pig heart CS (K368) suggest a larger perturbation of AcCoA/CoA binding affinity in contrast to a prior report [27]. For instance, Evans et al. [27] reported with natively purified pig heart citrate synthase the existence of a trimethylated K368 (equivalent to K395 in hCS) but concluded no kinetic impact relative to the unmodified protein generated in E. coli . However, a more recent report demonstrates succinylation of these residues (K393 and K395) in humans reduces CS activity [43]. Phosphorylation mimics S232E, Y381E, S225E, and Y80E resulted in decreased kcat and elevated AcCoA Km values. Altogether our data support the potentially large influence of PTM effects on hCS activity mediated through AcCoA/CoA binding affinity not demonstrated previously. Decreased CS activity by PTMs could negatively impact TCA cycle flux in humans to regulate aerobic metabolism and metabolic disease, and these mechanisms have been linked to colon cancer [43], Parkinson disease [44], and exercise performance [20, 21, 45, 46]. One can surmise that decreasing CS activity via PTMs would decrease TCA flux, hindering aerobic capacity and thus exercise capacity. PTMs can also influence fuel selection or metabolic flexibility [20, 21, 45], which is thought to be an underlying mechanism for metabolic disease [47, 48].
