BRIEF SUMMARY

This study tested whether a home-based exercise program helps older adults with
Parkinson's disease keep the improvements they gain from a community balance program.

Participants lived in small towns and rural communities in Oklahoma, where access to
movement disorder specialists is limited. All participants first took part in a
three-month community-based recreational therapy balance program. At the end of those
three months, participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups. One group
continued with a home-based program for twelve weeks, which included an exercise manual,
instructional videos, and a weekly telephone call from a researcher. The other group
received general wellness guidance without follow-up contact.

Participants were assessed three times: before the community program began, at three
months, and at six months. The assessments measured walking speed, stride length, and
cadence; postural stability; concern about falling; anxiety and depression; social
connectedness; and quality of life.

The purpose of the study was to learn whether continued support at home helps people hold
onto the gains they make in a community program, and how quickly those gains are lost
without that support.

DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Parkinson's disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder. In rural regions, where
access to movement disorder specialists is limited, individuals with Parkinson's disease
face increased risks of mobility decline, falls, and social isolation. In Oklahoma, 34%
of the population lives in rural areas. Although physical activity improves mobility,
reduces fall risk, and enhances quality of life, fewer than 30% of individuals with
Parkinson's disease exercise regularly. Rural patients face barriers including limited
transportation, fear of injury, and a lack of local programs or peer support.

Preliminary pilot work showed short-term gains in gait velocity and balance following
supervised training, but these improvements regressed during follow-up without continued
support. This study evaluated a hybrid community-to-home model designed to prevent that
regression.

Design. This was a randomized controlled trial conducted in small-city and rural
communities in Oklahoma. All participants first completed a three-month community-based
recreational therapy balance program. At the three-month assessment, participants were
randomly assigned to either a home-based program with weekly remote coaching or to a
control condition receiving general wellness guidance without targeted follow-up.

Intervention. The home-based phase lasted twelve weeks and included a structured exercise
manual, video-guided instruction, and weekly remote coaching by telephone. The coaching
was intended to reinforce adherence and to address motivation and safety. Adherence was
tracked through weekly contact records.

Assessments. Data were collected at baseline, three months, and six months. Gait speed,
stride length, and cadence were measured with the GAITRite electronic walkway (CIR
Systems, New Jersey, USA). Postural stability was assessed with the BioSway system
(Biodex Medical Systems, New York, USA). Secondary outcomes included fall-related
self-efficacy (Short Falls Efficacy Scale-International), psychosocial health (Hospital
Anxiety and Depression Scale), social connectedness (Lubben Social Network Scale), and
quality of life (PDQ-39).

Hypotheses. (1) Participants in the home-based program with remote coaching would show
greater improvement in gait velocity, stride length, and balance at six months than the
control group. (2) The intervention group would maintain higher adherence and show
reduced fear of falling. (3) The intervention group would report improved psychological
well-being and social connectedness.

Analysis. A mixed-model repeated-measures analysis of variance was used, with Bonferroni
correction for multiple comparisons and effect sizes to evaluate clinical significance.

  -
    Measure: GAITRite system
    Description
      Gait parameters in centimeters per second measured with the GAITRite electronic walkway
      (CIR Systems, New Jersey, USA). Higher values indicate faster walking.
    Time frame: Baseline, 3 months, 6 months
Secondary outcomes
  -
    Measure: BioSway system
    Description
      Postural stability measured with the BioSway balance system (Biodex Medical Systems, New
      York, USA). Higher sway values indicate poorer postural stability.
    Time frame: Baseline, 3 months, 6 months
  -
    Measure: Change in fall-related self-efficacy measured by the Short Falls Efficacy Scale-International
    Description
      Seven-item scale scored from 7 to 28. Higher scores indicate greater concern about
      falling.
    Time frame: Baseline, 3 months, 6 months
  -
    Measure: Change in anxiety and depression measured by the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale
    Description
      Fourteen-item scale scored from 0 to 42. Higher scores indicate greater anxiety and
      depressive symptoms.
    Time frame: Baseline, 3 months, 6 months
  -
    Measure: Change in social connectedness measured by the Lubben Social Network Scale-6
    Description: Six-item scale scored from 0 to 30. Higher scores indicate greater social connectedness.
    Time frame: Baseline, 3 months, 6 months
  -
    Measure: Change in quality of life measured by the PDQ-39
    Description
      Parkinson's Disease Questionnaire-39 summary index scored from 0 to 100. Higher scores
      indicate worse quality of life.
    Time frame: Baseline, 3 months, 6 months
