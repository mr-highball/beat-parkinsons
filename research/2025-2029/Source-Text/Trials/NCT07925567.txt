BRIEF SUMMARY

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by
motor and non-motor impairments that negatively affect functional independence and
quality of life. Although multimodal rehabilitation approaches have demonstrated
therapeutic benefits, underlying pathophysiological mechanisms may limit the magnitude
and persistence of treatment effects. In this context, transcranial direct current
stimulation (tDCS) has emerged as a potential strategy to enhance neuroplasticity and
optimize motor responses to physical rehabilitation.

This randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled clinical trial aimed to investigate the
effects of tDCS combined with Nordic walking as part of a multimodal rehabilitation
program on upper limb function in individuals with Parkinson's disease. Participants were
randomly assigned to receive either active or sham tDCS, both combined with the same
multimodal rehabilitation program over four consecutive weeks. Upper limb motor
performance, manual dexterity, handgrip strength, and quality of life were assessed
before and after the intervention, as well as at a 30-day follow-up. Data were analyzed
using linear mixed-effects models, with a significance level of 5%.

DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Background and Rationale

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by
motor and non-motor impairments that negatively affect functional independence and
quality of life. Upper limb motor dysfunction is particularly relevant in individuals
with PD, as bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor, and impaired motor coordination can
compromise manual dexterity, movement amplitude, and the performance of activities of
daily living.

Nordic walking (NW) is a physical exercise modality that promotes active upper limb
involvement during gait through the use of specialized poles, encouraging interlimb
coordination, rhythmic movements, and neuromuscular activation. This exercise modality
has been investigated as a therapeutic strategy to improve motor performance, functional
mobility, and quality of life in individuals with PD.

However, disease-related alterations in cortical excitability and neural plasticity may
limit the magnitude and persistence of exercise-induced improvements. Transcranial direct
current stimulation (tDCS) is a non-invasive neuromodulation technique capable of
modulating cortical excitability and potentially enhancing motor learning and
training-induced neuroplasticity. Combining tDCS with Nordic walking may therefore
provide additional therapeutic benefits for upper limb motor function in individuals with
PD.

Study Objective

The objective of this study was to investigate the effects of active tDCS compared with
sham tDCS, both combined with Nordic walking, on upper limb motor performance, manual
dexterity, handgrip strength, and quality of life in individuals with Parkinson's
disease. The study also investigated whether the effects of the intervention were
maintained at a 30-day follow-up.

Study Design

This was a prospective, randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled clinical trial
conducted at the State University of Pará (UEPA), Belém, Pará, Brazil, between June 2025
and April 2026.

A total of 34 individuals with Parkinson's disease were randomized into two parallel
groups in a 1:1 allocation ratio: an experimental group receiving active tDCS combined
with Nordic walking (n = 17) and a control group receiving sham tDCS combined with Nordic
walking (n = 17).

The randomization sequence was generated by an independent researcher using Microsoft
Excel 2016. Allocation concealment was ensured through sequentially numbered, sealed,
opaque envelopes. Following baseline assessment, an independent researcher assigned an
individual numerical code to each participant. The code was entered into the stimulation
device, which automatically delivered the corresponding active or sham stimulation
protocol without revealing the allocation.

Participants and researchers responsible for assessments and intervention delivery
remained blinded to group allocation throughout the study.

Intervention Protocol

The intervention program consisted of 12 sessions conducted three times per week over
four consecutive weeks. Both groups received the same Nordic walking protocol, differing
exclusively in the application of active or sham tDCS.

Before beginning the intervention program, all participants completed two familiarization
sessions with Nordic walking, without tDCS.

Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation

Transcranial direct current stimulation was administered using a Microestim-NKL
electrical stimulation device with silicone electrodes covered by saline-soaked sponges.

The anodal electrode was positioned over the supplementary motor area (SMA), while the
cathodal electrode was placed over the right frontopolar region (Fp2), according to the
international 10-20 electroencephalography system.

In the experimental group, active stimulation was delivered at an intensity of 2 mA for
20 minutes per session, with 30-second ramp-up and ramp-down periods.

In the control group, sham stimulation was administered using the same electrode montage
and initial stimulation parameters. The device delivered current for approximately 30
seconds before automatically discontinuing stimulation, without visual or auditory
signals. This procedure was intended to reproduce the initial scalp sensations associated
with active stimulation and preserve participant blinding.

Nordic Walking Training

Nordic walking was performed according to the principles of the International Nordic
Walking Federation (INWA). Participants used specialized walking poles and received
instructions regarding upright posture, coordinated movements of the upper and
contralateral lower limbs, trunk stabilization, shoulder and pelvic girdle dissociation,
and active pole propulsion.

Training sessions were conducted along an approximately 1-km outdoor circuit at the State
University of Pará. The route included different surfaces, such as paved pathways, grass,
ramps, and indoor corridors, to promote neuromuscular adaptation and dynamic postural
control.

Walking speed was self-selected, and the total distance covered was individualized
according to each participant's functional capacity and exercise tolerance. Five minutes
of regular walking were performed before and after the main activity as warm-up and
cool-down periods.

Outcome Assessments

Participants underwent clinical and functional assessments at baseline, immediately after
the four-week intervention, and at a 30-day follow-up.

Upper limb motor performance was assessed using the upper limb subscore of Part III of
the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UL-UPDRS-III), focusing on motor
manifestations affecting the upper limb most affected by Parkinson's disease, including
tremor, rigidity, finger tapping, hand movements, and pronation-supination movements.

Manual dexterity was evaluated bilaterally using the Box and Block Test (BBT), which
measures the number of wooden blocks transferred between compartments within 60 seconds.

Handgrip strength was measured bilaterally using a hand dynamometer, following
standardized procedures with three attempts and calculation of the mean value.

Quality of life was assessed using the 39-item Parkinson's Disease Questionnaire
(PDQ-39), which evaluates eight dimensions of health-related quality of life.

All assessments and interventions were conducted during the ON medication state to
minimize the influence of motor fluctuations. Heart rate, peripheral oxygen saturation,
and blood pressure were monitored before and after intervention sessions.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi software, version 2.6. Descriptive
statistics were used to characterize the study population, and the Shapiro-Wilk test was
applied to assess the normality of continuous variables.

Baseline comparisons between groups were conducted using Student's t-test or the
Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate, while categorical variables were compared using
Fisher's exact test.

Outcome analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle and were conducted using
linear mixed-effects models to examine group-by-time interactions. The significance of
these interactions was assessed using likelihood ratio tests. When significant
interactions were identified, post hoc comparisons were performed with Holm-Bonferroni
adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Hedges' g was calculated to estimate effect sizes for between-group comparisons.
Statistical significance was established at 5% (p < 0.05).

Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with Brazilian National Health Council Resolution
No. 466/2012 and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the State University of
Pará (CEP/UEPA), under ethical approval identification number CAAE 86894625.0.0000.8767.
All participants provided written informed consent before participating in the study.

REGISTERED OUTCOME MEASURES

Primary outcomes
  -
    Measure: Change in Upper Limb Motor Function Assessed by the MDS-UPDRS Part III
    Description
      Upper limb motor function was assessed using the upper limb motor items of Part III of
      the Movement Disorder Society-Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS). The
      assessment focused on the most affected upper limb and included motor manifestations such
      as rigidity, finger tapping, hand movements, pronation-supination movements, and tremor.
      Higher scores indicate greater motor impairment, whereas lower scores indicate better
      motor function. Changes in scores were evaluated across the three assessment time points.
    Time frame: Baseline, immediately after the 4-week intervention, and 30 days after intervention completion (follow-up).

ELIGIBILITY

Eligibility criteria
  Inclusion Criteria:

    -  Individuals of both sexes aged 45 to 80 years.

    -  Clinical diagnosis of Parkinson's disease confirmed by a medical report.

    -  Disease severity classified between stages 1.5 and 3 on the Hoehn and Yahr scale.

    -  Stable medication regimen for at least one month before the beginning of the
       intervention.

    -  Ability to attend assessments and intervention sessions during the ON medication
       state.

    -  Ability to walk independently.

    -  Willingness to participate in the study and provision of written informed consent.

  Exclusion Criteria:

    -  Other neurological, orthopedic, or cardiorespiratory conditions that could
       compromise the safe performance of assessments or interventions.

    -  Severe uncorrected visual or hearing impairments that could interfere with
       understanding instructions or performing study procedures.

    -  Previous deep brain stimulation (DBS) or other neurosurgical procedures for
       Parkinson's disease.

    -  Electronic devices or metallic implants incompatible with transcranial direct
       current stimulation (tDCS).

    -  History of epilepsy, seizures, or acquired brain injury.

    -  Skin lesions, infections, or other dermatological conditions affecting the scalp
       areas designated for electrode placement.

    -  Cognitive impairment identified using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE),
       based on education-adjusted cutoff scores: 13 for illiterate individuals, 18 for
       individuals with low or intermediate educational levels, and 26 for individuals with
       higher educational levels.

    -  Concurrent physiotherapy treatment or regular physical activity that could interfere
       with the effects of the study interventions. Individuals who discontinued these
       activities before study initiation were eligible to participate.
