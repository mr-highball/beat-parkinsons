Primary Abstract:
Longevity medicine increasingly focuses on extending healthspan rather than lifespan alone, and Nigella sativa (black seed), with its long history of traditional use, along with thymoquinone (TQ), its principal bioactive compound, has drawn growing interest for its effects on several pathways implicated in aging. To summarize the evidence for N. sativa as a potential healthspan-promoting agent, we conducted a narrative review of the peer-reviewed literature from PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, together with classical Islamic medical texts and authenticated Prophetic narrations, considering in vitro studies, animal studies, and human randomized controlled trials, with a focus on aging-related mechanisms, human evidence, Islamic medical tradition, and remaining research gaps. Case reports and case series were excluded. TQ shows antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, metabolic, neuroprotective, and cellular effects relevant to several hallmarks of aging, and human studies show modest improvements in cardiometabolic risk factors, inflammatory markers, oxidative stress, and some cognitive measures. Overall, N. sativa is a promising multimodal candidate for healthspan research, but evidence that it extends human healthspan is not yet established, and longer trials using standardized preparations and aging-specific outcomes are needed.

[Conclusions] The evidence reviewed here suggests that ancient interest in N. sativa has a plausible modern scientific basis, although its potential role in longevity remains unproven. Modern molecular and clinical research provides plausible explanations for several traditional observations: N. sativa and TQ can influence oxidative stress, inflammation, cardiometabolic risk, cognitive function, and cellular pathways relevant to aging, but most of the evidence remains short-term or preclinical. For longevity medicine, N. sativa is best viewed as a promising multimodal candidate rather than an established anti-aging therapy. It may help support healthspan through several biological pathways, but it is not a cure for aging, and traditional Hadith should not be interpreted as a literal clinical claim. The next step is rigorous, long-term clinical research using standardized preparations and aging-specific outcomes to determine whether the biological promise of black seed translates into meaningful improvements in human healthspan.
