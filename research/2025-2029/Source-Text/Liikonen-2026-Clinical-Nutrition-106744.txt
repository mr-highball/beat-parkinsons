Background & aims: A healthy diet rich in fibre-containing  foods such as oats supports cardiometabolic 
health. Bioprocessing methods, including fermentation and enzymatic treatment, may further enhance the 
health benefits of oat-based foods by altering their physicochemical properties. The aims of this study were 
to investigate the effects of consuming fermented and non-fermented oat-based products enriched with 
fibre and protein on cardiometabolic outcomes gastrointestinal symptoms, and to consider how assessed 
physicochemical and nutritional differences between the products might relate to any observed effects.
Methods: In a 12-week randomised crossover trial, 56 adults with mild metabolic deterioration 
consumed fermented (gurt) and non-fermented (porridge) oat-based products enriched with fibre and 
protein as part of their habitual diet for three weeks each. The study products were specifically 
developed and prepared for this study using identical ingredients. Primary cardiometabolic factors and 
gastrointestinal symptoms (GSRS) were measured at four time points, while secondary outcomes were 
assessed at baseline and after both product periods. Physicochemical and nutritional characterization of 
the study products included cereal β-glucan (BG) and protein molecular weight distribution, starch and 
sugar analysis, microscopy, acidity, and viscosity.
Results: During the gurt consumption, non-high-density lipoprotein (non-HDL) and low-density lipo
protein (LDL) cholesterol concentrations decreased (-0.15 ± 0.51 mmol/L, p = 0.028; and 
-0.12 ± 0.46 mmol/L, p = 0.047, respectively), with a minimal impact on blood pressure and GSRS scores. 
Additionally, ferritin was lower after the gurt compared with baseline (-4.00 [-16.50, 6.25] μg/L, 
p = 0.015). Similarly, ferritin levels were lower after the porridge period (-7.50 [-20.50, 4.25] μg/L), 
accompanied with a modest decrease in blood pressure and HbA1c. These effects, however, did not 
substantially differ between the product periods. Insulin showed a significant  sequence effect (pse

1. Introduction

In parallel with growing interest in plant-based diets, the 
popularity and market share of plant-based alternatives, such as 
plant-based dairy alternatives (PBDAs), is expanding [1,2]. The 
shift from dairy products to PBDAs might influence the intake of 
some nutrients [3]. Apart from soy-based dairy alternatives, most 
PBDAs have lower protein contents compared to dairy counter
parts [4,5]. While plant-based foods generally contain more fibre 
than animal-based products, the amounts of fibre are low in many 
PBDAs. With respect to fat quality, PBDAs typically contain a higher 
proportion of unsaturated fats than dairy products. Additionally, 
micronutrient content varies not only between dairy and PBDAs 
but also between different PBDAs [5]. These nutritional differences 
may cause distinct metabolic responses and therefore affect health 
outcomes differently compared with dairy products. In addition, 
the choice of raw materials used in PBDAs also significantly con
tributes to these impacts.

Oat (Avena sativa L.) has high levels of both micro- and mac
ronutrients, making it one of the most nutritious cereals [6]. 
Compared to other cereals, it is particularly rich in protein, un
saturated fats [6,7], antioxidants, and phytochemicals with po
tential health benefits  [8]. In addition (1→3) (1→4)-β-D-glucan 
(BG), the main soluble fibre in oat grain, is well known for its ef
fects on lowering cholesterol levels and reducing postprandial 
glucose responses, with approved health claims [9,10]. However, 
properties of oat BG, such as molecular weight (MW), are expected 
to impact health outcomes [11–13]. These attributes make oat a 
promising raw material for various food applications, including 
PBDAs [14–16]. Furthermore, combining different plant sources, 
such as oat and protein-rich faba bean (Vicia faba) [17], in such 
products can enhance nutritional quality by complementing 
essential amino acids [18].

Bioprocessing, including enzymatic treatments and fermenta
tion by lactic acid bacteria (LAB), may bring additional health 
benefits  to oat-based foods by modifying their physicochemical 
properties [14]. Tailored fermentation processes can increase the 
concentrations and bioavailability of nutrients and bioactive 
compounds in plant-based foods, while reducing the concentra
tions of antinutrients, like phytic acid and vicine, consequently 
enhancing the digestibility of nutrients [19–22]. Including en
zymes (e.g., amylase, xylanase, glucanase) in the process can 
further improve the digestibility and utilisation of cereal raw 
materials by targeting starch and BG structures [16,23,24]. 
Fermentation can also reduce the levels of other compounds 
commonly found in plant-based foods, which can cause gastro
intestinal symptoms in patients with gut-intolerance conditions, 
such as fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, mono
saccharides and polyols (FODMAPs) [25]. Despite evidence sup
porting the health benefits of oat consumption and fermentation, 
clinical evidence on the cardiometabolic effects of fermented oat- 
based foods remains scarce. To improve readability, the term 

“fermentation” is used throughout this work to refer collectively to 
both fermentation and enzymatic treatments.

Plant-based yoghurt alternatives, hereafter referred to as 
“gurts”, are typically produced through fermentation processes 
and heat treatment to obtain desirable properties, like appealing 
flavour and texture, in the end products [26,27]. Oat-based gurts 
commonly have high nutrient density, but the fibre  and protein 
content stays modest [5]. Thus, by developing novel products us
ing raw materials that align with growing interest, we aimed to 
demonstrate the potential to provide more nutritionally satisfying 
and health-promoting gurt. In the present study, we upscaled a 
fermented gurt containing oat and faba bean ingredients previ
ously described [28], which was developed to achieve higher fibre 
and protein contents than comparable commercial products. To 
further assess the effects of fermentation on product characteris
tics and, consequently, on health, the gurt was physicochemically 
characterised alongside a non-fermented counterpart produced 
using identical ingredients.

The main aim of this study was to investigate whether the 
consumption of fermented and non-fermented oat-based products 
enriched with fibre  and protein, as part of a habitual diet, in
fluences  cardiometabolic outcomes and gastrointestinal symp
toms, and to consider how assessed physicochemical and 
nutritional differences between the products might be related to 
any observed cardiometabolic outcomes. We hypothesised that 
daily consumption of the fermented oat-based gurt would differ
entially affect cardiometabolic markers compared with non- 
fermented porridge, without causing adverse effects on gastroin
testinal symptoms.

2. Material & methods

A randomised clinical intervention study was conducted at the 
University of Eastern Finland in Kuopio during 2024. The present 
study was conducted according to the guidelines laid down in the 
Declaration of Helsinki and all procedures involving human par
ticipants were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the 
Wellbeing Services county of North Savo (Finland) (no. 739/2023). 
Participation in the study was voluntary. Participants provided a 
written informed consent prior to participation, and they had right 
to decline participation or withdraw from the study at any point 
without providing a reason. The study was registered in 
ClinicalTrials.gov as NCT06393114 on January 4th, 2024.

Participants were recruited from the Kuopio region through 
electronic and printed advertisements during Spring and Autumn 
2024. In addition, a study nurse contacted eligible individuals who 
had previously participated in studies at the Institute of Public 
Health and Clinical Nutrition at the University of Eastern Finland 
and had given consent to be contacted. Inclusion criteria were the

ranging from 30 to 115 participants; our 56 participants fell within 
this range. Although inflammatory markers were designated as the 
primary outcomes for the overall study, the present analysis fo

sentative marker of systemic inflammation.  The sample size 
determined for inflammatory outcomes is expected to be adequate 
to detect effects in cardiometabolic markers. Accordingly, these 
outcomes are interpreted within the context of the original study 
design and its powering assumptions.

2.2. Study design

The study was a 12-week randomised, controlled crossover trial 
(Fig. 2), during which participants consumed two study products: 
1) a fermented gurt designed at the University of Helsinki (HPHF 
[28]) and upscaled and produced by Valio Ldt (Finland), and 2) a 
non-fermented porridge designed and produced by the University 
of Helsinki (Helsinki, Finland). The composition and characteri
sation of the products are described in later sections (Section 2.6). 
At baseline, participants attended a group session in which a 
dietitian or a master's student in nutrition science advised them 
on keeping a food diary and complete health-related question
naires. This group session was followed by three weeks with a 
habitual diet. After this run-in period, based on an allocation 
created by a computerised simple random sequence generator and 
performed by an external researcher, part of participants 
consumed the gurt daily for three weeks while others consumed 
the porridge. The daily consumption of study products was 3–6 dL. 
The precise amount depended on individual energy requirements, 
estimated by assessing the basal metabolic rate with the Mifflin-St 
Jeor formula [29]. The results were then multiplied by factor 1.3 to 
consider physical activity. To minimise the carry-over effect of the 
first  study product, participants next underwent a three-week 
washout period wherein, once again, they consumed their 
habitual diet. They then consumed the other study product for 

three weeks. Both products were served with a commercial berry 
soup (2 dL/d).

Before study visits, participants completed three-day food di
aries and filled the questionnaires electronically at home. During 
the study visits, a trained study nurse collected fasting blood 
samples and measured anthropometrics, and the dietitian or the 
master's student in nutrition science provided guidance on using 
the study products and necessary dietary modifications. To mod
erate the protein intake, the consumption of dairy and PBDAs was 
limited. Additionally, as increased fibre content in the meal could 
cause gastrointestinal symptoms, fibre intake, in the form of cereal 
product consumption, was slightly restricted. During both test 
periods, the modifications  in the habitual diet were similar. 
Outside these modifications,  throughout the study, participants 
were instructed to maintain their habitual diet and lifestyle habits 
and avoid intentional weight loss.

2.3. Food and nutrient intake

To assess dietary intake during the study, participants kept 
paper food diaries during three consecutive days at the end of each 
study period, including two weekdays and one weekend day. In 
addition, participants recorded the daily study product con
sumption into a separate paper form. The research personnel 
reviewed the food diaries and study production consumption 
together with participants during the study visits. The food diaries 
were entered by nutrition science master student and subse
quently food group consumption, energy, and nutrient intakes 
were calculated using the Finmeal program [30] utilizing the 
Finnish national food composition database Fineli® [31]. Food 
groups were determined by disaggregating the consumed foods 
into their constituent ingredients according to the standard rec
ipes in the Fineli® database. Our assessment focused on food 
groups that are key contributors to dietary fibre  (DF), BG, and 
protein intake, as well as whose consumption patterns changed in 
response to incorporating the study products into the diet. One 
participant did not fill  a food diary before the gurt period and 
another one before the porridge period. Therefore, they were 
excluded from the analyses assessing dietary changes during those 
product periods.

To further help us assess participants’ dietary habits, they 
completed a qualitative Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) [32] 
at the beginning and the end of the study. The FFQ contained the 
following food groups: red meat, poultry, fish, eggs, dairy products, 
vegetables/fruits/berries, plant-based meat alternatives (PBMAs), 
PBDAs, whole grain products, and oat products. Participants 
evaluated the current habitual consumption frequency of these 
foods using a seven-point Likert scale (1 = not consumed at all; 
2 = less than monthly; 3 = 1–2 times per month; 4 = once per 
week; 5 = 2–4 times per week; 6 = 5–6 times per week; 7 = at least 
once per day). The changes in the consumption frequency of 
selected food groups during the study were assessed on the whole 
study population level.

2.4. Blood sampling and anthropometrics

Weight, height, blood pressure and waist circumference were 
measured, and venous blood samples were collected after a 10-h 
fast on every study visit. Blood pressure was measured twice, 
with a 1-min break between measurements. Participants were 
instructed to withhold consuming alcohol for 48 h and intense 
exercising for 24 h before the laboratory visits, and to eat as nor
mally as possible while avoiding abnormally large portions of food.

2.5. Gastrointestinal symptoms and perceived health

To monitor possible gastrointestinal symptoms during the 
intervention, participants reported their symptoms with the 
Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale (GSRS) [35,36] after every 
study period. Responses are given in a seven-point Likert scale 
with higher scores indicating stronger symptoms. The items were 
divided into five symptom clusters: abdominal pain, reflux, indi
gestion, diarrhoea and constipation [36]. In addition, a total GSRS 
score was calculated as the mean score of these clusters to assess 
overall gastrointestinal discomfort. One participant did not fill the 
GSRS before the porridge period. Therefore, this participant was 
excluded from the analyses assessing changes in the gastrointes
tinal symptoms during the porridge period. In addition to 
completing the questionnaire, participants were instructed to 
contact the dietitian if abnormal or excessive gastrointestinal 
symptoms occurred.

To assess whether changes in participants’ perceived health 
might account for observed effects on cardiometabolic and 
gastrointestinal outcomes, participants assessed their perceived 
health with the RAND 36-item Health Survey 1.0 (RAND-36) [37] 
and perceived psychological distress with the 12-item General 
Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12) [38] after every study period. The 
RAND-36 responses were recoded and clustered into indices, 
which formed eight scales consisting of physical and social func
tionality, role limitations caused by physical and emotional health 
problems, energy/fatigue, emotional wellbeing, bodily pain, and 
general health perception [37]. Higher scores indicated a more 

favourable health state. The GHQ-12 responses were scored using 
a three-point Likert scale, where higher scores indicated higher 
psychological distress. One participant did not fill  the question
naires before the porridge period. Therefore, this participant was 
excluded from the analyses assessing changes in these parameters 
during the porridge period.

2.6.1. Preparation of the study products
The gurt was produced in approximately 100 L batches on a 

pilot line provided by Omve with tubular heaters and coolers (De 
Meern, The Netherlands) and a line equipped with aseptic pack
aging to guarantee safe delivery for consumer intervention. An 
Ystral mixer (Ystral GmbH Maschinenbau + Processtechnik, 
Ballrechten-Dottingen, Germany) was used to mix the dry in
gredients (20.9% fw), which were gradually added to tap water 
(78.9%) at 60 ◦C previously mixed with enzyme solutions pur
chased from DSM Food Specialties B.V. (Delft, The Netherlands). 
Amylase (0.1% fw), amyloglucosidase (0.007% fw), and β-glucanase 
(0.007% fw) were used to achieve a controlled hydrolysis of starch 
and BG and to keep viscosity under control during the gurt pro
duction. On each production date, three consecutive batches were 
made (37 kg each). Batches were individually incubated for 
approximately 1 h and combined into a 120 L jacketed tank. The 
mixture was homogenised and pasteurised (100/20 bar, 90 ◦C, 
5 min) and then cooled to the fermentation temperature (40 ◦C) 
using a tubular cooler. The microbial starter culture was added as 
0.1% and stirred for 5 min before overnight incubation at 40 ◦C. 
After the pH reached 4.6, the gurt was mixed and cooled to 20 ◦C 
using a tubular cooler in an aseptic tank for packing. The product 
was automatically packed into 2 L aluminium foil bags at 1.2 kg 
portions. Further cooling was performed in cold storage at 8 ◦C 
before transportation to the University of Eastern Finland. The gurt 
was ready to be consumed as such.

The porridge was prepared manually, with the dry ingredients 
individually weighed into single-use portions (42 g), packed and 
sealed. An instruction label was added to each bag. Packages were 
delivered to the University of Eastern Finland and kept at room 
temperature. Participants were instructed to add 2 dL of water per 
packaged portion and cook the porridge either in a microwave or 
in a pot for 2–3 min, stirring twice. The final volume of resulting 
mixture remained 2 dL, while the total mass increased to 242 g due 
to the added solids. As a result, the mass per volume (e.g., 1 dL was 
higher for the porridge than for the gurt, despite both products 
containing identical amounts of raw materials.

2.6.2. Physicochemical and nutritional properties of the study 
products
2.6.2.1. Acidity, viscosity and microbial cell density. Developed 
acidity was measured at the end of fermentation for the gurt and 
after cooking for the porridge via pH meter (Mettler Toledo Sev
en2Go™ pH Meter S2, Switzerland). Total titratable acidity (TTA) 
was measured on three biological replicates by using an automatic 
pH titrator (Easy Plus Mettler Toledo, Columbus, OH, USA), ac
cording to P€ori et al. (2023) [39]. Organic acid analysis was con
ducted on one biological replicate of gurt and porridge according 
to Tuccillo et al. (2022) [40] with slight modifications. Freeze-dried 
samples (500 mg) were analysed in triplicate with the addition of 
6 mL of Tris–HCl buffer (50 mM, pH 8.8). Following steps were as 
reported in the method cited. Viscosity was measured from two 
biological replicates in triplicate at 23 ◦C using a rotational 
rheometer (Rheolab QC, Anton Paar GmbH, Graz, Austria) equip
ped with a temperature device (C-PTD 180/AIR/QC), an 
ST22.02–4 V probe, and a measuring cup (C-CC27/QC-LTD). The 
reported viscosity corresponds to the mean value at a shear rate of 
75.5 s− 1. The growth of LAB was confirmed following Method ISO 
27205:2010 | IDF 149:2010 [41]. The microbial safety of the gurt 
produced was ensured by plating method ISO 13559:2002 | IDF 
153:2002 [42] (LAB excluded) for each batch delivered for the 
clinical trial and followed up for 2–3 months [37].

2.6.2.2. Sugars, oligosaccharides and total starch content. 
Mono- and disaccharides were quantified,  and oligosaccharide 

profile was analysed on freeze-dried samples as described in Xu 
et al. (2017) [43] and as previously described [28], respectively. 
Total starch content was quantified  by an external accredited 
laboratory (Eurofins Food & Feed Testing Leipzig GmbH, Leipzig, 
Germany) on one biological replicate according to Method NEN- 
EN-ISO 15914M, W55110 via spectrophotometric analysis [44].

2.6.2.3. Dietary fibre and β-glucan analyses. Total DF content and 
its composition were analysed on one biological replicate (freeze- 
dried) by an external accredited laboratory (Eurofins Food & Feed 
Testing Leipzig GmbH, Leipzig, Germany) using the method AOAC 
2022.01 [45]. Total BG content was measured on freeze-dried 
samples in triplicate using a commercial Mixed-Linkage β-glucan 
Assay kit (Megazyme Ltd, Bray, Ireland) and following the pro
cedure for processed cereal products). Oat flour was used as con
trol. Several batches of gurt produced were analysed to ensure the 
reproducibility of the manufacturing process. BG content was 
expressed as a percentage (%) of dw.

To determine weight-average molar mass (aMw), BG was 
extracted according to Wouters et al. (2026) [46]. In brief, freeze- 
dried and homogenised samples were washed and defatted with 
ethanol. BG was extracted by shaking the prepared samples sus
pended in water for 2 h at 40 ◦C. Subsequently, starch, protein and 
xylans were digested in 3 individual incubations with α-amylase 
from Bacillus licheniformis, pancreatin from porcine pancreas, and 
xylanase from Thermomyces lanuginosus, respectively. Finally, BG 
was precipitated with 80% EtOH. The precipitate (BG extract) was 
collected, washed and dried. Subsequently, 100 mg of the BG 
extract were suspended in water to a concentration of 1.2 % (w/v) 
and then further diluted to 0.5 mg/mL with mobile phase (0.1 M

2.7. Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses for clinical outcomes were performed using 
R version 4.2.2 (R Core Team, 2022). First, we assessed potential 
sequence effects for primary cardiometabolic outcomes visually 
and using linear mixed model for repeated measures (R packages 
lme4 [49], lmerTest [50], and emmeans [51]) with sequence*week 
interaction, specifically by comparing values at weeks 3 (after run- 
in) and 9 (after washout) to evaluate baseline equivalence across 
sequences. If evidence of baseline imbalance or potential carryover 
was observed, sequence-specific analyses were conducted. In this 
context, the GP group refers to participants who consumed the 
products in the order gurt followed by porridge, whereas the PG 
group refers to those who consumed them in the reverse order, 
porridge followed by gurt. If no sequence effect was found, we 
pooled data across sequences for a combined before-after analysis. 
For secondary cardiometabolic outcomes (HbA1C, FFAs, albumin, 
ferritin) measured only at weeks 3, 6, and 12, we treated week 3 as 
the baseline and pooled weeks 6 and 12 as post-intervention 
timepoints.

To assess changes in dietary intake, primary cardiometabolic 
measurements, GSRS, RAND-36, and GHQ-12 within each product 
and to compare these changes between the products, we used 
linear mixed models with the outcomes as the response variables, 
subject identifier as a random effect, and BMI, gender, phase, and 
product*time interaction as fixed effects. For weight and waist 
circumference, we excluded BMI from the model to avoid over
adjustment, as BMI is closely correlated with both outcomes and 
could obscure the direct effects of the interventions. For secondary 
cardiometabolic outcomes, contrasts to baseline and between 
product periods were analysed using linear mixed models with the 
outcomes as the response variables, subject identifier as a random 
effect, and BMI, gender, sequence, and product as fixed effects. 
Model assumptions were evaluated visually using quantile– 
quantile plots and histograms of residuals. Variables exhibiting 
substantial skewness were log10-transformed prior to analysis. In 
cases of mild deviations due to a few extreme observations, 
sensitivity analyses were conducted using robust linear mixed- 
effects models (R package robustlmm [52]). Statistical signifi
cances in changes within and between the products as well as 95 % 
confidential  intervals were tested with the emmeans function of 
the emmeans package. To assess potential changes in habitual food 
group consumption during the intervention, we used the Wilcoxon 
signed-rank test to compare FFQ scores between weeks 3 and 12. 
Sensitivity analyses were performed with linear mixed models to 
evaluate whether the use of antihypertensive and lipid-lowering 
medication influenced  the observed changes in systolic and dia
stolic blood pressure and lipid outcomes by adjusting the models 
with the medication status. Additionally, when a significant 
change in dietary intake was found, we conducted sensitivity an
alyses by adjusting for DF intake (for all clinical measurements) 
and for salt intake (for blood pressure outcomes) to assess their 
contribution to observed effects. Lastly, interaction analyses by 
gender were performed to assess potential differences in dietary 
intakes and cardiometabolic responses during the product periods. 
To ensure more sufficient  statistical power, females were 
compared with a combined group of males and participants 
identified  as other gender. Linear mixed model, as previously 
described, was used, with a product*time*gender interaction 
term.

Statistical analyses for assessing the differences in physico
chemical and nutritional properties of gurt and porridge were 
conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences 
(SPSS) (version number 30.0.0.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). 
Descriptive statistics were calculated for all data. However, no

3. Results

Baseline characteristics of participants are presented in Table 2. 
The study population consisted primarily of female participants 
with overweight or obesity accompanied by elevated cholesterol 
concentrations and blood pressure. Other cardiometabolic pa
rameters, including those used as inclusion criteria, were within 
normal ranges.

3.1. Compliance and dietary intake

The mean duration was 20.5 ± 2.4 days for the gurt period, 21.2 
± 2.5 days for the porridge period, and 20.4 ± 2.8 days for the 
washout period. Compliance with the study products and dietary 
modifications,  assessed both orally and through the food diaries 
and product records, remained mostly within an agreed range 
throughout the intervention, although some missing portions 
were reported. Three participants reported 1–3 days during both 
product periods, and six participants reported 2–5 days during the 
porridge period when they consumed only half of the assigned 
portion. Additionally, two participants reported one missed day of 
gurt consumption. The reported missing portions were primarily 
due to inconvenience or strong gastrointestinal symptoms.

The daily consumption of certain food groups changed during 
the intervention (Table 3). Expectedly, the consumption of whole 
grains (namely, rye, barley, and oat) and oat products increased 
during both gurt and porridge periods. Moreover, the consumption 
of dairy products decreased during both periods, especially in the 
form of fermented milk and other milk products. The consumption 
of cheese decreased during the porridge period, while no signifi
cant changes were observed during the gurt period. This change 
over time was significantly different between the study products. 
The changes in dairy product consumption reflect  the dietary 
modifications  made for these periods. In addition, the intake of 
total cereal as well as legumes and nuts increased during the gurt 
period, whereas no significant changes were observed during the 
porridge period. On the contrary, the consumption of cold cuts 
decreased during the porridge period. When the changes in the 
consumption frequency of selected food groups during the study 
were assessed within the whole study population with the FFQ, it 
was confirmed that apart from dairy and legume consumption, the 
habitual food group consumption remained somewhat unchanged 
during the intervention (Supplementary Table 1).

The intake of energy and some nutrients also changed during 
the product periods (Table 4). For energy and macronutrients, the 
intake of energy, protein, DF, dietary carbohydrates, and sugars 
increased during gurt period. During the porridge period, the 
intake of DF and sugars increased while the intake of saturated fat, 
cholesterol, and salt reduced. Protein, fibre,  carbohydrate, sugar, 
and salt intake changed differently between the products. Addi
tionally, there was a tendency of decreased saturated fat intake 
during the gurt period and decreased total fat intake during the 
porridge period. Estimated changes in energy, macronutrient, DF, 
and salt intake stratified by gender are reported in Supplementary 
Table 2.

For micronutrients, the iron intake increased during both the 
gurt and the porridge periods. This increase was significantly 
greater during the gurt period compared to the porridge period. 
Meanwhile, the intake of calcium, riboflavin, vitamin B12 and 
organic acids decreased during both the gurt and the porridge 
periods, with the reduction of calcium and riboflavin intakes being 
significantly greater during the porridge period compared to the 
gurt period. During the gurt period, the intake of magnesium and 
zinc increased while vitamin D intake decreased, while no signif
icant changes occurred during the porridge period. On the con
trary, during the porridge period, the intake of selenium and niacin 
decreased, whereas no significant changes were observed during 
the gurt period. Excluding vitamin D intake, these changes over 
time were significantly different between the products.

3.2. Cardiometabolic outcomes

showed no sequence effect, allowing all observations to be pooled 
for combined analyses. The intake of the study products resulted in 
minor yet statistically significant  changes in clinical measure

Tables 3 and 4). Following gurt consumption, non-HDL- and LDL- 
cholesterol concentrations were reduced, with a similar trend 
observed in total cholesterol levels. Similarly, systolic blood pres
sure tended to decrease during the gurt consumption. The 
porridge consumption was followed by lowered blood pressure 
and HbA1c concentration. However, observed changes did not 
significantly differ between the products. Ferritin concentrations 
were lower after both gurt and porridge intake. Due to the pres
ence of a few extreme observations in ferritin levels, a robust 
model was employed to assess the reliability of the results.

3.2.1. Sensitivity analyses
As increased DF intake during both product periods and 

decreased salt intake during the porridge period can explain the 
cardiometabolic outcomes, sensitivity analyses were performed. 
After adjusting for DF intake, all previously significant effects lost 
statistical significance  except systolic blood pressure during the 
porridge consumption (Supplementary Table 6A and 6B), indi
cating that DF intake may partly explain the observed health ef
fects. In contrary, the gurt period effect on HbA1c became 
significant,  and the porridge period effect further strengthened, 
suggesting this effect being likely independent of DF intake. 
Adjusting blood pressure measurements for salt intake amplified 
the observed effect on diastolic blood pressure during the porridge 
period (Supplementary Table 7). Adjusting for antihypertensive or 
lipid-lowering medications did not alter the observed effects 
(Supplementary Table 8).

ences between groups (Supplementary Tables 9 and 10). There was 
a trend toward a product*time*gender interaction for diastolic 
blood pressure (pproduct*time*gender = 0.063), indicating that 
changes over time differed between the products in a gender- 
specific manner. TG concentrations decreased during both prod

3.4. Differences in physicochemical and nutritional properties 
between the study products

Basic nutritional values of the study products per 1 dL are 
presented in Table 8. Depending on the assigned product portion, 
the BG intakes from the study products were 3.24–6.48 g/d from 
the gurt and 3.78–7.56 g/d from the porridge. The analysed phys
ical and nutritional properties of gurt and porridge are reported on 
dry weight basis in Table 9 (except for microbial cell density, SDS- 
Page, protein MW distribution, fatty acid composition, and 
microscopy).

3.4.1. Acidity, viscosity and microbial cell density
The gurt had significantly lower pH and higher TTA compared 

with the porridge. Additionally, the gurt was significantly  less 
viscous among the products. Expectedly, lactic acid was detected 
in the gurt and not in the porridge. LAB reached a cell density of 
107–108 CFU/g in the gurt. Contaminating microbes were below 
5 ×103 CFU/g for both products, indicating that products were safe 
to consume.

3.4.2. Sugars, oligosaccharides and total starch content
Sugars quantified by HPLC differed among the products. While 

the gurt contained glucose, sucrose, and maltose in order of 
increasing concentrations, only sucrose was detected in the 
porridge, showing an effect of fermentation on carbohydrates. 
Similarly, the total starch content was noticeably lower in the gurt 
compared to the porridge. Oligosaccharide profile showed several 
peaks in the gurt, whereas no peak was detected in the porridge 
(Supplementary Fig. 1).

3.4.3. Dietary fibre and β-glucan analyses
The total DF, total soluble DF, and soluble and insoluble high- 

MW DF contents were similar in the products. The low-MW DF 
content was low in both products, being higher in the gurt 
compared to the porridge.

The total BG content of the gurt was slightly lower than that of 
the porridge. The content remained the same throughout the 
batches prepared on different days of the gurt manufacturing 
process (Table 9, Supplementary Table 9). However, the aMw of 
extracted BG was significantly lower compared with the aMw of BG 
in the porridge (Table 4). The controlled reduction of the aMw

3.4.4. Protein analyses
The total protein content was similar among the products and 

did not differ among different batches (Supplementary Table 12). 
HPSEC analysis showed that fermentation did not induce a shift in 
the MW distribution of the proteins (Fig. 4). The extractability of 
protein in water was low for both samples, but also significantly 
lower in the gurt than in porridge. Likewise, it was significantly 
lower in the gurt compared to the porridge under alkaline condi
tions. The qualitative assessment with SDS-PAGE gel showed a 
visible fading of bands corresponding to high-MW proteins 
ranging 60 kDa–40 kDa in extracts from the gurt when compared 
to the bands of extracts from the porridge (Fig. 5).

3.4.5. Fatty acid analyses
Fatty acid composition was similar in gurt and porridge 

(Supplementary Table 13). Fatty acids detected were mainly 
polyunsaturated fatty acids, followed by monounsaturated fatty 
acids and saturated fatty acids. Omega-6 fatty acids constituted a 
similar portion of analysed fat in both products, whereas omega-3 
fatty acids represented a small portion of fat. Cis-linoleic acid 
(C18:2), cis-oleic acid (C18:1), and palmitic acid (C16:0) were the 
most abundant fatty acids in the two products.

3.4.6. Microscopy
Figure 6 shows clear visual differences in the spatial distribu

tion of fibre, protein and starch between gurt and porridge. In the 
gurt sample (Fig. 6A), DF (purple) appears as numerous small, 
discrete, and finely  dispersed structures distributed throughout 
the matrix. Protein (red) is observed mainly as distinct aggregates 
of varying size, often co-located with fibre-rich  regions, while 
starch (green) forms a discontinuous and fragmented phase with 

limited spatial dominance. In contrast, the porridge sample 
(Fig. 6B) exhibits a more continuous and homogeneous starch-rich 
matrix, with starch (green) forming a more continuous green 
background, suggesting partial starch gelatinisation. Protein (red) 
in porridge is more evenly distributed and appears less aggregated 
than in gurt, while DF (purple) is present as fewer but larger 
structures embedded within the starch matrix.

4. Discussion

In the present study, we assessed the effects of oat-based gurt 
on cardiometabolic health and perceived gastrointestinal and 
overall health compared with its non-fermented counterpart, 
porridge. We also investigated physicochemical and nutritional 
differences between the products resulting from the fermentation, 
including enzymatic treatment, to provide context for how these 
properties may relate to the observed health effects. To our best 
knowledge, this is the first study to compare the health effects of 
fermented and non-fermented oat-based products prepared from 
same ingredients in clinical trials. During the gurt consumption, 
non-HDL- and LDL-cholesterol, and ferritin concentrations 
decreased, with a minimal impact on blood pressure, perceived 
health, and gastrointestinal symptoms. Meanwhile, blood pres
sure, ferritin and HbA1c concentrations were lower after the 
porridge period. These effects, however, did not substantially differ 
between the product periods. When assessing insulin levels in 
sequence groups, i.e., in the order of the product periods, insulin 
levels altered noticeably in the group that started with the 
porridge. Fermentation induced changes in BG aMw, starch, and 
composition in the gurt, which may alongside dietary changes 
during the intervention explain the observed results.

As expected, integrating the study products into participants’ 
habitual diet altered both food and nutrient intake. To attenuate 
gastrointestinal symptoms associated with increased fibre and to 
moderate protein intake provided by the study products, partici
pants were instructed to reduce other cereal-based products, 
primarily porridge and bread, and dairy products. The modest 
increase in whole grain and oat intake, along with reduced dairy 
consumption, reflected these instructions. Similarly, increased 
legume intake was partly explained by the faba bean protein in the 
study products. Increased sugar intake during the product periods 
was due to the higher consumption of berry soup and, during the 
gurt consumption, to its higher mono- and disaccharide content 
following enzymatic processing. Other macro- and micronutrient 
intakes mirrored similar food consumption patterns. Importantly, 
the intakes remained within average intake requirements during 
the intervention periods [53]. Some nutrient intakes differed be
tween the gurt and porridge periods, indicating variations in di
etary habits. Particularly, fibre  and protein intakes were higher 
during the gurt consumption, reflecting  higher consumption of 
cereals and a smaller reduction in dairy consumption. Participants 
also reported missing portions of the study products, more 
frequently during the porridge consumption. However, most still 
consumed at least half of the assigned portions each day, ensuring 
consistent intake throughout the intervention.

The most noticeable health effect was the reduced non-HDL- 
cholesterol, particularly LDL-cholesterol, during gurt consump
tion. Although evidence on fermented oat products and choles
terol from clinical trials is still scarce, existing studies generally 
support our findings  [54,55]. These findings  are most probably 
explained by oat BG, which is well-documented to lower choles
terol levels, particularly in individuals with hypercholesterolemia 
[56,57]. Indeed, BG has an authorised health claim in the EU for 
reducing LDL-cholesterol levels with a daily intake of 3 g [9], a 
threshold that was exceeded through the consumption of our

confirmed by sensitivity analyses, reinforcing the beneficial role of 
oat fibre.  Although dietary fat intake also affects cholesterol 
metabolism and our products contained mainly polyunsaturated 
fats that could contribute to lower LDL-cholesterol concentrations 
[58], changes in dietary fat intake were modest and did not differ 
between the product periods. This supports the conclusion that 
increased intake of fibre, and BG in particular, had a prominent role 

in the observed effects. Additionally, interaction analyses by 
gender showed more pronounced reductions in lipid markers 
among others than females, particularly for TG, during gurt con
sumption. This pattern was accompanied by a greater increase in 
dietary fibre intake, further supporting its role in lipid metabolism.

Besides quantity, the physicochemical properties of BG can 
influence  its efficacy  and mechanisms of action. In the gurt, 
β-glucanase treatment led to the expected reductions in aMw, 
comparable to that observed in laboratory-scale gurt [28], and 
resulting in lower values than in the porridge. The cholesterol- 
lowering effects of BG are mainly attributed to its ability to in
crease digesta viscosity [59], which is determined by high BG MW 
and concentration [60,61]. While previous studies have reported 
greater effects of higher MW BG on bile acid-binding capacity [62] 
and LDL-cholesterol level reduction [59,63] compared with lower 
MW counterparts, the cholesterol-lowering effect remained in the 
gurt despite the considerably reduced BG MW. Low-MW BF has 
been demonstrated to form a gel in gastrointestinal conditions 
[64], which might mimic the behaviour of high-MW counterpart. 
In vitro studies also show that low-MW BG is preferably fermented 
by gut microbiota, leading to higher production of SCFAs [61,65], 
which has been linked to improved lipid profiles in animal models 
[66,67]. In addition to MW, the physiological action of BG may 
involve complex interactions with food matrix [68]. The gurt in our 
study was liquid, whereas porridge was semi-solid. A meta- 
analysis of BG enrichment indicates that BG exerts stronger ef
fects when incorporated into liquids compared to solid foods [56], 
which may partly explain the observed outcomes.

In addition to its effects on cholesterol metabolism, this study 
showed modest reductions in blood pressure and glucose meta
bolism. Nevertheless, the clinical significance  remains limited, 
given relatively short intervention duration. Findings on blood 
pressure are consistent with previous findings with oat and BG in 
individuals with obesity [69] or hypertension [70]. The reductions 
were independent of salt intake and antihypertensive medication 
use. Similarly, the small decrease in HbA1c levels after porridge 
consumption aligns with previous studies reporting no consider
able effects of oats on HbA1c or fasting glucose in individuals 
without diabetes [71–73]. Notably, sensitivity analyses indicated 
that fibre quantity partly explained the effect on blood pressure

of fermentation, may have moderated glucose responses through 
delayed gastric emptying and carbohydrate absorption [74–76]. 
Additionally, lower dietary carbohydrate and sugar intake during 
porridge consumption, compared with during gurt consumption, 
may have further influenced blood glucose exposure.

Unlike other glucose metabolism markers, or any other car
diometabolic factors, fasting insulin showed distinct patterns 
across the sequence groups. In the PG group, gurt consumption 
significantly  reduced fasting insulin levels that had increased 
during the washout period, suggesting improved insulin sensi
tivity or reduced chronic hyperglycaemia during this period. This 
contrasts with previous studies in individuals without diabetes, 
which generally report no effect of oat consumption on fasting 
insulin [71,73]. Lower-MW BG may support insulin sensitivity and 
reduce postprandial insulin demand through enhanced GLP-1 
secretion [77], partly regulated by SCFAs [78–80]. Regarding 
other factors, high fasting insulin is often associated with diets 
high in carbohydrates and glycaemic load [81], greater waist 
circumference [82], and inflammation [83]. However, in the pre
sent study, fibre and sugar intakes decreased during the washout 
but increased during both gurt and porridge consumption, sug
gesting that major dietary indicators are unlikely to explain the 
observed variation. Likewise, waist circumference and hs-CRP 
levels decreased or remained stable during the washout period 
and showed no change during gurt consumption. High variability 
among participants was also evident, and fasting insulin variations 
during the washout might reflect normal physiological variability 
rather than direct intervention effects. Furthermore, no equivalent 
decrease in insulin was observed in the GP group, where baseline 
insulin levels were lower. Whether oat-based gurt could 
contribute to normalising elevated insulin levels, as seen in our 
study, needs further studies.

Notably, ferritin levels were lower after consumptions of both 
products. Baseline ferritin levels were well within normal ranges, 
and the reduction did not compromise health of participants. 
Additionally, the greater reduction was related to higher baseline 
concentrations, as suggested by the gender interaction analysis 
and robust model. These finding  align with previous evidence 
related to cereal intake and lower ferritin, including observational 
studies on whole grains [84,85] and a clinical trial with fibre-rich 
bread in healthy women [86]. The decrease occurred despite 
increased dietary iron intake, which in a previous study prevented 
ferritin reductions after a 12-week plant protein intervention [87]. 

One potential explanation is iron form and absorption. Iron in 
plant sources is mainly non-haem iron, which has lower 
bioavailability compared to haem iron from animal sources [88]. 
Increased fibre  intake also partly explains our findings,  as indi
cated with the sensitivity analysis. Fibre and antinutrients asso
ciated with it, particularly phytate, can interfere with the iron 
absorption in the small intestine by binding to iron and forming 
insoluble complexes [89]. Although fermented oat gruel has 
improved non-haem iron absorption in a live strain-specific 
manner [90], our gurt had a similar effect on ferritin levels 
compared with porridge. This may suggest limited benefit  from 
fermentation with L. delbrueckii spp. bulgaricus and S. thermophilus 
in this matter. Additionally, changes in other dietary factors, 
including reduced calcium and increased or stable intake of 
vitamin C, meat, and poultry, may have been insufficient to sup
port optimal non-haem iron absorption. Despite the greater 
decrease observed among individuals with higher baseline levels, 
this finding highlights the importance of ensuring adequate iron 
intake and improving iron bioavailability from fibre-rich  cereal- 
based foods particularly among vulnerable populations.

Lastly, perceived gastrointestinal symptoms assessed with the 
GSRS largely remained unchanged during the intervention, sug
gesting overall good tolerance of both study products and suc
cessful moderation of fibre  intake. That said, some participants 
reported missing portions during the porridge consumption due to 
the gastrointestinal symptoms. This is reflected in a slight increase 
in abdominal pain was observed during porridge consumption. 
Stronger symptoms may be partly explained by the FODMAPs 
naturally present in faba bean, which can be reduced through 
fermentation [22]. Additionally, while previous studies have re
ported positive [71] or neutral [91] effects of oat consumption, 
increased fibre  intake can cause discomfort [92], with the fibre 
characteristics contributing to the magnitude. Wolever et al. 
(2021) [93] reported increased gastrointestinal symptoms, 
including abdominal pain with high-MW BG consumption, 
although no significant differences between different BG MW have 
been observed [62]. These symptoms tended to return toward 
baseline over time, indicating improved tolerance. This pattern 
may also have occurred in our study, possibly faster with gurt 
consumption.

This study has several strengths, but also some limitations. The 
crossover design used in our intervention minimises interindi
vidual variability by focusing on within-subject comparisons 
rather than between-subject differences. Despite this, the final 
sample size may still have been insufficient  to detect modest 
health effects, as it did not meet the strictest criteria of the power

Nevertheless, differences in effect magnitude may reflect  varia

responses. Primary cardiometabolic markers were measured at 
all four timepoints, enabling assessment of sequence effects and 
the efficacy of washout period. However, it is important to note 
that we only had three timepoints for secondary markers, 
including HbA1c and ferritin, limiting our ability to fully evaluate

observed health outcomes, we were able to assess food group

associated with metabolic health [95,96]. Lastly, some analyses of 
the study products, including DF, total starch content, total fat, and 
fatty acid composition, were performed by external accredited 
laboratories.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, consuming a fermented, oat-based gurt as a part 
of habitual diet improves cholesterol metabolism, likely due to 
increased oat fibre  intake rather than fermentation as such. In 
addition, both gurt and porridge appear to exert minor beneficial 
effects on blood pressure and glucose metabolism through distinct 
mechanisms, with differences potentially explained by 
processing-induced variations in physicochemical and nutritional 
properties. Finally, increased consumption of oat-based products 
and DF, regardless of processing, appears to reduce ferritin con
centrations, particularly among individuals with higher baseline 
levels.
