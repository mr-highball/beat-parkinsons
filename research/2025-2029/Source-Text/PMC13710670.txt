ABSTRACT

Aging, neuroinflammation and metabolic dysfunction are tightly intertwined in Alzheimer's disease (AD), but how transient pathological signals become persistent cellular states remains poorly understood. Histone post‐translational modifications (PTMs) provide a dynamic interface between extracellular stimuli, cellular metabolism and chromatin state. This Review examines how canonical histone modifications and emerging metabolite‐derived acylations regulate inflammatory and stress‐responsive programmes across microglia, astrocytes and neurons during aging and AD. We discuss how amyloid‐β, tau pathology, metabolic dysfunction and innate immune signaling converge on histone‐modifying machinery, and how reciprocal interactions between metabolism and chromatin may stabilize disease‐associated cell states. Particular attention is given to cellular senescence and neuroimmune memory, while emphasizing the context dependence of individual histone marks across cell types, genomic regions and disease stages. We further evaluate evidence for causality using three criteria: perturbation, persistence and reversibility. This framework distinguishes chromatin states that accompany ongoing pathology from those capable of sustaining pathological cell states beyond their initiating signals. Finally, we consider therapeutic strategies targeting histone‐modifying enzymes, chromatin readers and metabolic inputs to restore chromatin plasticity and promote resolution of chronic neuroinflammation.

This review examines how histone modifications integrate aging, metabolic dysfunction and neuroinflammation to shape persistent, cell‐type‐specific chromatin states in Alzheimer's disease. By distinguishing causal regulation from association through perturbation, persistence and reversibility, it highlights metabolic–epigenetic feedback loops and therapeutic opportunities for restoring chromatin plasticity.

1 Introduction

Aging is the strongest risk factor for Alzheimer's disease (AD), in which neurodegeneration emerges from the convergence of proteotoxic stress, immune dysregulation, and metabolic dysfunction (Butovsky et al. 2026; Heneka et al. 2025). Neuroinflammation is now recognized as an active component of AD pathogenesis rather than simply a secondary response to amyloid‐β (Aβ) and tau pathology. During brain aging and AD, microglia, astrocytes, and neurons undergo persistent changes in inflammatory, stress‐responsive, and metabolic programmes, and senescence‐associated states have been implicated in the stabilization of these phenotypes (Hu et al. 2025; Wendeln et al. 2018). Yet continued exposure to pathological stimuli alone does not fully explain why inflammatory and stress‐response programmes can become persistent and maladaptive. A fundamental question is therefore how transient or recurrent pathological signals are converted into durable cellular states.

Chromatin regulation provides a potential mechanism for this persistence. Human and experimental studies have identified extensive, cell‐type‐specific epigenomic remodeling in AD, including alterations in chromatin accessibility, enhancer activity and transcriptional regulation (Ardura‐Fabregat et al. 2025; Green et al. 2024; Liu, Zhang, et al. 2025; Murphy, Hu, et al. 2025; Nativio et al. 2020). Recent single‐cell, multiregion profiling of postmortem AD brains has further revealed widespread changes in regulatory chromatin landscapes across neural and glial populations, linking epigenomic instability to cellular state transitions and disease‐associated phenotypes (Liu, Zhang, et al. 2025). Histone post‐translational modifications (PTMs) are central components of this regulatory system. Acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation and ubiquitination shape nucleosome organization, enhancer and promoter activity, and transcription‐factor recruitment, whereas emerging metabolite‐derived modifications, including lactylation, crotonylation and succinylation, provide additional routes through which cellular physiology can influence chromatin state (Chatila et al. 2024; Datta et al. 2018). Rather than functioning as isolated molecular marks, these modifications act within combinatorial and context‐dependent chromatin environments that can influence the magnitude, duration and reversibility of inflammatory and stress‐responsive transcription. The concept of innate immune memory provides a broader precedent for this principle, showing that prior inflammatory experience can establish durable transcriptional and chromatin states that alter subsequent cellular responses (Wendeln et al. 2018; Yücel and Gladyshev 2026).

Histone modifications are also intimately coupled to cellular metabolism. Many histone‐modifying enzymes require metabolic substrates or cofactors, including acetyl‐CoA, S‐adenosylmethionine, NAD+ and α‐ketoglutarate, thereby linking nutrient availability, mitochondrial function and redox state to chromatin regulation (Yücel and Gladyshev 2026). Conversely, histone modifications can reinforce metabolic and inflammatory programmes, creating reciprocal feedback between cellular metabolism and gene regulation. This coupling is particularly relevant to aging and AD, in which altered glucose utilization, mitochondrial dysfunction and redox imbalance reshape the metabolic environment in which chromatin states are established and maintained. Metabolite‐derived acylations provide illustrative examples of this interface, linking changes in cellular metabolites to transcriptional regulation. Histone lactylation, for example, has been associated with metabolic and inflammatory reprogramming in AD‐related microglia, including glycolysis‐associated H4K12 lactylation and senescence‐associated H3K18 lactylation (Cirovic et al. 2020; Noh et al. 2026; Novakovic et al. 2016). However, these observations should not be interpreted as evidence that any single histone modification is intrinsically pathogenic or protective. The consequences of a given PTM depend on its genomic location, interacting chromatin machinery, cellular context, metabolic state and disease stage.

These considerations raise a broader mechanistic question: which histone modifications actively determine pathological cell states rather than simply reflecting them? Equally important, to what extent do AD‐associated chromatin states persist independently of the initiating stimulus, and can they be experimentally reversed? These questions are difficult to resolve because most studies identify altered chromatin marks under conditions of ongoing pathology, providing evidence of association but not necessarily of persistence. We therefore distinguish three experimentally separable properties of a candidate pathological chromatin state: perturbation, whether altering the modification changes cellular or disease‐relevant phenotypes; persistence, whether the state is maintained beyond the initiating signal; and reversibility, whether an established state can be reset by targeted intervention. This framework helps distinguish transient chromatin responses from mechanisms capable of stabilizing chronic neuroinflammation.

Here, we examine histone PTMs as a dynamic chromatin interface connecting aging, neuroinflammation and metabolism in AD (Figure 1) (Nativio et al. 2020; Qin et al. 2024; Zheng et al. 2019). We first consider how canonical histone modifications and emerging metabolite‐derived modifications shape chromatin states associated with cellular senescence and inflammatory activation. We then examine how these mechanisms operate across microglia, astrocytes and neurons and how Aβ, tau, innate immune signaling and metabolic dysfunction converge on histone‐modifying pathways. Finally, we assess the evidence for causality, persistence and reversibility and consider therapeutic strategies targeting histone‐modifying enzymes, chromatin readers and the metabolic inputs that sustain pathological chromatin states. Rather than assigning fixed functions to individual histone marks, we emphasize the context dependence and combinatorial nature of histone modification and propose that restoring appropriate chromatin plasticity may represent a route toward resolving persistent neuroinflammatory states in AD.

2 Histone PTMs as a Dynamic Chromatin System

Histone post‐translational modifications (PTMs) are one layer of chromatin regulation, operating alongside DNA methylation, histone variants, nucleosome remodeling, transcription‐factor binding and three‐dimensional genome organization (Duan et al. 2024; Wang, Liang, et al. 2026; Yao et al. 2024). They can influence histone–DNA interactions, nucleosome dynamics and the recruitment or exclusion of chromatin‐associated proteins. Histone PTMs should therefore not be treated as universal switches for transcriptional activation or repression. Their effects depend on the modified residue, modification state, abundance, genomic location, timing and cellular context (Huang et al. 2024; Lin et al. 2025; Lu et al. 2026; Park et al. 2024; Policarpi et al. 2024; Wu, Qu, and Liu 2024; Yamagishi et al. 2024; Zhou and Cole 2025). This framework provides the basis for examining how particular PTMs change in aging and disease in the following chapters.

2.1 The Landscape of Histone PTMs

The nucleosome, composed of an octamer containing two copies each of H2A, H2B, H3, and H4 wrapped by approximately 147 base pairs of DNA, is the fundamental structural unit of chromatin. Linker histone H1 contributes to nucleosome spacing and higher‐order chromatin organization. Histone tails and residues within the histone core can undergo a diverse range of enzymatically regulated PTMs, including acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation, ubiquitination, SUMOylation, O‐GlcNAcylation, citrullination, and ADP‐ribosylation, together with an expanding group of metabolite‐derived lysine acylations such as lactylation, crotonylation, and succinylation (Duan et al. 2024; Li, Qiu, et al. 2025; Wang, Liang, et al. 2026; Yao et al. 2024). These modifications can alter nucleosome properties or provide binding sites for regulatory proteins, influencing chromatin accessibility, transcription, DNA repair, and genome stability (Figure 2; Table 1).

The establishment, removal and recognition of histone PTMs are commonly described in terms of three functional classes of proteins: writers, which install modifications; erasers, which remove them; and readers, which recognize modified residues and help translate them into downstream chromatin functions (Narita et al. 2019). These categories are not strictly discrete. Many chromatin‐modifying enzymes also act on non‐histone substrates, and their activity, localization and substrate engagement can be regulated by signaling pathways, protein stability and cellular state (Park et al. 2024; Wu, Qu, and Liu 2024; Yamagishi et al. 2024; Zhou and Cole 2025). Histone PTMs are therefore embedded within signaling networks rather than operating as autonomous molecular switches.

Different classes of PTMs can have distinct but overlapping effects. Acetylation is often associated with transcriptionally permissive chromatin, in part through changes in nucleosome interactions and the recruitment of acetyl‐lysine readers. Methylation can be associated with either active or repressed chromatin, depending on the modified residue and the degree of methylation (Miziak et al. 2024; Moraitis and Piperi 2025). Phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and SUMOylation further connect chromatin to signal‐responsive transcription, DNA damage responses, and genome maintenance (Ai et al. 2024; Ciapponi et al. 2024; Ge et al. 2023; Leonen et al. 2021; Mattiroli and Penengo 2021; Onishi et al. 2024; Oss‐Ronen et al. 2022; Ryu and Hochstrasser 2021). O‐GlcNAcylation and ADP‐ribosylation provide additional links between nutrient sensing, cellular stress, and chromatin regulation, whereas citrullination can modify histone charge and alter chromatin structure and transcriptional regulation (Anagho et al. 2024; Deng et al. 2026; García Fernández et al. 2025; Hananya et al. 2021; Longarini et al. 2023; Love et al. 2025; Özdemir et al. 2024; Wu, Li, et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2026). Collectively, these modifications expand the regulatory repertoire of the nucleosome, but their biological outputs cannot be inferred from modification abundance alone.

Metabolite‐derived acylations represent a particularly important extension of this regulatory repertoire because they provide a biochemical route through which cellular metabolism can influence chromatin. Their formation reflects both the availability of metabolic intermediates and the activities of enzymes that install, remove or interpret the corresponding acyl groups (Han et al. 2025; Moreno‐Yruela et al. 2022; Yao et al. 2024). Mechanistic studies of histone lactylation have progressively expanded the underlying regulatory framework, including lactyltransferase activities, delactylases and candidate readers, while also clarifying that enzymatic and alternative lactylation pathways may coexist (Moreno‐Yruela et al. 2022; Niu et al. 2024; Sheng et al. 2026; Zhai et al. 2024; Zhang, Zhou, Zhang, et al. 2024). Histone lactylation is therefore best considered an emerging component of metabolic–chromatin coupling rather than an isolated lactate‐responsive switch. Similar principles apply to crotonylation and succinylation, which connect chromatin regulation to intermediary metabolic states and the availability of crotonyl‐CoA or succinyl‐CoA (Gao et al. 2024; Han et al. 2025; Li, Wang, et al. 2025; Weyh et al. 2024; Xie et al. 2024; Zhu et al. 2025; Zorro Shahidian et al. 2021). Importantly, metabolite abundance alone does not determine the genomic distribution or transcriptional consequence of a histone acylation; enzymatic targeting, chromatin context and competing modifications are also required to specify its functional output.

2.2 From Individual Marks to Chromatin States

The function of a histone PTM cannot be inferred from its presence at a single residue alone. Multiple modifications may coexist within the same nucleosome or regulatory region and influence one another through cis and trans crosstalk (Jiang et al. 2026; Kawaf et al. 2024; Li, Ren, et al. 2022). Such interactions can affect the installation, removal, or recognition of neighboring modifications, producing combinatorial chromatin states. Proteomic and mechanistic studies increasingly support this view, showing that histone modifications often occur in combinations rather than as independent events (Jiang et al. 2026; Kawaf et al. 2024; Li, Ren, et al. 2022).

One form of crosstalk occurs when different modifications compete for the same residue. H3K27 acetylation and H3K27 methylation, for example, are mutually exclusive at that lysine. H3K27ac is commonly associated with active regulatory elements, whereas H3K27me3 is associated with Polycomb‐mediated repression (Pasini et al. 2010; Tie et al. 2009). Crosstalk can also occur between adjacent residues. H3S28 phosphorylation, for instance, can influence the status and interpretation of neighboring H3K27 modifications, including in contexts where Polycomb‐associated repression is altered (Chen, Yue, et al. 2026; Li, Ding, et al. 2026; Sun et al. 2024). These examples illustrate how the effect of a modification may depend on the modification state of neighboring residues, rather than on a single mark considered in isolation.

The consequences of PTM crosstalk are also shaped by the regulatory proteins present at a genomic locus. At promoters and enhancers, combinations of histone modifications act alongside transcription factors and chromatin remodelers to influence transcriptional potential (Miziak et al. 2024; Moraitis and Piperi 2025). The same PTM may therefore be associated with different outcomes depending on its location, the surrounding chromatin state, and the proteins available to interpret it.

Cell identity and cell state add further layers of context. Distinct transcription‐factor repertoires, enhancer landscapes, and patterns of chromatin accessibility influence both where modifying enzymes act and how a given modification is interpreted (Hernandez et al. 2023; Li, Ren, et al. 2022). Thus, a histone PTM need not have the same consequence in every cell type or at every stage of a biological process.

Histone PTMs are best viewed as components of dynamic chromatin states rather than as isolated marks. Their effects emerge from the interplay between modification type, genomic location, temporal dynamics, regulatory machinery, and cellular context. This perspective shifts the question from whether a mark is simply increased or decreased to how combinations of PTMs contribute to the establishment, maintenance, or resolution of a cellular state. The next chapter considers how these principles apply to histone modifications in aging.

3 Histone and Chromatin Remodeling During Cellular Senescence: A Mechanistic Bridge to Chronic Neuroinflammation

Cellular senescence is a heterogeneous, stress‐responsive state defined by durable changes in cell‐cycle control, chromatin organization, transcription and metabolism, often accompanied by a senescence‐associated secretory phenotype (SASP) (Chandra et al. 2012; Narita et al. 2003; Sadaie et al. 2013; Shah et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2005). Its chromatin component is not a fixed histone code. Classical studies established senescence‐associated heterochromatin foci (SAHF) and retinoblastoma‐dependent repression of E2F targets, whereas genome‐wide analyses showed that repressive marks such as H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 are redistributed rather than uniformly gained or lost (Chandra et al. 2012; Narita et al. 2003; Zhang et al. 2005). The central issue is therefore not whether senescence adds a stereotyped histone signature, but how cells reorganize chromatin so that proliferation, stress responses and inflammatory transcription become durably reweighted.

This distinction is particularly important in AD, in which biological aging, metabolic dysfunction, proteostasis failure and chronic neuroinflammation coexist. A useful framework is to treat senescence as one route to chromatin persistence, rather than as the obligatory identity of every affected neural cell. In this view, the relevant unit is a stress‐stabilized chromatin state whose persistence depends on the interaction of nuclear architecture, enhancer activity, histone‐modification turnover and metabolic substrate supply (Figure 3, Table 2).

3.1 Establishment of Senescence‐Associated Chromatin States

The establishment of senescence involves large‐scale reorganization of chromatin architecture rather than a uniform gain or loss of individual histone modifications. In classical oncogene‐induced senescence, SAHF formation and retinoblastoma‐dependent chromatin remodeling contribute to repression of E2F target genes, whereas macroH2A‐containing chromatin and HIRA‐dependent histone deposition further support senescence‐associated heterochromatin domains (Narita et al. 2003; Zhang et al. 2005). Genome‐wide analyses nevertheless show that H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 can be redistributed into different nuclear compartments without a simple one‐to‐one relationship between the abundance of a repressive mark and physical compaction (Chandra et al. 2012). Senescent chromatin is therefore better understood as a spatially reorganized regulatory landscape than as a globally more repressive genome.

Loss of nuclear‐lamina integrity adds a structural layer to this transition. Lamin B1 depletion is accompanied by redistribution of heterochromatic domains, altered chromatin accessibility and region‐specific changes in histone modifications (Sadaie et al. 2013; Shah et al. 2013). The nucleosomal substrate itself also changes: senescent cells can extrude chromatin fragments that are processed through lysosomal pathways, while persistent telomere damage suppresses histone biosynthesis and destabilizes telomeric chromatin (Ivanov et al. 2013; O'Sullivan et al. 2010). These observations highlight an important interpretative point for AD studies: a change in bulk histone abundance can reflect altered histone supply or nuclear organization rather than a locus‐specific change in chromatin function.

Specific histone combinations further demonstrate the dependence of senescent chromatin on genomic context. In replicative senescence, loss of promoter‐associated H4R3me2s and redistribution of H3K79me3 across gene bodies accompany transcriptional changes; perturbation of DOT1L, PRMT1, PRMT5, or JMJD6 alters cellular lifespan and selected senescence‐associated gene responses (Suter et al. 2026). Thus, activating and repressive histone states can change simultaneously, and the same modification may have different consequences depending on the genomic compartment in which it is deposited.

Polycomb‐mediated repression provides another example. EZH2, the catalytic component of PRC2, deposits H3K27me3 and helps maintain transcriptionally repressed chromatin; during senescence, reduced EZH2 activity can promote locus‐specific H3K27me3 loss and induction of senescence‐associated genes (Ito et al. 2018). In other senescence‐associated contexts, EZH2 inhibition can instead reshape inflammatory secretory programmes (Chibaya et al. 2023). NOTCH‐mediated communication between senescent and neighboring cells can further alter heterochromatin organization and secretory output (Parry et al. 2018). In macrophages and microglia, Ezh2‐dependent H3K27me3 repression of Socs3 supports TLR‐driven inflammatory transcription (Zhang et al. 2018). The implication is that H3K27me3 loss or gain should never be interpreted in isolation from cell type, stimulus, and locus.

Histone variants, chromatin remodellers and architectural proteins add further layers. H2A.J accumulates during senescence and aging and promotes inflammatory gene expression, whereas HMGB2 maintains accessibility at selected SASP loci by limiting encroachment of repressive chromatin (Aird et al. 2016; Contrepois et al. 2017). ATP‐dependent chromatin remodeling also contributes to the establishment of senescence: loss of the SWI/SNF‐associated subunit ARID1A attenuates oncogene‐induced senescence (Liu et al. 2021). Together, these findings indicate that senescence is built through coordinated changes in histone modifications, histone supply, histone variants, nucleosome organization and chromatin‐remodeling activity rather than through a universal epigenetic endpoint.

3.2 Chromatin Control of the SASP

The SASP is a major route by which senescent cells alter their environment, but its composition varies with cell identity, inducing stimulus and duration (Chien et al. 2011). Persistent cytokine and chemokine production is therefore not simply the output of a single transcription factor. Instead, SASP expression emerges from the interaction between inflammatory signaling and a chromatin state that determines enhancer accessibility, transcriptional amplitude, and the capacity of inflammatory loci to remain active over time.

NF‐κB is a central regulator of many SASP components, but sustained SASP transcription also depends on enhancer remodeling. H3K27ac accumulation at inflammatory enhancers and BRD4 recruitment reinforce transcription, and BRD4 inhibition suppresses SASP expression and paracrine signaling (Chien et al. 2011; Tasdemir et al. 2016). Additional chromatin changes reinforce this state: HDAC4 degradation releases an AP‐1–p300 programme at selected enhancers and super‐enhancers, whereas MLL1 contributes to SASP gene expression (Capell et al. 2016; Di Giorgio et al. 2021). The combined evidence places enhancer activity, rather than promoter occupancy alone, at the centre of persistent inflammatory transcription.

Chromatin architecture can determine whether inflammatory loci remain accessible. HMGB2 selectively occupies SASP‐associated regions and limits repressive chromatin spreading, whereas H2A.J accumulation promotes inflammatory transcription (Aird et al. 2016; Contrepois et al. 2017). MacroH2A1 also participates in paracrine senescence and SASP regulation (Chen et al. 2015). Conversely, H3K27me3 can constrain inflammatory transcription in some contexts; in macrophages and microglia, Ezh2‐dependent H3K27me3 represses Socs3 and sustains TLR‐driven inflammatory programmes (Zhang et al. 2018). The same modification can therefore function as a brake or as part of an inflammatory state depending on the transcriptional network in which it is embedded.

Metabolite‐derived histone modifications provide a further route to persistent secretory states. Histone lactylation was first linked to glycolytic metabolism in activated macrophages, and H3K18 lactylation in senescent microglia has subsequently been associated with enhanced NF‐κB signaling and increased IL‐6 and IL‐8 production in aging‐ and AD‐related models (Wei et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2019). Senescent cells can also generate cytoplasmic chromatin fragments after mitochondrial dysfunction; in experimental senescence, low‐dose histone deacetylase inhibition attenuates chromatin‐fragment formation and the SASP (Vizioli et al. 2020). These findings suggest that the SASP is stabilized by a network of chromatin and innate‐immune processes rather than by one histone event.

3.3 Metabolic Inputs Into the Senescence‐Associated Chromatin State

Senescence and metabolism are reciprocally coupled. Chromatin‐modifying enzymes depend on metabolic substrates, cofactors, and redox conditions, whereas chromatin states regulate metabolic gene programmes (Malone et al. 2026). Importantly, metabolite abundance measured in the whole cell is not equivalent to the concentration experienced by a nuclear enzyme. Subcellular compartmentalization, local production, and consumption, enzyme recruitment, and modification turnover all determine whether a metabolic perturbation reaches a particular chromatin substrate.

Mitochondrial carbon metabolism illustrates this principle. In senescent cells, a mitochondrial pyruvate‐citrate‐acetyl‐CoA pathway supports histone acetylation, including H3K27ac at SASP‐associated loci; inhibition of this pathway reduces chromatin accessibility and inflammatory gene expression (Martini et al. 2026). In post‐mitotic neurons, ACSS2 can couple acetate metabolism to local acetyl‐CoA production, histone acetylation and memory‐associated transcription (Mews et al. 2017). NAD+‐dependent sirtuin activity provides a complementary route: reduced SIRT1 abundance or activity is associated with enhanced acetylation at selected inflammatory loci and a stronger SASP, while microglial SIRT1 can also restrain NF‐κB through deacetylation of RelA/p65 (Chen et al. 2005; Hayakawa et al. 2015; Xu et al. 2020). The latter effects involve non‐histone substrates as well as histones and should not be conflated.

Glycolysis provides another route through lactate. In AD‐associated microglia, H4K12 lactylation participates in a positive‐feedback circuit involving PKM2 and glycolytic gene expression, whereas H3K18 lactylation is associated with senescence‐associated inflammatory transcription (Pan et al. 2022; Wei et al. 2023). Other metabolic pathways provide additional inputs: crotonyl‐CoA supports histone crotonylation, succinyl‐CoA supports histone succinylation, β‐hydroxybutyrate inhibits selected histone deacetylases and can support histone β‐hydroxybutyrylation, and α‐ketoglutarate regulates 2‐oxoglutarate‐dependent dioxygenase activity (Akakura and Tabibzadeh 2026; Sabari et al. 2015; Shimazu et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2017; Xie et al. 2016). These pathways illustrate the biochemical breadth of metabolic–chromatin coupling without implying that any single metabolite dictates a uniform chromatin state.

Metabolic effects on chromatin are also evident outside mammalian senescence. LKRSDH‐dependent changes in H3R17me2 and H3K27me3 at insulin‐like peptide loci contribute to age‐related circadian changes in Drosophila, illustrating how altered intermediary metabolism can reshape defined histone states at aging‐responsive loci (Lv et al. 2024). Redox metabolism can further modify the activity of chromatin enzymes and the cellular response to DNA damage (Balamurli et al. 2024). Histone‐based aging clocks provide a complementary systems‐level approach for estimating biological age, although their cell‐type‐specific utility in AD remains to be established Niu et al. (2026).

The relationship is bidirectional. Mitochondrial dysfunction, altered carbon flux and changes in NAD+, acyl‐CoA or lactate availability can change the biochemical environment of chromatin enzymes, while chromatin‐dependent transcription can feed back to metabolic programmes (Martini et al. 2026; Wang, Zhu, et al. 2026). Cytoplasmic chromatin fragments (CCFs) provide a further bridge between metabolic stress and innate immunity: CCFs can activate cGAS–STING and promote inflammatory signaling in senescence, while a recent Nature Aging study showed that glucose limitation or metformin suppresses CCF formation through AMPK‐dependent control of the ESCRT‐associated protein ALIX, reducing cGAS‐mediated inflammation in aged mice (Dou et al. 2017; Kumazawa et al. 2026). In AD, where mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired energy metabolism and neuroinflammation coexist, this coupling provides a plausible route for stabilizing stress responses (Chaubey et al. 2026; Wang, Zhu, et al. 2026). Whether the same circuitry operates in specific CNS cell types and AD stages remains an open question.

3.4 Senescence‐Associated Chromatin States in the Aging and AD Brain

Evidence for senescence‐associated states in the CNS has increased substantially, but the evidence is strongest in glial compartments and should not be generalized to every neural cell. In human AD brain, single‐nucleus transcriptomic and imaging analyses identified the most complete canonical senescence programme in microglia within the cell types examined, with more partial senescence‐associated changes in oligodendroglia and astrocytes and little evidence for a canonical programme in neurons (Fancy et al. 2024). Experimental studies independently identified senescent TREM2‐expressing microglia in aging and AD models and a senescence‐like microglial subset within tau‐associated disease‐associated microglia (Ng et al. 2023; Rachmian et al. 2024). By contrast, evidence for a canonical senescence programme in post‐mitotic neurons remains limited (Wu, Sun, and Chow 2024). The field therefore has stronger support for senescence‐associated glial states than for a universal neuronal senescence state.

Microglia provide the clearest connection between senescence‐associated states and chronic neuroinflammation. Human microglial models exposed to mitochondrial proteotoxic stress show metabolic rewiring, S‐adenosylmethionine depletion and a senescent phenotype with altered inflammatory communication (Perez J et al. 2026). In parallel, recent work shows that aging‐associated loss of the histone chaperone DAXX promotes chromatin decompaction at retrotransposable elements, microglial reactivity, cell‐cycle re‐entry and DNA damage, followed by the emergence of microglia with senescence‐associated features; sustained senescence in this model depends on promyelocytic leukemia protein (Yan et al. 2026). These findings place mitochondrial stress and heterochromatin maintenance upstream of persistent microglial dysfunction, while leaving open whether one conserved histone signature links these processes across human AD.

Aging also alters the chromatin landscape on which AD pathology develops. Single‐cell epigenomic work in the aging mouse brain has identified cell‐type‐specific loss of heterochromatin integrity and dysfunction of lineage transcription factors, while recent single‐cell multi‐omic analysis has shown that transposon demethylation and three‐dimensional genome remodeling occur in a cell‐type‐specific manner during brain aging (Amaral et al. 2026; Zeng et al. 2026). Such changes matter because retroelement derepression, altered enhancer accessibility and changes in genome architecture can modify the threshold at which inflammatory or stress‐responsive genes are activated. They should, however, be distinguished from senescence‐specific alterations unless the relevant senescence phenotype is independently established.

Neuronal chromatin instability provides a second route to chronic glial activation. Chronic loss of neuronal heterochromatin can derepress endogenous retroelements and provoke reactive gliosis, whereas tau‐associated chromatin relaxation and loss of piRNA‐mediated repression connect proteinopathy to neuronal genome regulation (Frost et al. 2014; Newman et al. 2025; Sun et al. 2018). Border‐associated macrophages can also induce paracrine microglial senescence, indicating that senescence‐associated states may propagate through cell–cell signaling rather than arise exclusively within the initially stressed cell (Hu et al. 2025). Evidence for H3K9me3 loss and retroelement activation in peripheral aging tissues provides mechanistic precedent but should not be treated as direct evidence for an AD‐specific CNS pathway (Liu, Molchanov, et al. 2025).

Astrocytes undergo substantial aging‐ and disease‐associated transcriptional remodeling, but direct evidence that a defined histone PTM establishes a stable astrocytic senescence programme in AD remains limited (Fancy et al. 2024; Perez J et al. 2026). Neurons are even more distinct because they are post‐mitotic and do not undergo classical proliferative senescence; senescence‐like neuronal phenotypes should therefore be separated conceptually from canonical cell‐cycle arrest (Wu, Sun, and Chow 2024). Senescence is thus best considered one component of the aging brain chromatin landscape, not a synonym for all persistent histone reprogramming in AD.

Taken together, the evidence supports a model in which senescence contributes to, but does not fully define, persistent chromatin dysfunction in the aging and AD brain. The transition from transient stress responses to durable inflammatory states appears to require coordination among chromatin architecture, enhancer regulation, histone turnover, metabolic state, and innate immune sensing. The major unresolved question is which of these features are causal and persistent within specific human CNS cell types, rather than merely correlated with disease stage or altered cellular composition.

4 Cell‐Type‐Specific Histone Reprogramming in AD

Histone modifications are not deployed uniformly across the CNS. Each cell type has a distinct repertoire of lineage‐defining transcription factors, enhancers, writers, erasers, and readers, as well as distinct metabolic and inflammatory inputs. Consequently, the same PTM can mark a reparative state in one cell and a persistent inflammatory state in another. Cell type is therefore not a descriptive annotation added after the fact; it is a determinant of how a histone modification is written, interpreted, and functionally propagated (Figure 4) (Butovsky et al. 2014; Gosselin et al. 2017; Green et al. 2024; Hammond et al. 2019; Lavin et al. 2014; Saeki and Ozato 2025).

4.1 Microglia: Highly Plastic Chromatin States Under Inflammatory and Metabolic Stress

Microglia provide the strongest example of context‐dependent chromatin plasticity in AD. Homeostatic identity is maintained by an enhancer network involving PU.1, SALL1, and MEF2C and reinforced by TGF‐β‐dependent signaling (Butovsky et al. 2014; Gosselin et al. 2017; Hammond et al. 2019; Saeki and Ozato 2025). The surrounding tissue environment is integral to this regulatory state: human and mouse microglia progressively remodel their transcriptional programmes when removed from the CNS or exposed to injury‐associated cues (Gosselin et al. 2017; Hammond et al. 2019; Lavin et al. 2014). In primary human microglia from 150 donors, transcriptomic and chromatin‐accessibility analyses linked genetic variation at 21 ad risk loci to microglial regulatory networks and refined several loci to candidate target genes, placing AD susceptibility within a cell‐specific regulatory context rather than treating risk variants as isolated expression changes (Kosoy et al. 2022).

Spatial context adds another layer of complexity. Integrated fate mapping, single‐cell transcriptomics, epigenomic profiling, and histology in an AD mouse model showed that plaque‐associated and non‐plaque‐associated microglia occupy distinct, dynamic states with different chromatin‐accessibility profiles and can transition during amyloid deposition (Ardura‐Fabregat et al. 2025). This finding has an important methodological implication: bulk measurements of histone PTMs cannot readily distinguish a true within‐cell change from altered cell composition or state redistribution. Cell‐type‐resolved and spatially resolved chromatin profiling is therefore essential for defining AD‐associated histone states.

Inflammatory activation remodels enhancer‐associated histone marks. H3K27ac is consistently implicated in microglial enhancer activation, while H3K4me1 provides an additional indicator of enhancer priming (Huang et al. 2023; Wendeln et al. 2018). In the experimental brain‐training paradigm, inflammatory priming produced long‐lasting H3K4me1 changes together with altered H3K27ac patterns, and microglia‐specific Hdac1/2 deletion attenuated the trained response (Wendeln et al. 2018). These findings support durable enhancer reprogramming as a mechanism of inflammatory memory. Equivalent persistent histone memory after amyloid‐β exposure in AD, however, remains to be established; current evidence is stronger for state‐dependent enhancer remodeling than for a validated amyloid‐specific histone memory programme (Nirakis et al. 2025; Valiukas et al. 2025).

Histone lactylation illustrates the same principle at a different biochemical layer. In aging‐associated senescent microglia, H3K18 lactylation is associated with NF‐κB activation and SASP‐factor production (Wei et al. 2023). In AD itself, H4K12la is elevated in plaque‐adjacent microglia in both 5xFAD mice and human AD brain, is enriched at promoters of glycolytic genes, and participates in a positive‐feedback circuit linking glycolysis, histone lactylation, and PKM2; pharmacological inhibition of PKM2 attenuates microglial activation, whereas microglia‐specific Pkm2 ablation improves spatial learning and memory in 5xFAD mice (Pan et al. 2022). By contrast, H3K18la can support a neuroprotective microglial programme through Plxnb2 after ischaemic stroke, whereas H4K12la promotes PD‐1 transcription and facilitates microglial repair after spinal cord injury (Hu et al. 2024; Li, Wang, et al. 2025; Wang, Zhou, et al. 2025). In cerebral ischaemia, microglial SMEK1 deficiency increases lactate production through the PDK3–PDH pathway, elevates H3K9la, and promotes transcription of glycolytic genes such as Ldha and Hif1α, providing a direct example of a metabolic feedback loop in which a change in glycolytic flux is linked to a site‐specific histone modification (Si et al. 2024). Conversely, oxygen–glucose deprivation has been associated with H3K9la‐dependent pro‐inflammatory programmes in microglia, and a Parkinson disease model linked increased glycolysis‐derived lactate to microglial activation through histone lactylation (He et al. 2024; Qin et al. 2025). These observations argue against assigning a fixed biological direction to lactylation as a modification class.

A recent spinal cord injury study further emphasizes why cell identity must be specified. Lactate accumulation increased H3K9la in lesion‐infiltrating macrophages and circulating monocytes rather than resident microglia. Integrated CUT&Tag and RNA‐seq implicated TXNIP as a direct H3K9la‐associated target and connected this axis to NLRP3 activation, mitochondrial dysfunction and tissue inflammation; selective inhibition of macrophage H3K9la improved functional recovery (Shi et al. 2026). This study provides strong mechanistic evidence for a lactate–histone–inflammation pathway, but in a distinct myeloid compartment and disease context.

Repressive chromatin can also constrain inflammatory programmes in microglia. In an adolescent depression model, microglial PCGF1 overexpression increased RING1B/H2AK119ub and EZH2/H3K27me3 occupancy at the MMP10 promoter, suppressing MMP10 transcription and attenuating microglia‐mediated neuroinflammation. This study illustrates that increased deposition of repressive marks can be protective in one inflammatory context and that their effects depend on the genomic target rather than on the modification alone (Li, Du, et al. 2025).

Microglial lipid metabolism adds another layer of state control. The TREM2‐APOE axis regulates lipid sensing and disease‐associated microglial programmes, and disturbed lipid trafficking is linked to metabolically stressed states with altered phagocytic and homeostatic function (Cheng and Ho 2025; Jung et al. 2025; Krasemann et al. 2017; Sprenger et al. 2025; Traetta et al. 2025). Changes in lipid handling also modify the metabolic context in which acetyl‐CoA, NAD+, S‐adenosylmethionine, α‐ketoglutarate, and lactate are generated or consumed (Jung et al. 2025; Rahimpour et al. 2024). The resulting interaction between lipid state and chromatin is an important candidate mechanism because microglial metabolic transitions can change both the substrate supply to histone enzymes and the transcription factors that recruit those enzymes.

Other histone‐associated modifications expand the repertoire. Microglial H3K18 crotonylation is increased in 5xFAD and Aβ42‐injected mouse models, localizes predominantly to microglia and promotes STAT1 expression with downstream inflammatory and cognitive phenotypes (Weng et al. 2026). In a Parkinson disease model, γH2AX was linked to an α‐synuclein–STING–type I interferon axis, but this should be interpreted as disease‐specific evidence rather than as proof of an analogous AD neuronal pathway (Wang, Sun, et al. 2025). Likewise, H3Cit detected after stroke can be delivered by neutrophil extracellular traps and act extracellularly on microglia through AIM2, rather than representing a resident microglial nuclear programme (Chen, Ni, et al. 2025; Seol et al. 2025). The source, compartment, and molecular substrate of a histone PTM are therefore essential to interpreting its function.

Aging resets the baseline on which these microglial programmes operate. Age‐related loss of heterochromatin integrity and lineage‐factor function can precede disease‐associated state transitions, while senescent microglia can overlap phenotypically with disease‐associated microglia in experimental tauopathy (Amaral et al. 2026; Ng et al. 2023; Rachmian et al. 2024). Human microglial xenograft studies further show that APOE isoforms can reshape the transcriptional and epigenomic landscape of human microglia in an AD brain environment (Murphy, Hu, et al. 2025). These observations support a model in which aging does not simply add inflammatory stimuli; it alters the chromatin and metabolic starting state from which microglia respond to amyloid, tau, lipids and tissue damage.

Persistence is the critical criterion for distinguishing adaptive activation from epigenetic memory. Acute enhancer activation can support debris clearance and repair, whereas chronic disease‐associated stimulation can maintain complement activity, cytokine production, and synaptic remodeling (Parkhurst et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2020). Experimental immune training can leave durable histone‐associated changes in microglia, and APOE isoforms can reshape the transcriptional and chromatin‐accessibility landscape of human microglia engrafted into an AD mouse brain (Murphy, Hu, et al. 2025; Wendeln et al. 2018). Neither study, however, establishes that an AD‐associated histone PTM persists after removal of the initiating stimulus. Demonstrating such persistence in human AD‐relevant microglia remains a major experimental opportunity.

The same principle extends beyond resident microglia to other immune cells at the CNS interface. In experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, pharmacological inhibition of EED reduced H3K27me3 in dendritic cells, derepressed WNT pathway genes, and suppressed dendritic‐cell migration, thereby alleviating neuroinflammation. This study is not an AD model, but it illustrates how cell‐specific Polycomb states can regulate inflammatory trafficking in the CNS environment (Hong et al. 2025).

4.2 Astrocytes: Reactive Chromatin States and Intercellular Metabolic Control

Astrocytes undergo extensive transcriptional and metabolic remodeling during neuroinflammation, but the chromatin logic of these states is less completely defined than in microglia. Reactive astrocytes are heterogeneous and shaped by local tissue signals, including microglial cytokines such as IL‐1α, TNF, and C1q (Lee et al. 2024; Li, Huo, et al. 2025; Liddelow et al. 2017; Morales Pantoja and Mintz 2026; Wang, Qiao, et al. 2025). Microglia‐induced pathological conversion therefore provides an important model for asking whether glial reactivity can be encoded beyond the initiating inflammatory signal.

Histone acetylation is among the best‐characterized chromatin features of reactive astrocytes. Microglia‐induced pathological conversion has been associated with a stable increase in H3K9/K14ac, a transient increase in H3K27ac and a reduction in H3K9me3 (Villarreal et al. 2021). This temporal separation is informative: not every activation‐associated histone change is equally persistent, and stable H3K9/K14ac does not by itself establish a senescent state. Pharmacological HDAC inhibition can modify glial inflammatory responses, but these interventions affect both histone and non‐histone substrates and therefore require cautious mechanistic interpretation (Mancino et al. 2025; Pinheiro, Diniz, et al. 2025). Importantly, the consequences of HDAC modulation differ across neural progenitors, mature neurons and glial populations, so cell‐type‐aware dosing is required in the astrocyte compartment as elsewhere (Diniz et al. 2025). A primary study in cultured astrocytes and in mice receiving intracerebroventricular Aβ oligomers showed that HDAC inhibition (LASSBio‐1911) reduces astrocyte reactivity, enhances astrocytic synaptogenic potential and improves synaptic and memory outcome (Diniz et al. 2024).

Astrocytes can also regulate neuronal chromatin indirectly through metabolic coupling. Astrocyte‐derived ApoE particles deliver microRNAs that suppress neuronal cholesterol biosynthesis, increasing acetyl‐CoA availability and promoting neuronal histone acetylation and immediate‐early‐gene transcription; ApoE4 is less effective than ApoE3 in this pathway (Li, Zhang, et al. 2021). This is a striking example of an intercellular metabolic‐to‐chromatin relay: the cell that changes the metabolic environment is not the cell in which the histone modification is ultimately measured. Broader work on cell‐type‐specific brain cholesterol metabolism places this pathway within a larger division of metabolic labor across neural cell types (Li, Zhang, and Liu 2022).

Astrocytic epigenetic memory has been demonstrated in experimental CNS autoimmunity. Disease‐associated astrocytes retain a reactivatable inflammatory programme in which ACLY‐derived acetyl‐CoA and p300‐dependent chromatin regulation contribute to inflammatory transcription (Lee et al. 2024). Because this work derives from experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis and related CNS inflammatory settings rather than AD, it should be regarded as a mechanistic precedent for persistence rather than direct evidence of an AD astrocyte programme. A further example comes from experimental subarachnoid hemorrhage, in which BRD4‐dependent regulation of histone lactylation, particularly H4K8la, influences astrocyte polarization; silencing BRD4 reduces H4K8la and aggravates A1‐like polarization and neuronal injury (Zhang, Zhou, Lu, et al. 2024). Comparable cell‐type‐resolved evidence in AD remains sparse (Hu et al. 2026).

Metabolic regulation of astrocytic chromatin provides a complementary mechanistic link. In LPS‐stimulated astrocytes and an MPTP Parkinson disease model, PHGDH‐dependent serine synthesis sustains NADH and thereby facilitates histone acetylation at inflammatory cytokine promoters; PHGDH inhibition reduces promoter acetylation and pro‐inflammatory cytokine production (Lv et al. 2025). Similarly, in LPC‐induced demyelination, SIRT6 restrains astrocytic H3K9ac and CHI3L1 expression; SIRT6 inhibition increases H3K9ac occupancy at the CHI3L1 promoter and worsens remyelination, whereas astrocyte‐specific SIRT6 overexpression has the opposite effect (Du et al. 2024). These examples show that metabolic and deacetylase pathways can converge on astrocytic histone acetylation, while also underscoring that the relevant causal chain may be pathway‐ and locus‐specific.

4.3 Neurons: Activity‐Dependent Histone Regulation, Inflammatory Stress and Chromatin Instability

Neurons are post‐mitotic and therefore do not undergo classical proliferative senescence, yet they are highly dependent on dynamic chromatin regulation. Their relevant phenotypes include altered activity‐dependent transcription, changes in histone acetylation, DNA‐damage‐associated histone phosphorylation, and loss of heterochromatic integrity. Senescence‐like neuronal features may occur under selected pathological conditions, but these should not be equated with canonical cell‐cycle arrest (Wu, Kupferschmid, et al. 2025).

Activity‐dependent chromatin regulation is a physiological baseline against which pathological changes should be interpreted. Learning and synaptic stimulation recruit CREB, CBP/p300, MEF2 and SRF to immediate‐early and plasticity‐associated genes, while neuronal enhancers acquire activating histone acetylation that supports rapid transcriptional responses (Chen et al. 2019; Khan, Carretero‐Rey, et al. 2025; Malik et al. 2014; Singh and Paramanik 2024). HDAC2 elevation, by contrast, is associated with repression of plasticity‐associated genes and impaired cognitive function in experimental neurodegeneration (Asadi et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2019; Gräff et al. 2012; Malik et al. 2014; Singh and Paramanik 2024). These observations emphasize that increased acetylation is not intrinsically pathological; its functional effect depends on genomic location, neuronal state and the transcriptional programme engaged.

Neuronal chromatin is also shaped by glial metabolic signals. Astrocyte‐derived ApoE‐dependent suppression of neuronal cholesterol synthesis increases acetyl‐CoA availability and promotes neuronal histone acetylation, including H3K27ac at immediate‐early genes (Li, Zhang, and Liu 2022; Li, Zhang, et al. 2021). A separate lead‐exposure model showed that sodium butyrate increased ACSS2‐dependent H3K9ac and BDNF expression and improved neuroinflammatory and cognitive phenotypes (Li, Liu, et al. 2024). These studies illustrate two different routes to neuronal acetylation: intercellular metabolic coupling and pharmacological modulation of acetate utilization. Neither should be assumed to represent the dominant pathway in AD without cell‐type‐specific validation.

Neuronal histone citrullination provides an additional example of metabolic–epigenetic coupling that is mechanistically distinct from inflammatory CitH3 released by neutrophils. After ischaemic stroke, PLA2G2E‐derived lipid metabolism generates DGLA in peri‐infarct neurons, increases PADI4 expression and promotes histone citrullination‐associated transcription of recovery genes; scRNA‐seq and epigenetic profiling linked this axis to endogenous neural repair (Nakamura et al. 2023). This observation is particularly informative for the present framework because it connects a lipid‐derived signal to a neuronal histone PTM and a functional outcome, while also illustrating that histone citrullination can be reparative rather than uniformly inflammatory.

DNA‐damage‐associated histone marks provide another potential bridge between neuronal stress and neuroimmune signaling. γH2AX is a canonical marker of DNA double‐strand break responses in stressed neurons (Rahman et al. 2025; Scheijen et al. 2025). Microglial cGAS deletion and pharmacological STING blockade each alleviate AD‐associated phenotypes in experimental models, establishing the relevance of cGAS‐STING signaling in AD but not proving that neuronal γH2AX directly activates this pathway (Chung et al. 2024; He et al. 2025). The mechanistic gap is therefore precise: the field needs experiments that connect a defined neuronal DNA‐damage state to cytosolic DNA sensing, cell–cell signaling and downstream microglial responses (Wang, Sun, et al. 2025).

Heterochromatin is similarly vulnerable to aging and proteinopathy. H3K9me2, H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 contribute to repression of inappropriate programmes and genome stability, whereas aging and neurodegeneration can alter the distribution and integrity of these repressive states (Amaral et al. 2026; Katbe et al. 2026; Kumar and Beck 2025; Newman et al. 2025). Loss of heterochromatic control can derepress transposable elements, while tau pathology has been linked to global chromatin relaxation and altered histone acetylation (Frost et al. 2014; Klein et al. 2019; Kumar and Beck 2025; Min et al. 2015; Sun et al. 2018). These changes provide a plausible route by which proteinopathy can convert a normally plastic neuronal epigenome into a persistently dysregulated state.

Human AD brain studies provide the strongest evidence that disease‐associated histone reprogramming is not synonymous with cellular senescence. Regional loss of H4K16ac has been reported in AD, integrated multi‐omics studies identify gains in H3K27ac and H3K9ac, and histone acetylome‐wide analyses identify AD‐associated H3K27ac differences in the entorhinal cortex (Marzi et al. 2018; Nativio et al. 2018, 2020). Tau pathology is likewise associated with large‐scale changes in histone acetylation, and experimental manipulation of histone acetylation can modify tau‐mediated neurodegeneration (Klein et al. 2019; Min et al. 2015). A 2026 single‐nucleus epigenomic atlas of the adult human CNS further demonstrated strong cell‐type specificity of H3K27ac and H3K27me3 and identified primed, developmentally reminiscent chromatin states in adult oligodendroglia and astrocytes, but not microglia (Kabbe et al. 2026). This resource is not an AD cohort, but it reinforces the need to interpret disease‐associated chromatin through cellular identity. Together, these findings argue that human AD chromatin should be interpreted through cell type, regional anatomy and disease context rather than through a single global histone signature.

Histone H2A ubiquitination provides an additional chromatin link between DNA damage and proteostatic stress. In Brap loss‐of‐function mouse brain, increased H2A ubiquitination accompanies persistent DNA damage, altered proteostasis, senescence and neuroinflammation, and elevated H2A ubiquitination has also been detected in human AD brain tissue (Guo et al. 2022). The experimental model supports a mechanistic connection between H2A ubiquitination and aging‐associated pathology, whereas the human AD finding remains associative.

A final layer is provided by PTM diversity. A 2026 study of G9a inhibition with the SAM‐competitive compound FLAV‐27 showed therapeutic effects in both SAMP8 and 5xFAD models, accompanied by multi‐omic reprogramming of H3K9me2/H3K18me1–3‐associated repression (Bellver‐Sanchis et al. 2026). The study strengthens the case that histone methylation is functionally relevant in AD models, but because G9a regulates multiple genomic targets, the causal contribution of any individual histone residue remains unresolved. Similarly, recent PD work identified CBP/p300‐dependent H3K27ac at the PDLIM7 locus as a regulatory node that mitigates neuroimmune dysregulation in 6‐OHDA models (Zhang and Yin 2026). Such studies broaden the mechanistic landscape while reinforcing the need to separate direct site‐level causality from pharmacological or pathway‐level effects.

Neuronal chromatin is therefore best viewed as an emergent component of a multicellular regulatory system in which glial state, neurotrophic signaling and lipid metabolism can reshape neuronal transcription and synaptic function (Li, Zhang, and Liu 2022; Li, Zhang, et al. 2021; Liddelow et al. 2017; Parkhurst et al. 2013). Across microglia, astrocytes and neurons, the available evidence converges on three principles. First, the same PTM can support inflammatory, reparative or adaptive programmes depending on residue, locus, cell identity and disease context. Second, metabolic and extracellular signals can impose chromatin states across cellular boundaries, so cell‐autonomous measurements may underestimate the contribution of neuron–glia coupling. Third, the field still lacks sufficient locus‐resolved and longitudinal evidence to determine which disease‐associated PTM states persist after the initiating stimulus is removed. Future studies should therefore combine single‐cell or spatial chromatin profiling with metabolic tracing and cell‐type‐specific genetic perturbation to distinguish state‐associated marks from causal, persistent chromatin mechanisms in AD (Table 3).

5 Metabolic Control of Histone Post‐Translational Modifications

Metabolism regulates histone PTMs by supplying substrates and cofactors to modifying enzymes and by altering the biochemical conditions that determine their activity. This relationship is not deterministic. Whole‐cell metabolite abundance does not necessarily predict the nuclear pool, and substrate availability alone does not determine which genomic loci will be modified. Enzyme recruitment, compartmentalization, chromatin accessibility and modification turnover together determine the transcriptional output (Malone et al. 2026; Martini et al. 2026; Wang, Zhu, et al. 2026). The most useful way to interpret metabolic‐epigenetic coupling in AD is therefore as a set of constrained biochemical routes, each of which must be validated at the level of cell type, compartment and locus.

5.1 Acetyl‐CoA Supply and Histone Acetylation

Acetyl‐CoA integrates glucose oxidation, mitochondrial citrate export, acetate metabolism and other carbon sources. In senescent cells, mitochondrial pyruvate‐citrate‐acetyl‐CoA metabolism sustains histone acetylation at SASP‐associated loci; inhibition of this pathway reduces H3K27ac, chromatin accessibility and inflammatory gene expression (Martini et al. 2026). This study is important because it links a defined metabolic route to a chromatin phenotype and a downstream inflammatory programme rather than simply correlating metabolite abundance with histone acetylation.

Nuclear acetate metabolism provides a complementary route. ACSS2 can localize to chromatin in neurons, increase local acetyl‐CoA availability and support histone acetylation required for long‐term memory (Mews et al. 2017). In astrocyte‐neuron communication, ApoE‐dependent suppression of neuronal cholesterol synthesis similarly increases acetyl‐CoA availability and neuronal histone acetylation (Li, Zhang, et al. 2021). These findings demonstrate that acetyl‐CoA pools are functionally compartmentalized and can be supplied by different metabolic routes in different cell types.

For AD, this distinction matters because increased acetyl‐CoA is not equivalent to a pathogenic acetylation state. A causal mechanism requires identification of the acetyltransferase, the modified genomic loci, and the transcriptional programme that links the metabolic change to disease‐relevant phenotypes. The same metabolic intervention could also change protein acetylation, mitochondrial function, or ATP balance independently of histone regulation.

5.2 Lactate Metabolism and Histone Lactylation

Histone lactylation provides a direct biochemical route through which glycolytic metabolism can influence chromatin. Lactylation was first described in activated macrophages, where glycolysis‐derived lactate was linked to H3K18 lactylation and induction of reparative gene expression (Wei et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2019). The enzymatic framework has since expanded, with evidence for distinct lactyl‐CoA‐generating activities, lactyltransferase mechanisms and Class I HDAC delactylases (Liu, Ren, et al. 2025; Moreno‐Yruela et al. 2022; Zhu et al. 2025). Much of this mechanistic work comes from non‐CNS systems, so it establishes biochemical precedent rather than proving an equivalent pathway in AD brain cells.

Metabolic regulation can occur upstream of lactate itself. BCAP‐dependent metabolic reprogramming promotes aerobic glycolysis and lactate production and thereby supports histone lactylation during the transition of macrophages toward a reparative state (Irizarry‐Caro et al. 2020). The relevant conclusion is that BCAP‐linked metabolic flux can tune histone lactylation and macrophage state. Lactylation also occurs on non‐histone proteins, and its consequences differ from those of histone lactylation. In pro‐inflammatory macrophages, lactylation of PKM2 at K62 stabilizes the tetrameric form of the enzyme, increases pyruvate kinase activity, and reduces its nuclear distribution, thereby restraining inflammatory metabolic adaptation and favoring a reparative phenotype (Wang, Yang, et al. 2022). PKM2 lactylation should therefore be assessed separately from histone lactylation.

Microglial studies illustrate why residue and context matter. In AD‐associated microglia, H4K12 lactylation is enriched at glycolytic gene promoters and participates in a positive‐feedback circuit involving PKM2 and glucose metabolism (Pan et al. 2022). In senescent microglia, H3K18 lactylation is associated with NF‐κB activation and increased expression of SASP factors such as IL‐6 and IL‐8 (Wei et al. 2023). The different residues, cell states, and metabolic settings preclude treating these findings as one generalized lactate‐driven inflammatory pathway.

Other CNS models reinforce this context dependence. H4K12 lactylation can promote reparative responses after spinal cord injury, H3K18 lactylation can support neuroprotection after ischaemic stroke, and H3K9 lactylation has been linked to inflammatory programmes in cerebral ischaemia and oxygen–glucose deprivation (He et al. 2024; Hu et al. 2024; Li, Wang, et al. 2025; Si et al. 2024; Wang, Zhou, et al. 2025). A Parkinson disease model likewise connected enhanced glycolysis‐derived lactate to microglial activation through histone lactylation (Qin et al. 2025). These studies establish site‐specific biochemical precedent but cannot substitute for direct validation in human AD tissue.

A central causal challenge is to distinguish chromatin‐mediated effects from the broader consequences of changing glycolysis or lactate. Such interventions simultaneously alter ATP production, redox state, mitochondrial function, and signaling. A convincing lactylation mechanism would therefore need to connect metabolic flux to the relevant nuclear substrate pool, identify the specific histone site and genomic targets, and show that perturbing the site or its writer/eraser changes the disease‐relevant phenotype.

5.3 NAD+, Redox Metabolism and Sirtuin‐Dependent Histone Regulation

NAD+ connects metabolism to chromatin through sirtuin‐dependent deacetylation and through mitochondrial and DNA‐damage pathways. During senescence and aging, reduced SIRT1 activity is associated with increased acetylation at selected inflammatory loci and enhanced SASP expression (Hayakawa et al. 2015; Xu et al. 2020). In microglia, SIRT1 can also deacetylate RelA/p65 and limit NF‐κB‐dependent inflammatory responses and amyloid‐β‐associated toxicity (Chen et al. 2005). These actions include non‐histone targets, so a change in SIRT1 activity should not automatically be interpreted as a histone‐specific mechanism.

Brain NAD+ homeostasis is disrupted in human AD, and pharmacological restoration of NAD+ homeostasis can reverse multiple disease‐associated phenotypes in advanced mouse AD models (Chaubey et al. 2026). These findings support the disease relevance of NAD+ metabolism but do not establish histone acetylation as the dominant mediator. NAD+ restoration can affect sirtuins, mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, and DNA‐damage responses in parallel. The mechanistic question is therefore whether a defined NAD+‐sirtuin‐histone circuit contributes measurably to the therapeutic phenotype in the relevant CNS cell type.

Redox state provides a complementary connection between metabolism and chromatin. Reactive oxygen species and antioxidant capacity can alter chromatin‐regulatory enzymes while also contributing to DNA damage (Balamurli et al. 2024; Rahman et al. 2025). The same oxidative shift can therefore produce transcriptional effects through chromatin enzymes, DNA lesions, inflammatory signaling, or mitochondrial dysfunction. Disentangling these routes is essential before assigning a specific histone PTM a causal role.

5.4 S‐Adenosylmethionine and α‐Ketoglutarate‐Dependent Histone Methylation

Histone methylation is controlled by opposing methyltransferase and demethylase activities. S‐adenosylmethionine supplies methyl groups for histone methyltransferases, whereas α‐ketoglutarate supports JmjC‐domain histone demethylases (Akakura and Tabibzadeh 2026; Malone et al. 2026). Metabolic perturbations can therefore influence methylation from both sides of the deposition‐removal balance. As with acetyl‐CoA, however, a global metabolite measurement does not establish a global histone outcome.

Mitochondrial stress provides a relevant example. In human microglial models, activation of the mitochondrial unfolded protein response is associated with metabolic rewiring, S‐adenosylmethionine depletion and a senescent phenotype with altered inflammatory communication (Perez J et al. 2026). This observation links mitochondrial proteotoxic stress to methyl‐donor availability in a human microglial system, but it does not by itself identify which histone methylation sites change or whether those changes are required for the senescent phenotype.

Alpha‐ketoglutarate provides a complementary route through JmjC demethylases and TET enzymes. Experimental senescence models in which the α‐ketoglutarate‐TET axis is perturbed show altered chromatin states and reduced senescence plasticity, with reversal after restoration of the relevant metabolic pathway (Akakura and Tabibzadeh 2026). Because α‐ketoglutarate acts on both histone and DNA demethylation systems, effects attributed to “epigenetic regulation” should not automatically be assigned to histone methylation alone.

The outstanding problem is therefore specificity. The field needs measurements that distinguish methyl‐donor depletion from altered enzyme activity, define which methyltransferases or demethylases are rate limiting, and determine whether the resulting histone marks alter defined enhancers, promoters, or heterochromatic domains in AD‐relevant cells.

5.5 Other Acyl Donors: Crotonyl‐CoA, Succinyl‐CoA, Malonyl‐CoA and Ketone Bodies

Intermediary metabolism supplies a broader repertoire of acyl donors beyond acetyl‐CoA and lactate. Crotonyl‐CoA supports p300‐dependent histone crotonylation and transcriptional activation (Sabari et al. 2015). Succinyl‐CoA supports histone succinylation, and KAT2A coupled to the α‐ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex catalyzes H3K79 succinylation; H3K122 succinylation has also been linked to nucleosome destabilization (Wang et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2011; Zorro Shahidian et al. 2021). These findings establish a direct biochemical route from TCA‐cycle metabolism to histone acylation, but the neuroinflammatory consequences of these marks remain much less defined than those of acetylation or lactylation.

Histone malonylation provides a further potential connection to lipid metabolism. Malonyl‐CoA is generated by acetyl‐CoA carboxylase 1, while SIRT5 and KAT2A have been implicated in the regulation of histone malonylation (Peng et al. 2011; Zhang et al. 2023). Evidence for histone malonylation in neuroinflammation remains sparse. Non‐histone malonylation, such as GAPDH K213 malonylation in inflammatory signaling, should therefore be regarded as biochemical context rather than as direct evidence for a histone mechanism (Galván‐Peña et al. 2019).

Ketone metabolism illustrates a related but distinct route. Beta‐hydroxybutyrate can inhibit Class I histone deacetylases and can also serve as a substrate for histone lysine β‐hydroxybutyrylation (Shimazu et al. 2013; Xie et al. 2016). These mechanisms should not be conflated: changing deacetylase activity alters pre‐existing acetylation states, whereas installing β‐hydroxybutyrylation creates a different chemical signal with distinct reader and locus requirements.

5.6 From Metabolic Association to Causal Chromatin Regulation

Metabolic‐chromatin coupling provides a plausible mechanism through which persistent metabolic stress may stabilize cellular states, but metabolic association alone does not establish causality. A rigorous causal chain should connect four levels: a defined metabolic flux or substrate pool; the relevant writer, eraser or reader; a specific histone PTM at disease‐relevant loci; and a downstream transcriptional or cellular phenotype. The strongest evidence should also demonstrate perturbation and, where the model permits, persistence after the initiating stimulus is removed.

This standard is particularly important in AD. Changes in glycolysis, lactate, NAD+, oxidative stress and TCA‐cycle intermediates can alter ATP production, redox balance, protein modification and signaling independently of chromatin. Microglia, astrocytes and neurons also differ in metabolic flux, nuclear substrate availability and chromatin enzyme expression. A metabolic‐PTM pathway established in macrophages, fibroblasts, cancer cells or non‐AD injury models is therefore best described as mechanistic precedent until it is validated in the appropriate CNS population.

The most informative experiments will combine isotope tracing or compartment‐resolved metabolomics with locus‐resolved histone profiling and targeted perturbation of writers, erasers, or substrate‐generating pathways. Cell‐type‐specific genetic manipulation and single‐cell or spatial readouts will be particularly valuable because bulk tissue measurements cannot resolve whether a histone change reflects altered cell composition or an intracellular state transition. Finally, withdrawal and longitudinal experiments are needed to determine whether an altered metabolic‐histone circuit persists beyond continuous disease exposure. This progression from association to flux, locus, perturbation, and persistence provides a practical framework for testing whether metabolism merely accompanies AD chromatin remodeling or helps maintain it.

6 Histone Post‐Translational Modifications in AD Pathology: Proteinopathy–Chromatin–Neuroimmune Feedback

Aβ plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles are conventionally positioned upstream of the molecular and cellular changes that accompany AD. Increasing evidence instead supports a bidirectional relationship between proteinopathy and chromatin: Aβ and tau can reshape histone modifications and chromatin organization, whereas chromatin‐dependent transcription can influence amyloidogenic processing, neuronal resilience, and inflammatory competence.

6.1 Amyloid‐β: Bidirectional Coupling Between Amyloidogenic Processing and Chromatin

Aβ‐related pathology can be coupled to chromatin‐dependent regulation of amyloidogenic processing. In N2a cells expressing Swedish mutant APP, p300 binds the PS1 and BACE1 promoters and increases local H3 acetylation; pharmacological inhibition of p300 reduces promoter acetylation together with PS1 and BACE1 expression (Lu et al. 2014). More recent work in APP/PS1 mice showed that the histone deacetylase inhibitors valproic acid and WT161 reduce BACE1 and PSEN1 expression, increase ADAM10, and attenuate pathological and cognitive phenotypes (Zhang, Wang, Ye, et al. 2024). HDAC3 has also been shown to occupy the APP promoter, and its inhibition induces transcriptional programmes associated with neuroregeneration in AD models (Davis et al. 2024). Together, these studies support a role for histone acetylation in regulating APP processing, but pharmacological HDAC inhibition acts on multiple substrates and therefore does not establish any single histone residue as the sole mediator.

The relationship is not uniformly pro‐amyloidogenic. In induced neurons derived from individuals carrying an APP duplication, lowering CBP and p300 decreases H3K27ac and suppresses a set of homeostatic amyloid‐reducing genes, resulting in increased extracellular Aβ1–42 (Xu et al. 2024). Thus, an increase in H3K27ac detected in AD tissue cannot be interpreted as uniformly pathogenic; at some loci, acetylation may sustain compensatory or protective transcriptional programmes. Soluble Aβ can itself perturb neuronal chromatin. Across neural cell models, primary neurons, Tg2576 mice and human AD neocortex, Aβ exposure is associated with changes in H3K14ac, H3S10 phosphorylation and H3K9 dimethylation, indicating that amyloid stress can affect multiple layers of the histone modification landscape (Lithner et al. 2013). Whether these changes are adaptive, pathogenic or context‐dependent remains unresolved.

A second route connects Aβ pathology to metabolic chromatin signaling. In a 2024 ad study, reduction of the mitochondrial tricarboxylic‐acid‐cycle enzyme IDH3β increased lactate, histone lactylation and PAX6 expression, establishing a positive‐feedback loop in which PAX6 further suppressed IDH3β and was associated with tau hyperphosphorylation, synaptic impairment and cognitive deficits (Xin Wang et al. 2024). This study does not concern a single histone site as an isolated Aβ effector, but it provides a mechanistic example of how altered metabolism can become coupled to chromatin‐dependent transcription within AD pathology.

Microglia provide a more direct pathology‐associated example of this metabolic–chromatin interface. In 5xFAD mice and human AD brain, H4K12 lactylation is increased in plaque‐adjacent microglia and enriched at promoters of glycolytic genes; interference with the lactate–H4K12la–PKM2 circuit attenuates microglial activation and AD‐associated phenotypes (Pan et al. 2022). A distinct H3K18 lactylation programme has been linked to senescent microglia, NF‐κB activity and production of IL‐6 and IL‐8 (Wei et al. 2023). More recently, H4K12la was shown to promote NLRP3 transcription in a cigarette‐smoke‐accelerated AD model, coupling elevated lactate to microglial autophagy and inflammatory activation (Wang, Xia, et al. 2025). These studies should not be collapsed into a single “histone lactylation programme”: the residues, initiating stimuli and causal interventions differ. Their convergence instead suggests that metabolic changes generated by or superimposed on proteinopathy can alter the transcriptional threshold of responding microglia.

6.2 Tau: Distributed Chromatin Remodeling and Loss of Transcriptional Constraint

Tau is associated with a broader chromatin footprint than can be captured by any single promoter. In 669 aged human prefrontal cortex samples, tau burden was associated with altered H3K9ac across 5990 of 26,384 chromatin domains, whereas genome‐wide association with Aβ was substantially weaker (Klein et al. 2019). The tau‐associated changes occurred in large genomic domains linked to three‐dimensional chromatin organization and nuclear‐lamina association, and analogous patterns were detected in mouse tau models and tau‐expressing induced neurons (Klein et al. 2019). These data establish a widespread association between tau burden and histone acetylation, but they do not by themselves establish causality.

Targeted perturbations provide more specific examples in which chromatin repression constrains tau‐related pathology. BMI1, a component of Polycomb repressive complexes, is reduced in sporadic AD brain and in AD patient‐derived neurons; BMI1 deficiency is accompanied by reduced H2A monoubiquitination, increased MAPT expression and activation of GSK3β‐ and p53‐associated pathways, together with Aβ secretion, tau phosphorylation and neurodegenerative phenotypes (Flamier et al. 2018). The mechanistic inference is therefore that BMI1‐dependent chromatin repression is protective in this model; H2A ubiquitination should not be regarded as the sole downstream effector. The long non‐coding RNA NEAT1 provides a complementary route from RNA regulation to tau‐associated chromatin control: in SH‐SY5Y cells and APP/PS1 mice, NEAT1 recruits p300 to the FZD3 promoter and maintains H3K27ac, whereas NEAT1 loss reduces FZD3 expression, alters Wnt signaling and increases tau phosphorylation (Zhao et al. 2020).

Human AD tissue further argues against a uniform histone signature. Regional changes in H4K16ac were reported in aging and AD brain, whereas integrated multi‐omics identified disease‐associated gains in H3K27ac and H3K9ac, and acetylome‐wide profiling of entorhinal cortex identified 4162 ad‐associated H3K27ac peaks (Marzi et al. 2018; Nativio et al. 2018, 2020). Recent work in tauopathy adds another layer: tauopathic hippocampal neurons show histone hyperacetylation at neuronal enhancers linked to glutamatergic regulation, together with dysregulated cholesterol biosynthesis; correction with the epi‐drug CSP‐TTK21 counteracts enhancer hyperacetylation and improves object memory (Paiva et al. 2024). A complementary 2025 study found that tau reshapes the transcriptional landscape toward intermediate AD stages and is accompanied by progressive loss of heterochromatin‐associated features in cellular and human AD material (Siano et al. 2025). Collectively, these findings favor a model of region‐, locus‐ and disease‐stage‐specific chromatin remodeling rather than a single global “AD histone state”.

Astrocytic chromatin regulation provides a further route by which tau pathology can be amplified outside neurons. HDAC7 is increased in astrocytes from AD patients and PS19 tau mice; astrocyte‐specific genetic or pharmacological inhibition of HDAC7 enhances tau uptake and lysosomal degradation by preserving acetylation of TFEB at K310, restoring its nuclear translocation and lysosomal biogenesis, and improving tau pathology and cognitive performance (Ye et al. 2025). This study is important because it links a defined chromatin enzyme to a cell‐type‐specific protein‐clearance programme and, in turn, to tau burden.

Histone methylation is also emerging as a functionally relevant regulator of AD‐associated proteopathy. A brain‐penetrant G9a inhibitor, MS1262, improved memory and noncognitive phenotypes in multiple AD mouse models and reversed expression or phosphorylation of several AD‐associated proteins (Xie et al. 2025). A second study of the selective G9a inhibitor FLAV‐27 reported reduced Aβ and phosphorylated tau aggregation, improved neuritic complexity and cognitive or synaptic phenotypes in both 5xFAD and SAMP8 models, accompanied by multi‐omic remodeling of H3K9me2/H3K18me‐associated repression (Bellver‐Sanchis et al. 2026). These pharmacological studies strengthen the case for G9a‐dependent repressive chromatin as a disease‐relevant node, but because G9a has multiple molecular outputs, they should not be interpreted as proof that H3K9me2 or H3K18me2 at a particular locus is itself the singular driver of proteopathy.

6.3 Convergence on Chromatin States That Control Inflammatory Competence

Once proteinopathy has altered neuronal and glial states, chromatin‐regulatory systems can determine whether the ensuing immune response remains transient or becomes self‐reinforcing. Repressive methylation provides one example. In an AD mouse model, reduced JMJD3 was associated with increased H3K27me3, lower BDNF expression and exacerbated mitochondrial‐stress and Aβ‐related phenotypes; increasing JMJD3 or BDNF partially reversed these abnormalities (Wang, Zhang, et al. 2022). A related mechanistic precedent from Parkinson disease showed that microglial Jmjd3 is required for H3K27me3 removal during a beneficial microglial state transition (Tang et al. 2014). Together, these studies support a state‐ and context‐dependent role for the JMJD3–H3K27me3 axis rather than a universal inflammatory interpretation of the mark.

Acetylation offers more locus‐resolved examples in AD. In early AD brain and an hAPP knock‐in model, reduced FZD1 and FZD7 expression accompanies loss of promoter‐associated H4K16ac and increased recruitment of nuclear SIRT2; SIRT2 inhibition restores H4K16ac and FZD1/FZD7 expression, linking aberrant deacetylation to impaired Wnt signaling (Palomer et al. 2022). Complementary work in APP/PS1 mice showed that selective Class I HDAC inhibition reduces microglial inflammatory changes in the entorhinal cortex (Wang et al. 2023). Because the latter intervention targets an enzyme class rather than a single histone residue, it supports the tractability of chromatin regulation without assigning the phenotype to one PTM.

Metabolic acylation and innate immune signaling provide another convergence. H4K12la at plaque‐adjacent microglial glycolytic promoters participates in a lactate–PKM2 feedback loop, while H3K18la is associated with inflammatory activation in senescent microglia and H4K12la can promote NLRP3 transcription in a stress‐accelerated AD model (Pan et al. 2022; Wang, Xia, et al. 2025; Wei et al. 2023). In 2026, increased microglial H3K18 crotonylation was linked experimentally to STAT1 expression and cognitive dysfunction in both 5xFAD and Aβ42‐injection models; elevating crotonylation increased microgliosis and inflammatory cytokines, whereas STAT1 inhibition attenuated these effects (Weng et al. 2026). The distinction between residues is mechanistically important: “histone acylation” is not itself the regulatory unit, and the direction of effect depends on the modified residue, cell state, genomic context and initiating metabolic signal.

A particularly direct link between histone acetylation and innate immunity was reported in 2026. KAT7 and its associated H3K14ac mark were elevated in microglia from 5xFAD mice and human AD brains. Integrative epigenomic and transcriptomic analyses identified Cmpk2 as a KAT7‐responsive target; loss of KAT7 reduced Cmpk2 transcription, impaired mitochondrial DNA replication and cytosolic mtDNA release, and consequently suppressed cGAS–STING and NLRP3 signaling. Microglia‐specific Kat7 deletion or pharmacological inhibition reduced neuroinflammation and Aβ burden and improved synaptic plasticity and cognition in 5xFAD mice (Liu et al. 2026). This study provides a much more direct mechanistic bridge between a defined histone acetylation programme and microglial mitochondrial innate immunity than is available from pathway‐level cGAS–STING studies alone.

Another 2026 study identified the histone demethylase PHF2 as an AD‐associated regulator of inflammatory genes. PHF2 was increased in AD postmortem tissue, AD patient‐derived iPSC neurons and 5xFAD brain; bidirectional manipulation coupled with ChIP‐seq and transcriptional analyses linked PHF2 to inflammatory and neurodegeneration‐related genes including Stat3, Nfkbia, Nfkb2, Tnfrsf1a, IL6st, Notch2 and Csf1. Phf2 knockdown in 5xFAD reduced microglial and astrocytic activation and restored glutamatergic synaptic function (Yang, Ren, et al. 2026). The study places chromatin enzyme activity upstream of a distributed inflammatory transcriptional programme, although it does not imply that all PHF2‐responsive genes are regulated through one histone site.

Genome instability provides a second route into innate immune activation. Loss of BRAP increases H2A mono‐ and poly‐ubiquitination, persistent DNA damage, proteostasis defects, senescence and neuroinflammation, and increased H2A ubiquitination has been detected in human AD brain tissue (Guo et al. 2022). Separately, microglia‐specific cGAS deletion and STING blockade attenuate Aβ‐associated pathology and inflammatory signaling in experimental AD models (Chung et al. 2024; He et al. 2025). A 2025 PNAS study further showed that excessive Aβ engulfment by microglia causes mitochondrial stress and cytosolic mtDNA release, activating cGAS–STING; the resulting inflammatory response impairs further Aβ clearance, creating an Aβ–STING negative‐feedback loop that can be interrupted experimentally (Tian et al. 2025). These studies establish the biological relevance of the cGAS–STING branch but, before the KAT7 work, did not identify a defined histone lesion upstream of the mitochondrial DNA signal. The newer KAT7 study therefore closes an important mechanistic gap, while the precise contribution of chromatin damage itself remains context‐dependent.

Finally, recent epigenomic studies argue that inflammatory chromatin states are spatially and genetically heterogeneous. In a mouse AD model, plaque‐associated and non‐plaque‐associated microglia displayed distinct chromatin accessibility states, with non‐plaque microglia representing a dynamic population that can transition toward plaque‐associated states (Ardura‐Fabregat et al. 2025). In human microglia xenografted into APPNL‐G‐F brains, APOE isoforms produced widespread genotype‐dependent differences in both transcription and chromatin accessibility, indicating that disease‐associated microglial state is shaped by genotype as well as local pathology (Murphy, Hu, et al. 2025). These observations provide an important framework for interpreting histone PTMs: the same residue cannot be assumed to have the same transcriptional consequence in every microglial population or anatomical niche.

6.4 From Feedback Loops to Testable Models of Persistent Disease

Taken together, the evidence supports a nested feedback architecture rather than a linear “proteinopathy → inflammation” pathway. Aβ‐related stress can alter chromatin‐dependent regulation of APP processing and neuronal transcription; tau can impose broader and spatially organized chromatin changes; and proteinopathic signals can reshape the metabolic and inflammatory states of glial cells. Histone PTMs then influence the transcriptional thresholds that govern amyloid processing, proteostasis, synaptic signaling, cellular resilience, and inflammatory competence, thereby feeding back on the tissue environment in which Aβ and tau accumulate. The relevant regulatory unit is therefore not an isolated histone mark but a chromatin state embedded within a cellular and metabolic state.

The strength of evidence across this network is uneven. The most direct AD evidence now comes from locus‐ or cell‐resolved studies of p300‐dependent APP processing, CBP/p300‐dependent amyloid‐reducing programmes, SIRT2–H4K16ac control of Wnt receptors, H4K12la‐associated microglial metabolic feedback, H3K18cr–STAT1 signaling, HDAC7–TFEB control of astrocytic tau clearance and KAT7–H3K14ac control of mitochondrial innate immunity (Liu et al. 2026; Lu et al. 2014; Weng et al. 2026; Xu et al. 2024; Ye et al. 2025). Human epigenomic studies establish reproducible changes in H4K16ac, H3K27ac and H3K9ac and demonstrate that tau‐associated chromatin remodeling is extensive, but these association studies do not by themselves establish causality (Klein et al. 2019; Marzi et al. 2018; Nativio et al. 2018, 2020; Pan et al. 2022). Pharmacological studies of G9a, HDACs and other chromatin enzymes provide convergent functional evidence but necessarily require caution because these regulators have multiple genomic and non‐genomic substrates (Bellver‐Sanchis et al. 2026; Wang et al. 2023; Xie et al. 2025; Zhang, Wang, Ye, et al. 2024).

Recent studies also broaden the metabolic–epigenetic interface beyond lactylation. In 3xTg‐AD mice and human AD tissue, β‐hydroxybutyrate and H3K9 β‐hydroxybutyrylation are reduced; restoring HMGCS2 or replenishing β‐hydroxybutyrate increases H3K9bhb at promoters of NMDA receptor subunits, Syn1, and axon‐related genes and improves synaptic plasticity and cognition (Yu et al. 2026). This finding reinforces a central principle of this section: metabolic intermediates can act as chromatin substrates, but their functional consequences remain locus‐ and cell‐state‐specific.

The central unresolved question is whether any AD‐associated histone modification functions as a persistent memory variable rather than as a contemporaneous readout of disease stress. Demonstrating such a role requires more than detecting a changed mark. It requires cell‐type‐ and locus‐resolved measurements; perturbation of the relevant writer or eraser; evidence that the modification changes a defined transcriptional programme; and demonstration that the altered state persists, or is reproducibly re‐established, after the initiating proteinopathic stimulus is removed. Longitudinal and spatial profiling combined with metabolic tracing, cell‐type‐specific perturbation and residue‐resolved genetic manipulation could therefore move the field from cataloging AD‐associated chromatin changes toward identifying which chromatin states actually store, amplify or propagate neurodegenerative stress.

7 Therapeutic Potential and Limitations

Interventions targeting histone PTMs during AD pathological progression are moving from concept to therapeutic testing. Given the chronic nature of AD pathological states, their cell‐type specificity, and the progressive decline in reversibility with disease progression, simply blocking cytokines or clearing protein aggregates is often insufficient to dismantle established pathological circuits (Amniouel et al. 2025; Li, Qiu, et al. 2025; Olawade et al. 2025) By contrast, resetting pathological chromatin toward homeostasis offers a complementary strategy. This chapter discusses five classes of interventions—small‐molecule epigenetic modulators, metabolic–epigenetic interventions, natural products, CRISPR‐based epigenome editing, and lifestyle and dietary approaches—and evaluates the therapeutic potential and limitations of each (Figure 5; Table 4).

7.1 Small‐Molecule Epigenetic Modulators

Small‐molecule epigenetic modulators remain the most extensively studied class of strategies in AD. They are currently divided into four main categories: HDAC inhibitors (Luchsinger et al. 2016), histone methyltransferase (HMT) inhibitors (Griñán‐Ferré et al. 2019), histone acetyltransferase (HAT) inhibitors (Neal and Richardson 2018), and SIRT modulators (Moussa et al. 2017). Among these, HDAC inhibitors have been studied in greater depth, whereas the other categories remain exploratory.

The cognitive improvement effects of pan‐HDAC inhibitors in AD animal models have been replicated in numerous studies, which have found restoration of marks such as H3K9ac and modulation of non‐histone substrates including NF‐κB p65 (Diniz et al. 2024; McClarty et al. 2026; Palomer et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2023). However, their clinical translation is limited by the lack of isoform selectivity, lack of cell‐type targeting, and the inability to separate histone from non‐histone effects (Joshi et al. 2025; Li, Izhar, and Kanekiyo 2025). The consequences of HDAC modulation also differ markedly among neural progenitor cells, mature neurons, and glial cells, supporting cell‐type‐aware dosing strategies (Diniz et al. 2025). Pan‐HDAC inhibition is often accompanied by higher neurotoxicity, whereas HDAC6‐selective inhibition shows a better safety window in multiple models; HDAC2 inhibition is associated with improved synaptic plasticity, HDAC3 overactivity with neuronal toxicity, and HDAC5 inhibition may impair memory consolidation (Rodrigues et al. 2020). Therefore, simply pursuing broad‐spectrum HDAC inhibition does not align with the therapeutic logic of cell‐type and chromatin‐state heterogeneity in AD, and pan‐HDAC inhibitors may best serve as pharmacological probes (Asadi et al. 2025; Li, Izhar, and Kanekiyo 2025).

Isoform‐selective inhibitors show better benefit–risk profiles in preclinical studies. The HDAC3 inhibitor RGFP966 ameliorates memory impairment in 5xFAD mice by promoting a microglial M1‐to‐M2 phenotypic switch and increasing the secretion of factors that normalize degenerating axons; its mechanism involves HDAC3 occupancy at the APP promoter and regulation of neuroregeneration‐related transcriptional programs (Davis et al. 2024). The HDAC6 inhibitors Tubastatin A and ACY‐1215 combine anti‐inflammatory activity, microtubule stabilization, and improved axonal transport in AD mouse models, restoring axonal transport function by increasing α‐tubulin acetylation (Pulya et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2014). Multitarget design offers another optional development path: hybrid compounds combining GPR40 agonism with HDAC6 inhibitory activity can modulate tubulin acetylation, ERK phosphorylation, and microglial immune responses in cellular models (Pinheiro, de Chirico, et al. 2025).

In terms of clinical progress, the most advanced test remains the VostatAD01 study of vorinostat (SAHA) (NCT03056495). This Phase Ib trial enrolled nine AD patients before being terminated due to insufficient recruitment (Bondarev et al. 2021; Gupta et al. 2020). The reason for termination is important for interpretation: the trial provided logistical information on feasibility and safety but neither establishes inefficacy nor unacceptable toxicity. From the clinical pipeline, in addition to vorinostat, HDAC‐related agents such as EVP‐0334, RDN‐929, and CKD‐504 have entered early trials for CNS indications, with RDN‐929 targeting the HDAC‐CoREST complex and CKD‐504 being a selective HDAC6 inhibitor. However, it should be noted that the vast majority of human safety data for HDACi come from oncology trials, and the maximum tolerated dose, brain target occupancy, and long‐term safety for CNS indications remain undefined (Rodrigues et al. 2020). All of these compounds still lack cell‐type specificity, and global acetylation gains risk off‐target gene activation; progress hinges on pharmacodynamic biomarkers and patient stratification (Paniri et al. 2024; Rocha et al. 2026).

HMT inhibitors have also achieved mechanistic depth. Among them, GSK126 (EZH2), UNC0642 (G9a), and related agents have been reported to reduce H3K27me3/H3K18me/H3K9me2 and improve synaptic plasticity (Bellver‐Sanchis et al. 2026; Cao et al. 2020; Griñán‐Ferré et al. 2019; Li, Zhao, et al. 2022; Mu et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2023; Williams et al. 2023; Zhang, Wang, Zhou, et al. 2024; Zhong et al. 2026), although most studies are limited to a single model, small sample sizes, and lack BBB or long‐term safety data. For p300/CBP, two mechanistically distinct strategies must be distinguished: C646 inhibits the catalytic acetyltransferase domain, whereas CCS1477 (inobrodib) is a p300/CBP bromodomain inhibitor that blocks reader function rather than acetylation writing (Di Fede et al. 2025; Maity et al. 2021). Thus, the decrease in histone acetylation after CCS1477 treatment reflects loss of bromodomain‐mediated recruitment rather than inhibition of catalytic activity. Both show modest anti‐senescence activity in vitro but face selectivity and toxicity hurdles.

SIRT2 inhibition represents the sirtuin strategy with the most clearly defined locus‐resolved evidence in AD. The SIRT2 inhibitor AGK2 restored H4K16ac levels at the Fzd1/Fzd7 promoters, reactivated Wnt signaling, and rescued synaptic deficits in AD models, including in vivo experiments (Palomer et al. 2022). The therapeutic appeal of this pathway lies in the relative completeness of its causal chain. However, clinical development of SIRT2 inhibitors remains limited: no AD clinical trials have been conducted, and isoform‐specific risk–benefit has not been systematically evaluated. Because sirtuins depend on NAD+, indirect modulation of sirtuin activity via NAD+ precursors may offer a more physiological route of intervention, but the cell‐type‐resolved effects of this strategy remain unclear.

7.2 Metabolic–Epigenetic Interventions

The preceding analysis has established the close relationship between metabolism and epigenetics. The core of metabolic–epigenetic strategies lies in exploiting the direct dependence of histone modifications on metabolic substrates and cofactors (Chilton et al. 2024; Han et al. 2025; Voukia et al. 2026; Yu et al. 2026; Zhang, Yang, Li, and Zhang 2025). Specific interventions include limiting lactate production to constrain lactylation, supplementing NAD+ precursors (NMN, NR), and enhancing ABCA1 to restore ApoE lipidation and correct cholesterol–acetyl‐CoA coupling (AlHayani et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2023; Wang, Wang, et al. 2026). A further advantage is that lactate and NAD+ can be monitored in blood and CSF, and NR/NMN have demonstrated preliminary safety in mild cognitive impairment (Wu, Kupferschmid, et al. 2025; Zhang, Yang, Yu, et al. 2025), making this the clinically most proximate class.

The countervailing weakness, however, is pleiotropy: the relationship between metabolite supply and chromatin state is not a simple matter of “substrate abundance translating directly into increased modification.” Compartmentalization of nuclear metabolite pools, the efficiency of enzyme recruitment, and the rates of modification turnover jointly determine transcriptional output. One framework has proposed a “lactate signal decoding” model that integrates the lactate/pyruvate ratio, cytosolic NADH/NAD+ status, LDH isoenzyme background, proton‐coupled monocarboxylate transport, HCAR1 signaling, and enzymatic/non‐enzymatic lactylation, emphasizing that AD progression may involve a loss of coordination among lactate transport, oxidation, receptor signaling, pH control, and covalent modification rather than an absolute increase in lactate itself (Zaman et al. 2026). Similarly, NAD+ precursor supplementation has shown evidence of ameliorating AD phenotypes in preclinical models, and NR have entered early‐stage clinical evaluation in individuals with mild cognitive impairment, yet their mechanism of action is difficult to attribute to a single histone deacetylation event (Orr et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2021). Sirtuin substrates include histones and non‐histone proteins such as RelA/p65, PGC‐1α, and TFEB, and restoration of NAD+ levels also affects mitochondrial function and DNA damage responses (Vinten et al. 2025). Moreover, chronic manipulation of lactate, NAD+, or lipid metabolism engages numerous physiological processes and lacks cell‐type resolution (Mogol et al. 2024). These interventions are therefore best deployed as biomarker‐guided adjuncts rather than standalone disease modifiers.

7.3 Natural Products

Natural products generally refer to secondary metabolites and endogenous bioactive molecules derived from plants, microorganisms, or marine organisms, encompassing diverse chemical classes such as polyphenols, terpenoids, and alkaloids. Their high structural diversity and broad target range have made them both an important source of drug leads and frequently used pharmacological probes for dissecting signaling pathways and epigenetic regulatory networks. In AD research, natural polyphenols such as hydroxytyrosol, curcumin, and resveratrol have been shown to modulate HDACs, SIRT1, and inflammatory pathways in cellular and animal models, though largely through pleiotropic mechanisms (Fabiani et al. 2021; Hou et al. 2024; Jiao et al. 2025; Li, Wang, et al. 2021; Puranik et al. 2025; Wang, Zhang, et al. 2022). With respect to clinical evidence, the progress of different compounds varies markedly: hydroxytyrosol currently has only preclinical AD evidence, with no completed clinical trial in AD patients (Fabiani et al. 2021); the high‐bioavailability curcumin formulation Theracurmin (180 mg/day for 18 months) improved memory in non‐demented adults and reduced PET‐detected amyloid and tau accumulation (NCT01383161), whereas conventional curcumin formulations failed in a mild‐to‐moderate AD trial (NCT00099710) (Liu et al. 2022; Perluigi et al. 2024); a Phase II trial of resveratrol reported reductions in Aβ and tau deposition in patients with mild‐to‐moderate AD, but the overall evidence has not shown consistent cognitive benefit (Turner et al. 2015). These discrepancies suggest that formulation, dose, exposure level, and study population significantly influence outcomes, and that positive imaging or biomarker signals alone are insufficient to demonstrate that a chromatin mechanism mediates the clinical effect.

The common bottleneck for clinical translation of natural products remains poor oral bioavailability, rapid metabolism, limited BBB penetration, and substantial interindividual pharmacokinetic variability (Chen, Guan, et al. 2025; Puranik et al. 2025). Therefore, a more reasonable current positioning is to regard natural products as mechanistic discovery tools for mapping epigenetic regulatory networks rather than as prioritized AD therapeutic candidates (Kumari and Zeng 2026). Fisetin, for example, has provided conceptual validation for regulatory axes such as HDAC3–NRF2 (Jo et al. 2025). Future advances in nanoparticle delivery, structural optimization, and standardized formulations may improve their drug‐likeness, but in AD they still require rigorous target engagement, locus‐resolved chromatin readouts, and clinical endpoint validation (Kumbhar et al. 2025).

7.4 Epigenome Editing

CRISPR/dCas9‐based epigenome editing represents an intervention logic conceptually distinct from all the strategies discussed above: rather than modulating enzyme activity or metabolite levels, it directs effector domains—such as the p300 acetyltransferase core, the KRAB repressor domain, or the DNMT3A methyltransferase domain—to specific genomic loci. On this basis, it provides the most precise theoretical framework for cell‐type‐ and locus‐specific chromatin manipulation (Khan et al. 2026; Zhou et al. 2026). Fusion of dCas9 with epigenetic effector domains enables targeted regulation of inflammatory genes and enhancers in animal models, demonstrating feasibility for remodeling pathological chromatin states (Chai et al. 2024; Kantor et al. 2024; Khan et al. 2026; Kincaid et al. 2025; Xu et al. 2024). PROTAC‐based protein degradation further offers a complementary strategy for selectively eliminating pathogenic epigenetic enzymes (Rodrigues et al. 2022; Singh et al. 2026).

In AD, APOE4‐targeted epigenome therapy is a representative case: snRNA‐seq‐guided, APOE‐targeted, microglia‐specific epigenome therapy aims to precisely and specifically reduce APOE4 expression in microglia without altering the DNA sequence (Yassine et al. 2025). The design logic of this strategy is directly related to the findings in Section 6.3 regarding APOE isoform‐dependent remodeling of the transcriptional and chromatin accessibility landscape in human microglia: if the pathogenic effects of APOE4 are mediated in part through a specific chromatin state established in microglia, then chromatin editing targeted to the APOE4 locus may offer a better benefit–risk profile than systemic APOE4 reduction.

Broader CRISPR‐based epigenome editing tools are being extended to neuroinflammatory and proteinopathy pathways. One group evaluated the therapeutic potential of CRISPR‐Cas9 and next‐generation gene editing technologies, including base editors and prime editors, in AD, covering targeting of APOE4, APP, PSEN1, PSEN2, and MAPT, as well as strategies using CRISPR interference/activation and epigenome editing tools for allele‐specific correction, gene silencing, and transcriptional regulation (Khan et al. 2026).

However, this field remains largely preclinical. Future efforts must overcome CNS delivery barriers, including engineered AAV vectors, lentiviral systems, lipid nanoparticles, polymeric nanocarriers, exosome delivery, receptor‐mediated transcytosis, immune‐evasive vector design, and focused ultrasound‐mediated BBB modulation (Dou et al. 2025; Onohuean et al. 2025; White et al. 2025).

7.5 Lifestyle and Diet Management

Exercise and dietary interventions indirectly affect nuclear metabolite pools and chromatin enzyme activity by altering systemic metabolic status, and can delay age‐related neuroinflammation (Chai et al. 2026; Gong et al. 2015; Li, Deng, et al. 2025). The strength of this mode of action lies in its physiological and systemic character, but its limitation is the lack of cell‐type resolution—the metabolic signals influenced by exercise or diet (lactate, β‐OHB, NAD+, acetyl‐CoA) reach neurons, microglia, and astrocytes with distinct kinetics and amplitudes, and the cell‐type distribution of their chromatin consequences is heterogeneous (Pang et al. 2026; Wang, Feng, et al. 2026).

Ketone body metabolism provides a concrete example linking lifestyle interventions to specific chromatin modifications. Ketogenic diets and ketone bodies act by inhibiting oxidative stress and modulating histone and non‐histone PTMs (Ravussin et al. 2015). In a randomized trial in mild cognitive impairment, a ketogenic beverage was found to aid cognitive improvement, possibly related to histone β‐hydroxybutyrylation (Fortier et al. 2021; Zhou et al. 2022); ketogenic regimens also modulate autophagy, neurotransmission, and gut microbiota to reduce neuroinflammation (Jang et al. 2023). However, caution is warranted: no clinical study has directly demonstrated that a ketogenic diet alters β‐hydroxybutyrylation levels at specific genomic loci in AD patients, let alone that such changes are causal mediators of cognitive improvement. Lifestyle measures are therefore adjuncts for epigenetic control, but effect sizes, optimal timing, and combination with pharmacological therapy remain to be systematically defined.

The study of exercise‐induced chromatin effects presents a different picture. Exercise raises circulating lactate levels, and lactate may exert neuroprotective effects after crossing the BBB (Gomez‐Pinilla and Thapak 2024); in PD, aquatic exercise improved motor performance and was accompanied by increased peripheral BDNF and histone H4 acetylation, but the small sample size precludes causal conclusions (Monteiro‐Junior et al. 2015). The difficulty in interpreting this finding lies in the fact that the relationship between exercise‐induced lactate elevation and the lactate–lactylation inflammatory program in microglia under AD pathology is complex and potentially opposite in direction. In the physiological context of exercise, lactate as an oxidative substrate and signaling molecule may promote neuroplasticity; in the pathological context of AD, lactate produced by glycolytic reprogramming in microglia drives inflammatory programs through H4K12la (Pan et al. 2022; Zong et al. 2025). Thus, the two therapeutic recommendations—“exercise increases lactate” and “inhibit lactate production to reduce neuroinflammation”—may not be contradictory, but rather reflect the functional heterogeneity of lactate signaling across different cell types, metabolic environments, and timescales. This recognition further reinforces the core argument of this review: changes in metabolite concentration alone are not a sufficient basis for therapeutic decision‐making and must be evaluated in the context of cell type, genomic localization, and downstream transcriptional consequences.

8 Translation and Unresolved Questions

Translating histone‐modification biology into AD diagnostics and therapies requires, above all, a shift from association to causation. The senescence‐centred framework developed in the preceding sections makes that requirement concrete: a mark is mechanistically meaningful only if it can be shown to initiate senescence entry, to sustain SASP output, or neither—if it merely reports these states (Alkhammash and Alotaibi 2025; Liu, Zhang, et al. 2025). We therefore treat three evidentiary tests as the standard for the claims reviewed here. First, perturbation: does locus‐specific alteration of a mark change senescence‐associated transcription? Second, persistence: does the resulting chromatin state survive withdrawal of the initiating stimulus—bona fide epigenetic memory—or does it exist only under continued stimulation? Third, reversibility: does erasing the mark restore homeostatic glial function? Most of the evidence discussed in this Review satisfies none of these tests fully; the technologies and biomarkers considered below, and the agenda that closes this section, are organized around closing that gap (Barberis and Xie 2026; Subasinghe et al. 2025; Yang, Lin, et al. 2026).

8.1 Emerging Technologies: From Descriptive Maps to Causal Inference

Cellular heterogeneity in the AD brain is now resolvable at the level of individual nuclei and of tissue neighborhoods: single‐nucleus ATAC‐seq and RNA‐seq map cell‐type‐specific programmes, spatial transcriptomics registers them against plaques and vessels, and CUT&Tag and low‐input ChIP‐seq profile marks such as H3K27ac, H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 from limited material (Barberis and Xie 2026; Kaya‐Okur et al. 2019). Single‐cell multiregion epigenomics has already shown that chromatin rewiring tracks AD progression and cognitive resilience across brain regions (Liu, Zhang, et al. 2025), and spatial‐CUT&Tag extends mark profiling to intact tissue sections at near‐cellular resolution (Deng et al. 2025). These maps are necessary but not sufficient, because they document association rather than causation. Two complementary developments address the gap. Locus‐targeted editing with CRISPR–dCas9 effectors can deposit or erase a mark at a single regulatory element and read out the transcriptional consequence; in the adult mouse brain this approach has now provided direct causal evidence that editing one locus is sufficient to alter a learned behavior, reversibly within the same animal (Coda et al. 2025). In AD models specifically, targeted methylation of the APP promoter with dCas9–Dnmt3a reduced APP expression, lowered Aβ and attenuated cognitive impairment in vivo, establishing that the approach engages disease‐relevant loci (Park et al. 2022). Spatial metabolomics—MALDI and DESI imaging—can register metabolic gradients of lactate, acetyl‐CoA precursors and lipids against chromatin landscapes around plaques, linking the metabolic nodes of Section 5 to histology (Qiao et al. 2026; Yang et al. 2025). The near‐term priority is integration: perturbation experiments performed in aged, human‐cell‐relevant systems, with mark occupancy and metabolite abundance measured in the same cells. Sample quality, cost and the lack of shared analytical standards remain the main constraints (Khan, Arslanturk, and Draghici 2025).

8.2 Biofluid Biomarkers: From Direct Detection to Indirect Surrogates

Histone marks are nuclear and inaccessible in the living brain, so their direct clinical use as biomarkers is limited (Deng et al. 2025); measurable surrogates are the realistic path. Three classes are being pursued, each with a distinct evidentiary status. Brain‐derived extracellular vesicles—microglial and neuronal exosomes isolated from plasma or CSF—carry cell‐type‐specific protein cargo and potentially chromatin‐associated signatures, and microfluidic isolation of neuron‐ and astrocyte‐derived vesicles has already distinguished Lewy body pathology from AD (Huang et al. 2022; Yang, Lin, et al. 2026). Metabolic intermediates form a second, more tractable class: lactate, NAD+‐related metabolites and acetyl‐CoA‐linked lipid species report the same nodes that drive histone acylation and deacylation, and oral nicotinamide riboside has been shown in humans to raise NAD+ and to alter biomarkers in neuron‐enriched plasma vesicles (Francia et al. 2025; Pourshafie et al. 2026; Vreones et al. 2023). Circulating nucleosomes and cell‐free‐chromatin assays form the third: cell‐free chromatin state tracing can now infer tissue of origin and therapy response from peripheral blood, providing proof of principle that chromatin information is accessible outside the brain (Chen, Meng, et al. 2026; Main et al. 2025). None of these readouts yet reports a specific histone mark at a defined locus in a defined glial cell type, and source specificity, analyte stability and assay standardization remain the decisive hurdles (Aydın et al. 2025). The protein‐based tests against which any chromatin‐derived marker will be judged are already demanding: plasma %p‐tau217 matches or exceeds clinically approved CSF assays for both amyloid and tau pathology (Barthélemy et al. 2024). A realistic trajectory is therefore a composite panel in which vesicle cargo, metabolite ratios and cell‐free chromatin features complement Aβ42/40, p‐tau217 and NfL rather than replace them.

8.3 Comparative Perspectives Across Neurodegenerative Diseases

Chronic neuroinflammation with epigenetic remodeling is not unique to AD: Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and multiple sclerosis all feature glial activation and altered histone‐mark patterns (Li and Barres 2018). Cross‐disease comparison separates shared programmes from disease‐specific signatures. Despite distinct initiating triggers—Aβ and tau, α‐synuclein, TDP‐43, mutant huntingtin—these disorders converge on microglial and astrocytic enhancer reprogramming: H3K27ac gains, increased accessibility at inflammatory loci and transcription‐factor reorganization, with TREM2–APOE, NF‐κB, and interferon pathways as central nodes (Jung et al. 2025; Li, Chen, et al. 2026; Murphy, Ye, et al. 2025). Metabolism‐driven marks, notably lactylation and crotonylation, recur across models, implying shared inflammatory chemistry (Arancibia‐Opazo et al. 2023). Inherited risk sits upstream of these shared programmes: in AD cortex, integrated multi‐omics has linked specific histone‐acetylation and DNA‐methylation alterations to AD risk loci, indicating that genetic susceptibility and chromatin state are not independent (Nativio et al. 2020). A dual‐layer picture follows: shared epigenetic programmes are the highest‐priority targets because interventions against them are most likely to generalize, whereas disease‐specific signatures enable precision stratification (Gopalakrishna et al. 2026; Roy et al. 2025).

The same regulatory logic extends beyond neurodegeneration. Cellular senescence and SASP‐driven inflammation are shared features of chronic inflammatory disease outside the CNS—rheumatoid arthritis, atherosclerosis, inflammatory bowel disease and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease—and histone PTMs have been implicated in each. Metabolite‐derived acylation provides a concrete parallel: in CD4+ T cells from patients with rheumatoid arthritis, succinyl‐CoA accumulation drives BRD2 succinylation and represses the proteasomal subunit PSMB5, coupling a metabolic intermediate to an inflammatory cell fate through chromatin (Wu, Su, et al. 2024). Macrophage histone lactylation, first defined in peripheral innate immunity, likewise operates in both tissue‐reparative and inflammatory programmes across organs (Zhang et al. 2019). Two transferable principles follow. First, the enzymes that install and remove inflammation‐relevant marks—p300/CBP, HDACs, sirtuins and the lysine demethylases—are common to these conditions, so pharmacological lessons on isoform selectivity, cell‐type targeting and biomarker‐guided stratification should be exchanged across fields. Second, disease specificity resides less in the enzymatic machinery than in the cell types, loci and metabolic environments on which it acts; cross‐disease epigenomic atlases of senescent cells would therefore help to distinguish shared, high‐priority targets from context‐specific ones. We anticipate that the senescence–chromatin framework developed here for AD will serve as a template for dissecting chromatin‐encoded inflammation in age‐associated inflammatory disease more broadly.

8.4 Outstanding Questions

The three evidentiary tests set out above define the standard; six concrete problems define the agenda. The first concerns causal hierarchy. Whether a histone‐mark change initiates, maintains, or merely accompanies an inflammatory state can be decided only by locus‐specific perturbation, and CRISPR–dCas9‐based editing has now demonstrated in vivo that targeting a single regulatory locus is sufficient to alter a learned behavior, reversibly within the same animal (Coda et al. 2025); the equivalent experiment at a senescence‐associated enhancer has not yet been performed. The second concerns coverage. Cell‐type‐ and region‐resolved epigenomic maps across disease stages remain incomplete, and the maps that exist concentrate on a small set of marks in bulk or mixed‐cell material (Liu, Zhang, et al. 2025). The third concerns persistence and reversibility. Direct evidence that a mark‐level state can outlive its trigger exists in the CNS but outside the senescence field: a single peripheral inflammatory challenge reprograms microglial enhancers in a manner that persists for at least 6 months and modifies amyloid pathology in an AD mouse model (Wendeln et al. 2018). That precedent shows persistence is achievable and, equally, that it must be measured directly rather than inferred from continued stimulation; longitudinal tracking with stimulus‐withdrawal designs is therefore required (Wu, He, et al. 2025). The fourth concerns network buffering. Crosstalk among marks, and the shared use of writers and erasers, may compensate for single‐target interventions, so network‐level strategies may be required (Lisek et al. 2025). The fifth concerns substrate attribution. The pleiotropy of p300/CBP and of the HDACs makes it difficult to separate histone from non‐histone effects of any pharmacological intervention, and conclusions drawn from enzyme inhibitors alone should be treated accordingly (Wang, Liu, et al. 2026). The sixth concerns combination design. The timing, sequencing, and safety of immunotherapy–metabolic–epigenetic combinations are undefined, and rational design will require pharmacodynamic readouts that report the state of the circuit rather than a single analyte (Ballard et al. 2025). None of these problems is unique to histone biology; what the senescence framework adds is a way to rank them because a mark that fails the persistence test cannot, by definition, be the substrate of the chronic inflammatory state.

9 Conclusion and Perspectives

Chronic neuroinflammation in AD is not merely a downstream consequence of amyloid‐β and tau pathology. In large part, it is the inflammatory signature of cellular senescence, written into and maintained by chromatin states that histone PTMs help establish. This epigenetic layer integrates metabolic dysfunction, mitochondrial impairment, innate immune signaling, and proteotoxic stress into self‐reinforcing transcriptional circuits. It also provides a unified explanation for three features of AD‐associated inflammation that protein‐centric models leave unresolved: its persistence over years, its cell‐type specificity, and its declining reversibility with disease stage.

The central challenge is therefore no longer descriptive annotation but causal attribution: distinguishing the chromatin marks that drive glial senescence from those that merely accompany it, and identifying the chromatin nodes that remain reversible. Three criteria—perturbation, persistence, and reversibility—provide the benchmark. When applied to the current literature, these criteria reveal a consistent pattern. Most AD evidence documents chromatin states under continued pathological stimulation; almost none demonstrates that a state survives withdrawal of the stimulus, and even fewer demonstrate that erasing the mark restores homeostatic function. Claims of irreversibility, or of locked inflammatory states, should therefore be treated as hypotheses, as we do throughout this Review.

Progress will require convergence across three experimental axes: single‐cell and spatial epigenomic mapping of senescent glia and neurons across disease trajectories; cell‐type‐specific perturbation, using CRISPR‐based epigenome editing and locus‐targeted tools, to establish causality at candidate loci; and translational biomarkers—extracellular vesicle cargo, metabolic intermediates and circulating nucleosomes—that report brain chromatin states in accessible biofluids. The same logic indicates where leverage is greatest. Because the marks that sustain senescence‐associated inflammation are deposited by metabolite‐dependent enzymes, the principal metabolic nodes—glycolysis and lactate, acetyl‐CoA, NAD+, and one‐carbon metabolism—are simultaneously mechanisms and measurement points. Likewise, the feedforward circuit linking proteinopathy, immune activation, and chromatin identifies the junctions at which intervention is most likely to be durable. Only through such a closed loop can histone PTMs move from correlative markers to actionable mechanistic nodes.

Therapeutically, epigenetic strategies should complement, not replace, amyloid‐ and tau‐directed approaches, with the goal of dismantling entrenched senescence–SASP circuits and restoring immune homeostasis. Among current options, metabolic–epigenetic interventions that modulate NAD+, lactate and acetyl‐CoA availability offer the most measurable near‐term path; isoform‐selective enzyme inhibitors have the strongest preclinical foundation; and cell‐type‐specific epigenome editing and senomorphic regimens represent the longer‐term precision strategy—although delivery, specificity, safety and monitoring remain substantial constraints in each case.

Ultimately, the question is not simply which marks change in AD, but which are functionally actionable, in which cell type and at which disease stage. If such causal maps can be drawn, chromatin‐encoded cellular senescence may emerge as a therapeutic axis running parallel to protein‐targeted strategies. Histone PTMs are not epigenetic noise: they form a structured molecular language through which the aging brain acquires and maintains its senescent, inflammatory state. Learning to rewrite that language is a central frontier for the biology of aging.
