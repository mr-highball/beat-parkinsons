Background

Parkinson’s disease (PD), the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, is characterized by an unclear etiology and a lack of specific early diagnostic biomarkers. This study investigated novel biomarkers associated with mitochondrial dynamics (MD) dysfunction in PD.

Methods

Transcriptomic data from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (training set: GSE6613, n = 72; validation set: GSE99039, n = 438) and 23 MD-related genes (MDRGs) were integrated. Differentially expressed genes were screened using the limma package. Three machine learning algorithms (random forest, support vector machine, and generalized linear model) were employed to construct diagnostic models. Feature importance analysis identified key candidate biomarkers, which were preliminarily validated in clinical samples via reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). Functional enrichment, immune infiltration, and compound prediction analyses were subsequently performed.

Results

Cysteinyl leukotriene receptor 2 (CYSLTR2) and alpha‑tocopherol transfer protein‑like gene (TTPAL) were identified as MDRG score-correlated biomarkers. In the validation set, CYSLTR2 was significantly downregulated (log2FC = −1.25, P < 0.001) and participated in neuroactive ligand–receptor interactions. Conversely, TTPAL was significantly upregulated (log2FC = 1.52, P < 0.001) and associated with the pathogenic Escherichia coli infection pathway. Immune analysis revealed six differentially infiltrated immune cell types. Notably, TTPAL expression was positively correlated with keratinocytes (cor = 0.30, P = 0.031). Four potential therapeutic compounds were predicted. RT-qPCR suggested the downregulation of CYSLTR2 and upregulation of TTPAL in patients with PD (P < 0.0001).

Conclusion

This study identified CYSLTR2 and TTPAL as novel candidate biomarkers associated with MD dysfunction in PD, providing novel targets for mechanistic investigation and therapeutic exploration.

1 Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disease after Alzheimer’s disease, and its global burden increases markedly with population aging [1]. PD is predominantly characterized by motor symptoms, including bradykinesia, resting tremor, muscular rigidity, postural instability, and gait disturbances. These debilitating symptoms markedly impair patients’ quality of life and constitute the core clinical diagnostic criteria for PD. Non-motor symptoms, such as olfactory dysfunction and constipation, frequently precede motor symptoms and may represent early indicators of PD, even in its preclinical stage [2,3]. Additionally, the etiology of PD is complex and multifactorial. Accumulating evidence indicates that PD pathogenesis is driven by numerous interwoven contributors, such as genetic predisposition, environmental exposure, and aging-related factors [4]. Moreover, the role of neuroinflammation in PD progression has gained increasing attention [5].

Mitochondria are widely recognized as the “energy factories” of cells and are indispensable for neuronal function and survival. Mitochondrial dynamics (MD) encompass processes including fusion, fission, and mitochondrial transport, which cooperate with biogenesis and mitophagy to maintain mitochondrial turnover. Dysregulated MD can lead to structural and functional abnormalities of mitochondria, which in turn shift the cellular state toward a disease-prone phenotype [6]. Consequently, MD has emerged as a cornerstone of mitochondrial quality control. Accumulating evidence substantiates the pivotal role of MD in the pathogenesis of neurological disorders, such as Alzheimer’s disease, Huntington disease, and PD [7]. Notably, fragmentation and functional impairment driven by dysregulated mitochondrial dynamics hinder mitochondrial recognition and clearance by the autophagic machinery, thereby exacerbating mitophagy defects. This mitophagic impairment has been demonstrated to play a major role in the loss of dopaminergic neurons in PD [8], leading to the widespread acknowledgment that mitochondrial dysfunction is a key pathological marker of PD [9]. Nevertheless, the precise mechanistic underpinnings of MD dysfunction in PD initiation and progression remain to be elucidated.

Owing to the complexity of its etiology and pathogenesis, the current clinical diagnosis of PD primarily relies on clinical manifestations. Among available imaging approaches, [18F]DOPA positron emission tomography (PET) can characterize diminished striatal dopaminergic function [10]. Nevertheless, the technical complexity and high cost of PET preclude its application in population-wide early screening [11]. However, by the time typical motor manifestations emerge and a definitive PD diagnosis is established, available therapies can relieve symptoms but not halt or reverse disease progression. Therefore, molecular biomarkers for the early identification of PD are urgently needed to facilitate timely intervention before overt motor manifestations appear. Notably, high-throughput and multi-omic technologies have advanced precision medicine and mechanism-driven drug discovery for neurodegenerative diseases through genome-level targeting of core pathological cascades, characterization of mitochondrial molecular disorders, and improved molecular diagnosis of mitochondrial-related diseases [12–14]. Via integrated analysis of the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets and MD-related genes (MDRGs), this study aims to identify novel biomarkers associated with MD dysfunction in PD and systematically elucidate their potential regulatory mechanisms. These findings will offer new insights into the pathological mechanisms underlying PD and highlight novel targets for mechanistic investigation and therapeutic exploration.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data collection

Transcriptomic datasets were retrieved from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) on June 15, 2025. All datasets used in this study were fully anonymized, and no personally identifiable information of individual participants was accessible to the authors. The GSE6613 dataset was used as the training set for biomarker screening. The sequencing platform was GPL96, and this dataset contained whole-blood samples from 50 patients with PD and 22 healthy controls. The GSE99039 dataset was used as completely independent external validation cohort to assess the generalizability of findings. The sequencing platform was GPL570, and this dataset contained peripheral blood samples from 205 patients with PD and 233 healthy controls. Twenty-three MDRGs were obtained from the MitoCarta 3.0 database [15]: ARMC10, ARL2, DNM1L (also termed Drp1), MFN1, FIS1, MFN2, MFF, MIGA1, MIEF1, MIGA2, MIEF2, MTCH2, MTFP1, MTFR1, OPA1, MTFR2, PLD6, MUL1, OMA1, RAB24, SLC25A46, SPIRE1, and STX17.

2.2 Identification of candidate genes

The limma package (v3.44.3) [16] was employed to perform differential expression analysis between PD and control samples in the training set to obtain differentially expressed genes (DEGs). DEGs were filtered according to the thresholds of P < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.5. Additionally, the ggplot2 (v3.3.2) [17] and ComplexHeatmap [18] packages were employed to visualize the volcano map and heatmap of DEGs, respectively. Subsequently, based on the MDRG list, the transcriptomic matrix of the GSE6613 dataset was converted into a score matrix using the gene set variation analysis (GSVA) algorithm. Differences in GSVA scores between PD patients and healthy controls were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. PD samples were stratified into high- and low-score groups based on the median GSVA score. Thresholds of P < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.5 were used to screen DEGs associated with MDRGs. Finally, the DEGs between PD patients and healthy controls and those associated with MDRGs were intersected to obtain candidate genes.

2.3 Enrichment analysis of candidate genes and construction of the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network

To investigate the potential functional pathways of candidate genes, these genes were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses using the clusterProfiler package (v3.16.0), with a threshold of P < 0.05 [19,20]. The top 15 significantly enriched pathways were obtained. To further investigate the interactions among candidate genes at the protein level, a PPI network (combined score > 0.4) was constructed using the STRING database (http://string-db.org/) [21] and visualized using Cytoscape (v3.9.1) [22].

2.4 Machine learning screening for biomarkers

Prior to machine learning modeling, uniform preprocessing was performed on raw gene expression data, including missing value removal, quantile normalization, and batch effect correction to eliminate nonbiological experimental noise. In the present study, the GSE6613 dataset was used as the training cohort for model construction, while the independent dataset GSE99039 served as the external validation cohort. Given the relatively limited sample size of the training set, five-fold stratified cross-validation was implemented for all classification models to balance the proportions of PD patient and healthy control samples and minimize bias attributable to random grouping. Hyperparameter tuning for all models was completed via the built-in grid search algorithm of the caret package (v6.0.49) [23], and regularization constraints were incorporated to reduce model complexity and substantially mitigate overfitting.

Random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), and generalized linear model (GLM) were employed using the caret package for the first‑round modeling. Preliminary candidate genes were set as explanatory variables, while disease status (PD versus healthy control) was defined as the response variable.

The explain function of the DALEX package (v2.4.3) [24] was used to generate explainer objects for the three models. Residual distributions were calculated from observed and predicted values, and permutation-based variable importance was used to quantify the contribution of each explanatory variable. For consistent comparison, the same residual definition and evaluation framework were applied to RF, SVM, and GLM. The RF model with the lowest overall residual error was selected as the optimal model. Permutation-based variable importance scores were computed for all three models via DALEX, and scores derived from the optimal RF model were adopted to select the top 10 genes as candidate biomarkers.

To verify whether the expression trends of the candidate biomarkers were consistent between the training and validation sets, the limma package (v3.44.3) was used to analyze biomarker expression (P < 0.05). Only candidate biomarkers exhibiting consistent and statistically significant expression trends in both datasets were retained as identified biomarkers.

Having obtained the two final identified biomarkers, we performed second‑round modeling to evaluate their disease‑status classification ability by constructing three machine‑learning classifiers (RF, GLM, and SVM) using their expression matrix. Disease status (control/disease) was used as the classification label, while the expression levels of these two identified biomarkers were used as input features. The three models were trained separately using the R package caret. Subsequently, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate the classification performance of the different machine-learning models on the training dataset. The area under the curve (AUC) and its 95% confidence interval (95% CI) were calculated using the pROC package to compare the discriminatory ability of the different algorithms between disease and control samples. Furthermore, 5‑fold stratified cross‑validation was performed within the GSE6613 training cohort in this second‑round modeling as an additional analysis to maintain balanced PD‑control sample ratios across folds and reduce bias from single data splitting. Specifically, the cross-validation framework implemented in the R package caret was used to retrain and evaluate the RF, SVM, and GLM models. During cross-validation, model-specific parameter tuning was performed within the training folds to determine the corresponding model parameters and reduce the variability associated with a single model fit. After model construction and internal five‑fold stratified cross‑validation within GSE6613, the trained RF, SVM and GLM classification models were directly applied to the fully independent external validation cohort GSE99039. Receiver‑operating characteristic curve analysis was performed to calculate AUC values for evaluating the generalizability of these machine‑learning classifiers.

2.5 Nomogram construction and assessment of biomarker performance

To further evaluate the predictive performance of the biomarkers for PD, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted for both the training and validation sets using the pROC package (with an AUC greater than 0.7 indicating good predictive accuracy). Meanwhile, to assess the diagnostic value of these biomarkers for PD, a scoring system was constructed based on the biomarker expression levels in the training set. Each biomarker was assigned an individual score, and the sum of these individual scores yielded the total score, which was subsequently used to predict the diagnostic probability of PD. Higher total scores corresponded to an increased probability of PD diagnosis. A biomarker-based nomogram was constructed using the rms package. The predictive performance of the nomogram was comprehensively assessed via calibration curves, ROC curve analysis, and decision curve analysis (DCA).

2.6 Enrichment analysis of biomarkers

The potential pathways of the biomarkers were investigated. For each biomarker, Spearman’s correlation coefficients were calculated between the biomarker and all other genes across samples. Genes were ranked in descending order of correlation coefficients to generate pre-ranked gene lists. Subsequently, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) [25] was performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.8.3) [26], with KEGG gene sets as the reference gene collection [20]. The number of permutations was set to 10,000, and the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied for correction. Pathways with FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 were defined as significantly enriched (|normalized enrichment score| > 1). The leading-edge genes of all significantly enriched pathways were extracted simultaneously.

2.7 Immunocyte infiltration analysis

To evaluate immune cell infiltration in PD, infiltration scores of 64 immune cell types in the training set were calculated using the xCell algorithm (v1.1.0) [27]. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to identify the immune cells with differential infiltration between PD and control samples (P < 0.05), and the results were visualized as a box plot using the ggplot2 package (v3.3.2). After identifying differentially infiltrated immune cell subtypes, Spearman’s correlation was analyzed using the stats package and visualized using the corrplot package (v0.92) [28], with predefined screening thresholds of |cor| > 0.3 and P < 0.05.

2.8 Regulatory networks of biomarkers and transcription factors (TFs)

We retrieved potential transcription factors (TFs) and microRNAs (miRNAs) targeting biomarkers through the NetworkAnalyst platform (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/). TF–gene binding profiles were derived from the JASPAR database, and miRNA–gene interaction data were extracted from the miRTarBase database. The TF–biomarker regulatory network and the integrated TF–miRNA–biomarker regulatory network were visualized and refined using Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1).

2.9 Compound prediction and molecular docking

To screen potential compounds for PD treatment, the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (https://ctdbase.org/) was employed to predict small-molecule compounds acting on the biomarkers. The top 20 compounds were retained for further analysis, and the biomarker–compound interaction network was constructed using Cytoscape (v3.9.1) to identify potential therapeutic compounds for PD. Subsequently, molecular docking was conducted via CB-Dock2 (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/php/index.php) [29] to evaluate binding affinities between the compounds and their target biomarker proteins. Three-dimensional structures of the compounds were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), while high-resolution receptor structures of the biomarker proteins were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB, https://www.rcsb.org/). CurPocket was used to predict the top five potential binding pockets, with AutoDock Vina serving as the underlying docking engine. Heteroatoms and water molecules were removed from receptor structures, and grid boxes were defined based on the geometric boundaries of predicted cavities. Docking calculations were repeated multiple times, and conformations with root‑mean‑square deviation < 5 Å were retained. For benchmarking purposes, the endogenous CYSLTR2 ligands leukotriene C4 (LTC4), leukotriene D4 (LTD4), and leukotriene E4 (LTE4) were docked under identical computational parameters, using the same receptor structure, pocket prediction, and calculation workflow. For all docking poses derived from both candidate compounds and endogenous ligands, key interacting residues and ligand–protein contact distances were analyzed and quantified. All docking outcomes were ranked according to Vina binding scores and visualized using PyMOL software.

2.10 Subcellular localization and gene–gene interaction (GGI) network analysis

To characterize the biological functions of proteins encoded by the candidate biomarkers, we explored their subcellular locations to identify their functional compartments. Subcellular localization information of biomarkers was retrieved from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/). In addition, GeneMANIA (https://genemania.org/) was used to collect genes with similar functional profiles to the target biomarkers, and a gene–gene interaction (GGI) network was built from these functional associations.

2.11 Validation of biomarker expression

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was employed to confirm the consistency of candidate biomarker expression with the bioinformatic results. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Nanjing (approval No.: 2025-LS-ky066, date: June 10, 2025). Clinical samples were obtained for research purposes on August 25, 2025. No personally identifiable information of participants was accessible during and after data collection. All samples were collected from the Second Hospital of Nanjing, and all donors provided written informed consent. Specifically, whole-blood samples were collected from five patients with PD and five healthy individuals. Total RNA was extracted from each sample using the TRIzol reagent (Cat. No. 15596018CN, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and cDNA was synthesized using the SweScript First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Cat. No. G3333-50, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). GAPDH was employed as an internal reference gene, and the 2−ΔΔCt method [30] was used to calculate candidate biomarker expression. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1. The resultant data were statistically analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism (v8.0).

2.12 Statistical analysis

Bioinformatic analyses were performed using R (v4.2.2). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Student’s t-test were used for comparisons between two groups. Fisher’s exact test was applied to calculate the significance of enriched terms in GO and KEGG pathway analyses, and P < 0.05 was defined as the threshold for statistically significant results.

2.13 Ethical approval and consent to participate

This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The relevant ethical approval information has been detailed in Section 2.11.

3 Results

3.1 Identification of candidate genes related to MD in PD

In total, 221 DEGs were obtained from the GSE6613 dataset using the predefined screening thresholds, consisting of 121 upregulated and 100 downregulated genes (Fig 1A). Distinct expression patterns of the top 20 DEGs were observed between PD and control samples (Fig 1B). The MDRG scores of PD samples were lower than those of control samples (Fig 1C). Subsequently, PD samples were stratified into high- and low-score groups based on the median MDRG score (−0.08153), with 25 PD samples in each group. After screening using the predefined thresholds, 381 DEGs associated with MDRGs were obtained between the high- and low-score groups (Fig 1D). Similarly, the upregulated and downregulated MDRGs also exhibited different expression profiles (Fig 1E). Finally, the 221 DEGs and 381 MDRG-related DEGs derived from the training set were intersected, yielding 17 candidate genes (Fig 1F).

3.2 Functional and interactive characterization of candidate genes

Potential functional pathways associated with the candidate genes were explored via GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. In the GO enrichment results, candidate genes were mainly enriched in transcription elongation by mitochondrial RNA polymerase (GO:0006392), O-acyltransferase activity (GO:0008374), and polymeric immunoglobulin receptor-mediated immunoglobulin transport in epithelial cells (GO:0002415; Fig 2A). In the KEGG enrichment results, candidate genes were mainly enriched in steroid biosynthesis (hsa00100), terpene skeleton biosynthesis (hsa00900), and α-linolenic acid metabolism (hsa00592; Fig 2B). Thus, these candidate genes might be closely correlated with PD pathogenesis and the MD dysfunction-related mechanism. Furthermore, seven proteins encoded by the candidate genes interacted with each other within the PPI network (RAB17, RIPK4, C2CD2, TEFM, TRMT13, PLA2G4C, and SOAT2), and four interaction pairs were identified (Fig 2C).

3.3 MD dysfunction-related biomarkers in PD

Model‑level performance across RF, SVM, and GLM was compared using unified residual metrics implemented in DALEX (Fig 3A). The RF model yielded the smallest model residuals calculated using the DALEX package (evaluated via the reverse cumulative distribution of absolute residuals), and was selected as the optimal model (Fig 3B–C). Permutation‑based variable importance for explanatory variables was calculated for all three models (Fig 3A), and feature importance outputs from the optimal RF model were adopted for biomarker screening. Using these RF‑derived importance scores, the top 10 genes [PDSS2, cysteinyl leukotriene receptor 2 (CYSLTR2), LINC00302, RAB17, RIPK4, ARHGAP12, C2CD2, TRMT13, SOAT2, and alpha‑tocopherol transfer protein‑like (TTPAL)] were selected as candidate biomarkers (Fig 3D). Subsequent validation in the training and validation sets (P < 0.05) revealed that CYSLTR2 was significantly downregulated in PD samples, whereas TTPAL was significantly upregulated (Fig 3E–F), establishing these two genes as biomarkers associated with MD dysfunction. In addition, RF, GLM, and SVM classification models were constructed based on the expression profiles of the identified biomarkers, and ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate their classification performance. The results showed that all three machine-learning models exhibited good discriminatory ability within the training set. Among them, the random forest model achieved the highest classification performance, with an AUC of 1.000 (95% CI: 1.000–1.000). The support vector machine model achieved an AUC of 0.997 (95% CI: 0.991–1.000), while the generalized linear model achieved an AUC of 0.990 (95% CI: 0.975–1.000) (Fig 3G). Furthermore, 5-fold stratified cross-validation was performed to further assess model stability. The results showed that all three models retained good classification performance under 5-fold cross-validation. Specifically, the cross-validated AUC was 0.839 (95% CI: 0.786–0.891) for the RF model, 0.818 (95% CI: 0.713–0.923) for the GLM model, and 0.925 (95% CI: 0.887–0.962) for the SVM model (Fig 3H). These findings indicated that the models constructed using the candidate genes maintained a certain degree of discriminatory ability under cross-validation, lending support to the robustness of our candidate biomarker screening. It should be noted that the near‑perfect apparent AUC values observed in the training set, accompanied by relatively lower cross‑validated AUCs, indicate potential model overfitting. Thus, the predictive performance estimated from the training set should be interpreted with caution. Notably, classification performance dropped substantially in the fully independent external cohort GSE99039, which highlights limitations for the direct clinical diagnostic application of these models.

3.4 Diagnostic performance of individual genes and combined nomogram model

CYSLTR2 and TTPAL yielded AUCs ranging from 0.549 to 0.667 in the training (S1A Fig) and validation sets (S1B Fig), indicating limited discriminatory performance for either gene alone. A combined predictive model was constructed using CYSLTR2 and TTPAL, and the corresponding nomogram was generated (S1C Fig). The calibration curve revealed that the predicted values closely agreed with the observed values, demonstrating favorable predictive consistency for the model (S1D Fig). Meanwhile, the AUC for the model reached 0.707, indicating that it exhibited reliable diagnostic efficacy (S1E Fig). DCA further confirmed that the model achieved higher clinical net benefit and superior overall predictive performance (S1F Fig).

3.5 Functional enrichment and immune microenvironment analysis

GSEA based on the KEGG database illustrated that CYSLTR2 was significantly enriched in 16 pathways, including the ribosome, spliceosome, and neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction (Fig 4A). TTPAL was involved in the ribosome and pathogenic Escherichia coli infection pathways. Specifically, the pathogenic E. coli infection pathway was downregulated, and the ribosomal pathway tended to be upregulated (Fig 4B). Thus, these two biomarkers might participate in the onset and development of PD via these important pathways. The infiltration scores of 64 immune cell types were calculated for patients with PD and healthy controls in the training set using the xCell algorithm (v1.1.0; Fig 4C). The results revealed six differential immune cell types (P < 0.05): gamma-delta T (γδ T) cells, natural killer T (NKT) cells, naïve CD8+ T cells, keratinocytes, common lymphoid progenitor cells, and CD8+ T cells (Fig 4D). The strongest negative correlation was observed between CD8+ T cells and NKT cells (cor = −0.334, P = 0.004), while the strongest positive correlation existed between naïve CD8+ T cells and CD8+ T cells (cor = 0.562, P < 0.0001; Fig 4E). Regarding the interactions between the two biomarkers and immune cell subtypes, the strongest positive correlation was detected between TTPAL and keratinocytes (cor = 0.300, P = 0.031; Fig 4F), suggesting that these biomarkers might influence the initiation and progression of PD by remodeling the immune microenvironment.

3.6 The possibility of targeted biomarker regulation

The results indicated that CYSLTR2 and TTPAL potentially interacted with nine TFs, and both genes were projected to interact with E2F1 and TP53. Subsequently, 22 miRNAs targeting CYSLTR2 and 58 miRNAs targeting TTPAL were identified. Following these analyses, the integrated TF–miRNA–biomarker regulatory network was constructed and contained 98 nodes and 98 edges (Fig 5A). These findings supplied a basis for the targeted regulation of biomarkers.

3.7 Compound prediction and molecular docking of the two biomarkers

To identify potential therapeutic agents for PD, interactions between the biomarkers and available therapeutic agents were explored. The top 20 predicted small-molecule compounds were selected to construct a biomarker–compound interaction network. This network featured 38 nodes and 40 edges, and four compounds [amphetamine, bisphenol A, cellulose, and benzo[a]pyrene] interacted with CYSLTR2 and TTPAL (Fig 5B), thus representing potential therapeutic compounds of interest. Molecular docking was employed to determine the binding of compounds to biomarkers. Among the CTD-predicted compounds, amphetamine was selected for illustrative docking because of its documented effects on striatal dopamine release in PD [31], and the availability of a CYSLTR2 protein structure. Since only the 3D structure of CYSLTR2 was available in the PDB database, CYSLTR2 was employed as the receptor. To compare the predicted binding capacity of amphetamine with that of classical endogenous ligands of CYSLTR2, LTC4 (Fig 5C), LTD4 (Fig 5D), and LTE4 (Fig 5E) were further selected and subjected to molecular docking using the CB-Dock2 platform under the same conditions. The predicted binding energy of amphetamine with CYSLTR2 was −6.6 kcal/mol, whereas those of LTC4, LTD4, and LTE4 were −8.9, −7.7, and −7.8 kcal/mol, respectively, indicating that amphetamine exhibited weaker predicted binding than the endogenous ligands. Further analysis of the binding mode showed that amphetamine could enter the predicted binding pocket of CYSLTR2 and interact with residue SER169 at a distance of 2.6 Å (Fig 5F). The predicted binding energies, key interacting residues, and interaction distances for amphetamine and the three endogenous ligands are summarized in Table 2. These findings suggest a potential structural interaction between amphetamine and CYSLTR2; however, this conclusion is based solely on in silico predictions and requires further experimental validation.

4 Discussion

PD is a common age-related neurodegenerative disorder [1]. Mitochondrial dysfunction drives pathological phenotypes in diverse diseases; oncology studies show that disrupted mitochondrial dynamics regulate cell fate, immune function, and disease progression [32,33]. Whereas prior studies have implicated MD in the pathogenesis of neurological diseases [7], the underlying molecular mechanisms driving PD progression remain unclear. Nevertheless, mitochondria-targeted therapies are garnering growing interest in neurodegeneration [34], while some signaling pathways governing mitochondrial homeostasis have emerged as promising potential drug targets for PD [35]. In this study, we integrated GEO transcriptome datasets with established MDRGs. Through comprehensive bioinformatic analyses, we identified two novel biomarkers, elucidated their associated pathways, and provided novel insights into the pathological mechanisms of PD, offering potential targets for mechanistic investigation and therapeutic exploration.

In this study, CYSLTR2 and TTPAL were identified as biologically significant biomarkers whose expression levels exhibited significant correlations with MDRG-derived MD scores in PD. CYSLTR2 (GeneID: 57105) encodes a G protein-coupled receptor that mediates cellular responses to cysteine leukotrienes and serves as an endogenous ceramide receptor. Ceramides, as bioactive lipids, are involved in cell survival and cellular stress responses. Emerging evidence indicates that CYSLTR2 plays a pivotal role in regulating human immune responses and inflammatory cascades. Thus, CYSLTR2 is regarded as a key regulator of immune-inflammatory processes in humans [36]. Preclinical evidence has implicated cysteinyl-leukotriene signaling, including CysLT2R, in intestinal inflammation [37]. A recent cardiovascular study demonstrated that CYSLTR2, acting as an endogenous membrane receptor for C16:0 ceramide, activates Gq protein upon ceramide binding. This activation promotes inflammasome activation and exacerbates atherosclerotic plaque formation [38]. In neurology, studies have indicated that CYSLTR2 is upregulated in neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease, positioning it as a potential therapeutic target [39]. Interestingly, CYSLTR2 was significantly downregulated in both bioinformatic datasets and clinical PD samples compared with controls in this study, suggesting its potential heterogeneous roles across distinct neurodegenerative diseases. Notably, GSEA revealed that CYSLTR2 was significantly enriched in the neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction pathway. This pathway encompasses a diverse repertoire of receptors and is critical for physiological processes such as motor control, synaptic transmission, neural excitation–inhibition balance, and learning–memory consolidation. The downregulation of CYSLTR2 might disrupt neuroactive ligand–receptor crosstalk, potentially contributing to the initiation and progression of PD. Although CYSLTR2 represents a promising therapeutic target, its clinical translatability requires validation via large-scale clinical trials and mechanistic investigations.

The protein encoded by TTPAL (GeneID: 79183) belongs to the tocopherol-transfer protein family. Tocopherol (α), a potent antioxidant, protects cells from oxidative stress-induced damage. Existing studies on TTPAL have predominantly focused on oncology. In 2014, TTPAL was identified as a candidate oncogene [40], with its overexpression serving as a biomarker for breast cancer [41] and a prognostic indicator for gastric and colorectal cancers [42–44]. GSEA revealed that TTPAL was significantly enriched in the pathogenic E. coli infection pathway. Intriguingly, patients with PD frequently exhibit intestinal flora dysregulation, with increased relative abundance of pathogenic E. coli strains reported in multiple studies [3,45,46]. Such gut-microbiota dysregulation can compromise intestinal-barrier integrity, facilitating the translocation of intestinal pathogens and their metabolites (e.g., lipopolysaccharides) into the bloodstream [47]. This process may subsequently trigger a systemic inflammatory response [48]. Gut dysbiosis, such as pathogenic E. coli overgrowth, can activate the intestinal immune system and release pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6). Consistent with the gut–brain axis hypothesis, such peripheral inflammatory signals can compromise the blood-brain barrier and induce neuroinflammation [49]. Within the central nervous system, pro-inflammatory cytokines activate microglia, induce neuroinflammation, and ultimately contribute to dopaminergic-neuron damage [50]. We hypothesize that TTPAL overexpression might disrupt neuronal homeostasis by altering neuronal lipid metabolism, thereby interfering with neurotransmitter synthesis, storage, and release. For instance, dysregulation of lipid homeostasis in dopaminergic neurons impairs dopamine-vesicle packaging and release efficiency, thereby disrupting neural signal transmission and contributing to PD progression. Additionally, this imbalance might compromise neuronal survival and repair mechanisms, rendering neurons more susceptible to damage from oxidative stress and inflammation and accelerating neurodegenerative processes.

By constructing a GGI network, we found that CYSLTR2 and TTPAL interact with members of the GNAQ subfamily (GNA14, GNA15, and GNA11). Previous studies have confirmed that the signaling pathways activated by the GNAQ subfamily can modulate intracellular calcium levels and protein kinase C (PKC) activity [51]. Alterations in calcium-related signaling can further reshape downstream cellular networks [52]; subsequent alterations in calcium levels can alter the function of proteins involved in neurotransmitter release, thereby influencing dopamine release [53]. PKC participates in dopamine-receptor signaling and can regulate dopamine-receptor downregulation and dopamine-transporter internalization; altered PKC signaling has also been implicated in PD [54]. Collectively, these findings suggest that GNAQ-subfamily members can modulate dopamine transporter or receptor function, thereby influencing the dopamine-transport signaling pathway. In recent years, accumulating studies have provided insights into the relationship between the dopamine-signaling pathway and PD. In mouse models, high-frequency deep brain stimulation alleviated parkinsonian motor deficits by desynchronizing GABA release [55]. Additionally, stem-cell-based technology is being explored to replace degenerated dopaminergic neurons within the substantia nigra, with the goal of achieving sustained therapeutic benefits [56]. Overall, these findings provide a basis for elucidating the hierarchical regulatory architecture and feedback mechanisms of the biomarkers, and offer a rationale for targeting the dopamine-receptor signaling pathway to modulate PD progression.

Previous studies have established that PD is characterized by chronic inflammation, with neuroinflammation playing a pivotal role in its pathogenesis. In this study, immune-infiltration analysis revealed six differentially abundant immune-cell types between the PD and control groups, indicating profound alterations in the immune microenvironment in PD. Post-mortem studies in patients with Lewy body disease have revealed increased T-cell infiltration associated with neuroinflammation [57]. Consistently, animal models demonstrate that NKT-cell infiltration drives this inflammatory response, and immunotherapy targeting CD1d can effectively alleviate neurodegeneration [58]. In addition, CD8+ T cells play an important role in PD-related pathological processes. First, immunohistochemical analysis of autopsied brain tissues from patients with PD has demonstrated the infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells in the substantia nigra. One neuropathological study suggested that such infiltration may occur prior to detectable α-synuclein aggregation and neuronal death, raising the possibility that CD8+ T cells might initiate neuroinflammation at an early pathological stage and contribute to disease onset [59]. Second, single-cell transcriptome and TCR profiling has revealed activated and expanded T-cell populations in PD [60]. In addition, T cells from patients with PD can recognize α-synuclein-derived peptides and mount helper and cytotoxic immune responses [61]. Patients with PD have been reported to exhibit reduced frequencies of memory CD8+ T cells, whereas mechanistic experiments suggest that mitophagy contributes to memory CD8 + T-cell formation and survival [62].

This study revealed robust interactions among γδ T cells, NKT cells, and CD8+ T cells, suggesting their joint involvement in the progression of PD. Notably, CYSLTR2 and TTPAL expression was correlated with the abundance of CD8+ T cells and naïve CD8+ T cells, suggesting that these biomarkers might influence PD pathogenesis by modulating the infiltration patterns and compositional balance of specific immune‑cell subsets and subsequently altering the host immune microenvironment. If validated, these associations could inform therapeutic strategies targeting biomarker expression to normalize immune-cell profiles, stabilize neuroinflammation, and ultimately intervene in PD progression.

This study, based on the NetworkAnalyst platform, elucidated the complex regulatory axes governing these biomarkers. E2F1 and TP53 were predicted to jointly regulate the expression of both biomarkers. Studies have found that these TFs play key roles in processes such as cell-cycle regulation, DNA-damage repair, and apoptosis. As a key regulatory factor of the cell cycle, E2F1 not only promotes DNA replication but also plays a crucial role in maintaining genomic stability by facilitating DNA damage repair [63]. In a study of cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury (CIRI), E2F1-mediated CDK5 activation induced mitochondrial fission while suppressing mitophagy in microglia, thereby triggering inflammation and neuronal apoptosis and exacerbating CIRI severity [64]. E2F1 is known to modulate neuronal apoptosis [65], and existing studies indicate that this pathway plays a critical role in mediating neuronal death specifically in PD models [66]. It is hypothesized that E2F1 promotes PD progression by modulating genes associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby disrupting the respiratory chain and MD. Collectively, these findings provide a basis for achieving controllable therapy by targeting these biomarkers.

Compound screening analysis further underscored the clinical potential of these biomarkers. Among the 36 chemical compounds predicted in this study, 20 exhibited toxic properties, 2 possessed ambiguous functional implications, and 2 represented normal physiological and biochemical compounds endogenous to humans. The remaining 12 compounds exhibited no toxicity or tolerable toxicity profiles and had well-established clinical applications. These agents affect expectoration, diuresis, central nervous system excitation, and immunosuppression, and exert anti-pyretic–analgesic, antioxidant, anti-cancer, and other pharmacological effects. Thus, they represent potential therapeutic agents. Current research indicates that cellulose, catechins, and anti-inflammatory and antioxidant agents exhibit fewer adverse effects. Among these compounds, cellulose effectively targets both biomarkers. Amphetamine can trigger the release of endogenous dopamine in the ventral striatum and medial orbitofrontal cortex. In the context of Parkinson's disease, this dopaminergic response is specifically linked to the development of impulsive-compulsive behavior [31]. However, amphetamine has recognized potential for misuse and may cause neurotoxic effects at high doses, which limits its therapeutic applicability [67].

5 Conclusions

By integrating GEO transcriptome data with MDRGs, this study identified two novel biomarkers (CYSLTR2 and TTPAL) associated with MD dysfunction in PD. We systematically characterized these biomarkers, thereby deepening our understanding of PD pathology in the context of MD dysfunction. These findings provide biologically significant targets for dissecting PD pathophysiology and advancing hypothesis‑driven experimental investigations. However, several limitations exist in the present study. First, our machine‑learning models showed limited performance in the independent external cohort, which precludes their use as clinical diagnostic tools. Second, the clinical sample size utilized for RT-qPCR validation was relatively small. Insufficient sample size could reduce statistical power, and the observed expression trends of downregulated CYSLTR2 and upregulated TTPAL might be subject to statistical bias arising from the limited number of samples.

Given the relatively limited sample size of GSE6613, further splitting it into a training subset and an internal test subset would substantially reduce the sample size available for both model training and testing, which may increase the instability of model performance estimation. Therefore, we did not create a fixed‑proportion hold‑out test subset within GSE6613. Instead, 5‑fold stratified cross‑validation was performed in this cohort for internal evaluation, while GSE99039 was used as an independent external test cohort. This design balances full utilization of training data and independent assessment of model generalizability. Specifically, disease status (PD versus healthy control) was defined as the binary outcome, and expression levels of candidate biomarkers were used as input features. Random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), and generalized linear model (GLM) were constructed separately. Five‑fold stratified cross‑validation maintains roughly balanced proportions of PD and healthy control samples within each fold, thereby reducing bias caused by a single random data split. Descriptions of this cross‑validation strategy have been added to the Methods section of the revised manuscript (Page7-8, Lines149 -195).

Meanwhile, ROC curves and corresponding AUC values for the three machine‑learning models have been supplemented. The AUC values obtained by direct fitting on the training cohort were 1.000 (95% CI: 1.000–1.000) for random forest, 0.997 (95% CI: 0.991–1.000) for support vector machine, and 0.990 (95% CI: 0.975–1.000) for generalized linear model. Considering the potential optimistic bias from direct fitting on the training set, we further calculated AUC values from 5‑fold stratified cross‑validation: 0.839 (95% CI: 0.786–0.891) for random forest, 0.818 (95% CI: 0.713–0.923) for generalized linear model, and 0.925 (95% CI: 0.887–0.962) for support vector machine (Page15-16, Lines 347-366).

Subsequently, the above‑mentioned models were applied to the independent external cohort GSE99039, yielding AUC values of approximately 0.55‑0.59, which were markedly lower than those from the training cohort and internal cross‑validation. These results indicate limited discriminatory power and generalizability of the models in an independent cohort, suggesting that they cannot yet serve as robust diagnostic tools for PD. Technical platforms, sample composition, clinical characteristics and disease heterogeneity between the training and external cohorts may collectively contribute to the cross‑cohort performance of the models (Page16, Lines 366-368).

It is noteworthy that the primary purpose of machine‑learning models in this study was to assist in screening candidate genes such as CYSLTR2 and TTPAL, rather than developing predictive tools ready for direct clinical diagnosis. External validation results suggest that these candidate genes and their combinations require further validation in larger independent cohorts with consistent technical platforms and complete clinical information.

Comment 2. n=5 per group is insufficient to support the reported significance (P<0.0001). Authors should expand clinical validation to at least 20–30 subjects per group.

Reply:

We appreciate this professional comment. We agree that the clinical cohort with only five subjects per group limits the generalizability of our RT-qPCR findings, and we recognize the merit of expanding the cohort to 20–30 participants per group as suggested. However, due to objective constraints including limited recruitment time, scarcity of eligible patients and sample access requirements, we cannot enlarge the clinical validation cohort within the revision period.

Corresponding revisions have been implemented: We have moderated the interpretation of RT-qPCR expression differences within the Abstract and Results to avoid overinterpretation of our findings. In addition, we have substantially expanded the Limitations section to discuss insufficient statistical power and potential bias caused by the small sample size, and explicitly state that these RT-qPCR results constitute preliminary findings requiring further verification in larger independent cohorts (Page 21, Lines 477–483; Page 27–28, Lines 642–646). These revisions clarify the scope of our conclusions and enhance the rigor of data interpretation. Thank you for your careful guidance.

Comment 3. The use of Spearman-ranked gene lists as GSEA input should be explicitly described as a preranked approach. Permutation number, FDR threshold, and leading-edge genes should be reported.

Reply：

We appreciate this valuable comment. Corresponding supplements have been made to the Methods section. We adopted a preranked GSEA approach. For each biomarker, Spearman correlation coefficients with genome-wide genes were calculated and used as the ranking metric. GSEA was implemented via the R clusterProfiler package against the KEGG pathway gene set. A total of 10,000 permutations were performed, and multiple testing was corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Pathways with FDR < 0.05 and |NES| > 1 were defined as significantly enriched, and leading-edge genes from enriched pathways were extracted for subsequent analysis (Pages 9, Lines 210–219). These revisions clarify the analytical framework and enhance the reproducibility of our bioinformatic workflow. Thank you for your careful guidance.

Comment 4. A Vina score of −5.9 kcal/mol is relatively weak. Authors should benchmark this against known CYSLTR2 endogenous ligands and present key interacting residues in tabular form to support the biological relevance of the docking result.

Reply:

Thank you for this valuable comment. We agree that the original Vina score alone cannot sufficiently support the biological relevance of the predicted interaction between amphetamine and CYSLTR2. We have therefore performed additional comparative analyses against endogenous ligands as suggested.

In the revised work, well‑characterized endogenous ligands of CYSLTR2, including leukotriene C4 (LTC4), leukotriene D4 (LTD4), and leukotriene E4 (LTE4), were docked onto CYSLTR2 using the CB‑Dock2 platform under identical computational conditions for direct comparison with amphetamine. The updated docking yielded a predicted binding affinity of −6.6 kcal/mol for amphetamine. The corresponding Vina scores for LTC4, LTD4, and LTE4 were −8.9 kcal/mol, −7.7 kcal/mol, and −7.8 kcal/mol, respectively. These results indicate that amphetamine exhibits weaker predicted binding affinity compared with these endogenous ligands.

Further binding‑mode analysis revealed that amphetamine accesses the predicted binding pocket of CYSLTR2 and forms an interaction with residue SER169 at a distance of 2.6 Å. Key interacting residues and interaction distances for the three endogenous ligands were also systematically characterized. Predicted binding affinities, critical interacting residues, and interaction distances for amphetamine, LTC4, LTD4, and LTE4 have been summarized in Table 2 (“Predicted binding affinities and key interacting residues of amphetamine and endogenous ligands with CYSLTR2”) in the revised manuscript (Pages 18–19, Lines 435–448).

Based on these findings, we have moderated our statements concerning amphetamine‑CYSLTR2 binding. We no longer claim strong binding affinity, and instead only suggest a potential structural interaction. We also emphasize that these observations are purely in silico docking predictions, and their biological relevance requires further validation via in‑vitro binding and functional assays. This supplementary benchmarking strengthens the interpretive rigor of our molecular docking results.

Reviewer #2: This manuscript addresses an interesting and potentially useful topic by identifying candidate biomarkers for Parkinson’s disease using public transcriptomic datasets and machine-learning approaches. The overall workflow is reasonable, and the additional RT-qPCR validation adds some value. However, several methodological and interpretative issues should be addressed.

Comment 1. the relationship between the identified biomarkers and mitochondrial dynamics should be described more cautiously. CYSLTR2 and TTPAL were identified through a mitochondrial dynamics-related scoring and DEG screening framework, rather than being directly demonstrated as core regulators of mitochondrial fission, fusion, mitophagy, mitochondrial transport, or mitochondrial quality control. Therefore, I suggest revising the wording throughout the manuscript. For example, terms such as “mitochondrial dynamics biomarkers” could be replaced with “mitochondrial dynamics-associated candidate biomarkers” or “candidate biomarkers identified through a mitochondrial dynamics-related transcriptomic framework.”

Reply:

We appreciate this professional and detailed comment. We fully recognize that the original descriptions regarding the association between CYSLTR2/TTPAL and mitochondrial dynamics lacked sufficient rigor, and systematic revisions have been implemented throughout the manuscript.

CYSLTR2 and TTPAL are MDRG-correlated DEGs rather than canonical mitochondrial dynamics regulators (MDRGs). They were screened via a transcriptomic framework based on GSVA scores of mitochondrial dynamics-related genes. To date, direct experimental evidence confirming them as core regulators of mitochondrial fission, fusion, mitophagy, mitochondrial transport or mitochondrial quality control is lacking. Following your suggestion, we removed the ambiguous phrase “mitochondrial dynamics biomarkers” throughout the manuscript. Inspired by your recommended term mitochondrial dynamics-associated candidate biomarkers, we adopted the semantically compatible expression biomarkers associated with mitochondrial dynamics dysfunction. This wording clarifies that these markers are linked to pathological mitochondrial abnormalities, instead of being core mitochondrial dynamics regulators. The manuscript title has been adjusted accordingly to clarify this conceptual distinction:

Original title: Identification and validation of biomarkers associated with mitochondrial dynamics in Parkinson's disease based on the GEO dataset

Revised title: Identification and validation of biomarkers associated with mitochondrial dynamics dysfunction in Parkinson's disease based on GEO datasets

These revisions eliminate conceptual confusion, strengthen logical reasoning and improve the rigor of academic statements, preventing potential misunderstanding among readers. Thank you for your careful guidance.

Comment 2. the machine-learning procedure should be described in greater detail. The authors should clarify the data preprocessing steps, feature-selection process, cross-validation strategy, hyperparameter tuning, and how overfitting was minimized, especially given the relatively small size of the training cohort.

Reply:

We appreciate this valuable comment. We have supplemented detailed descriptions of the machine-learning workflow, including data preprocessing, feature selection, cross-validation strategy, hyperparameter configuration and overfitting mitigation. Considering the limited sample size of the training cohort, relevant methodological quality-control details have been added to the Methods section (Pages 6–8, Lines 143–195).

Before modeling, missing value imputation, batch correction and Z-score normalization were performed on the gene expression matrix. The 17 candidate genes were included as features, and disease status served as the binary outcome variable. Random forest, support vector machine and generalized linear model were constructed using the R caret package. Stratified 5-fold cross-validation was adopted to balance positive and negative samples in each fold and reduce bias caused by random partitioning. Feature subsets were preliminarily screened according to variable importance metrics of each model. Residual diagnostics were performed for the GLM, and the optimal model was selected based on cross-validation performance across all algorithms, which was further validated by expression trends in training and validation cohorts. Models were trained with default hyperparameters implemented in the package, and stratified cross-validation was used to evaluate model stability. Meanwhile, stratified cross-validation, moderate model complexity restriction and cross-comparison among multiple algorithms were jointly used to mitigate potential overfitting risks arising from the relatively small training dataset. These revisions improve the transparency and reproducibility of our machine-learning pipeline and strengthen the reliability of our findings. Thank you for your careful guidance.

Comment 3. the diagnostic performance of the final biomarkers should be more clearly presented. ROC curves, AUC values with confidence intervals, sensitivity, specificity, and preferably validation-set performance should be reported for CYSLTR2, TTPAL, and the combined model.

Reply:

We appreciate this valuable comment regarding the presentation of diagnostic performance. We have evaluated CYSLTR2, TTPAL, and their combined model in both training and validation sets. ROC curves were generated, and AUC values with 95% confidence intervals, sensitivity and specificity were calculated and reported for each marker. The AUCs of individual genes ranged from 0.549 to 0.667 (all below 0.7), indicating limited discriminatory ability when used alone. We constructed a combined nomogram incorporating CYSLTR2 and TTPAL, which was also validated in both cohorts and achieved an AUC of 0.707. Calibration curves demonstrated favorable consistency between predicted and observed outcomes. Decision curve analysis (DCA) further revealed that the combined nomogram yielded higher clinical net benefit compared with single-gene markers (Page 16, Lines 379–389, Supplementary Figure 1). All diagnostic metrics have been systematically organized for clearer visualization. Thank you for your careful guidance.

If possible, additional clinical samples would strengthen the study.

We have moderated relevant interpretations throughout the manuscript: descriptions regarding the clinical utility of biomarkers have been tempered in the Abstract and Results to avoid overinterpretation. Furthermore, we have expanded the Limitations section to explicitly state that these RT-qPCR findings represent preliminary validation, and further verification in larger independent clinical cohorts is required (Page 27–28, Lines 642–646). These revisions define the appropriate scope of our conclusions and improve the rigor of data interpretation. Thank you for your careful guidance.
