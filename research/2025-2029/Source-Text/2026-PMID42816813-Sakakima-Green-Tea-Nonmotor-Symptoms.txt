Title: A comparative analysis of effects of daily green tea consumption on non-motor symptoms among community-dwelling older patients with Parkinson's disease
Source: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/02601060261493252?rfr_dat=cr_pub++0pubmed&rfr_id=ori%3Arid%3Acrossref.org&url_ver=Z39.88-2003
PMID: 42816813
DOI: 10.1177/02601060261493252
First published online: 2026-09-30
Authors: Harutoshi Sakakima, Toshiro Uchi, Kazuki Nakanishi, Yuki Kato-Eguchi, Teruki Matsuoka, Ryoma Matsuzaki, Shogo Kakimoto, Yoshitake Baba, Makoto Kobayashi, Takanobu Takihara
Coverage: Original publisher methods, results and all five tables reviewed on 2026-11-20. This is a local factual summary. The original article is available under CC BY-NC 4.0.

Study design

This cross-sectional Kagoshima survey recruited community-dwelling older people with Parkinson disease between October 2022 and May 2025. Of 114 recruited, two were excluded for prior stroke and seven for missing data. The 105 analyzed participants included five who drank no green tea, 22 who drank it less than daily, and 78 daily drinkers. The daily volume groups were less than 500 mL (42 people), 500–1000 mL (12), and more than 1000 mL (24). Reported beverages included sencha, gyokuro and hojicha. The authors did not quantify participant-level caffeine content, brewing parameters, or intake of other beverages such as water. Study funding came from a tea company that employed three coauthors.

Hyposmia

Table 1 reports hyposmia in 4/5, 16/22 and 60/78 across the no-tea, less-than-daily and daily groups, respectively. The overall comparison gives p = .854. Table 2 gives 32/42, 8/12 and 20/24 across the increasing daily volume groups, with p = .519. Both prevalence sequences are nonmonotonic. The tables give a binary characteristic without a reported smell severity measure, an adjusted olfactory model or severity change over time.

Nighttime sleep and constipation

Among daily drinkers, Table 3 reports moderate or severe sleep problems for 10/42, 2/12 and 1/24 across increasing volumes (three-group p = .029). Moderate or severe constipation occurred in 20/42, 4/12 and 5/24 (three-group p = .144). Across the daily drinkers, Spearman coefficients for sleep problems and constipation were −0.35 (p = .002) and −0.28 (p = .018). In Table 5, the reported odds ratios for mild-or-worse scores versus normal or slight were 0.57 for sleep (95% CI 0.39–0.83) and 0.71 for constipation (95% CI 0.50–0.99), adjusted for age, sex and total medications. The article does not clearly specify the scaling increment of the volume term. Adjusted analyses were selected after screening correlations. These are associations within daily drinkers.

The study's sleep-problem item corresponds to MDS-UPDRS Part I item 1.7, which concerns initiating and maintaining nighttime sleep. The scale has separate items for daytime sleepiness and fatigue, and the authors did not include those items in their specified analysis. The paper discusses daytime symptoms and nocturnal tremor without reporting separate estimates for them. Constipation is a distinct Part I outcome. The scale definitions are available from the [MDS scale hosted by CMS](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/mds-updrsenglishfinal.pdf).

Other endpoints and limitations

Table 4 reports depressed mood, anxious mood and apathy coefficients of −0.03 (p = .772), −0.12 (p = .294) and 0.06 (p = .614), respectively. Hallucination and psychosis is a combined item, with r = −0.11 (p = .360). The cognitive item and the separate MMSE are broad measures. Walking speed appears in the descriptive comparisons. No item-level small-handwriting result is reported. The small non-drinker group, one-time self-reported behavior, potential hydration and medication confounding, and absent preparation-specific results limit causal interpretation. The authors indicate that underlying study data are available by request. No public participant-level dataset was verified.

Study-family linkage

The [existing cohort notes](../Food-Experiment-Kagoshima-Tea-Cohort.md) trace the same ethics approval, 220137, across the 2025 conference tea analysis, the 2026 tea paper and a 2024 posture and sarcopenia analysis. The two tea reports count as a single evidence trail. Differences in eligibility and volume groups are documented in the cohort notes. The extent of participant overlap remains unverified.
