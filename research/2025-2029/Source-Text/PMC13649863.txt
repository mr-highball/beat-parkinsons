Objective

Neurosurgical modulation of reward circuitry has been explored for severe, treatment-refractory substance use disorders (SUDs), yet the human literature remains fragmented across targets, modalities, and outcomes, limiting target selection and future trial design. We systematically reviewed human clinical evidence for deep brain stimulation (DBS) and lesion approaches targeting reward network nodes for SUDs, and summarized convergent preclinical findings to identify where the strongest clinical signal exists and to provide a framework for mechanism-informed future trials.

Methods

We systematically searched PubMed, PubMed Central, Embase, and Scopus (inception-September 9, 2025) for human DBS/ablation studies in primary SUDs targeting addiction-relevant nodes (e.g., nucleus accumbens [NAc], ventral capsule/ventral striatum/anterior limb of internal capsule [ALIC], subthalamic nucleus) and reporting extractable outcomes; in vivo addiction-model animal studies were included for mechanistic context. Findings were synthesized narratively.

Results

Forty-seven studies met inclusion criteria. The clearest controlled human signal came from bilateral NAc DBS in severe alcohol use disorder, with additional promising but less mature findings in heroin/opioid DBS. In the only randomized delayed-activation trial of bilateral NAc DBS for severe alcohol use disorder (n = 12), abstinent days increased from 27.6% to 56.0% at 6 months (p = 0.014) and 74.2% at 18 months (p = 0.004), and heavy drinking days decreased from 69.6% to 47.4% (p = 0.020) and 35.5% (p = 0.031). Craving improved (OCDS 21.0 to 10.5 at 6 months, p = 0.014; 5.9 at 18 months, p = 0.008), with blinded-phase separation favoring earlier stimulation for abstinent/heavy drinking days (p = 0.048). An open-label alcohol DBS pilot reported drinks/day decreasing from 10.4 to 2.7 at 12 months (p < 0.05) and OCDS from 28.7 to 8.3 (p < 0.05). In heroin use disorder DBS targeting NAc ± ALIC (n = 8), 5/8 remained abstinent for > 3 years; craving VAS decreased from 7.6 to 1.4 at 3 months and 0.8 at 24 months (p < 0.001), with depressive symptoms improving (HDRS 12.4 to 3.4; p = 0.001). In a polysubstance feasibility study (n = 4), two participants maintained abstinence for > 1150 and > 520 days; craving decreased in individuals (opioid craving p = 0.036; benzodiazepine p = 0.001). Biomarker-guided opioid DBS reduced cue-induced craving (6.93 vs 3.09; p < 0.001). Ablation cohorts showed short-term improvements (alcohol NAc ablation: 3/12 relapsed; dependence severity 39.08 to 9.25, p < 0.01), but longer-term relapse and neuropsychological tradeoffs were reported (4-year abstinence 53.8% overall). Preclinical studies highlighted strong target-and state-dependence, including paradoxical relapse augmentation in alcohol-dependent models.

Conclusion

DBS and lesion approaches should be interpreted as distinct neurosurgical modalities rather than a single therapeutic class. The strongest controlled human signal currently comes from bilateral NAc DBS in severe alcohol use disorder, with additional but lower-certainty DBS evidence in opioid/heroin and polysubstance use disorders. Lesion approaches provide historical and mechanistic support for the relevance of ventral striatal circuitry, but their irreversibility, heterogeneous follow-up, and neuropsychological tradeoffs require a higher evidentiary and ethical threshold. Overall, current data support clinical plausibility and trial-design rationale, but not definitive efficacy across SUD subtypes

Introduction

Substance use disorders (SUDs) are chronic, relapsing neuropsychiatric conditions characterized by maladaptive reinforcement learning, impaired inhibitory control, and persistent cue reactivity [28, 51]. Despite effective behavioral and pharmacologic therapies for many patients, a subgroup of patients with SUD develop severe, refractory illness with repeated relapse, medical complications, and elevated mortality [39, 49]. For these individuals, the urgency of innovation has led to renewed interest in neurosurgical interventions, particularly deep brain stimulation (DBS). DBS can modulate circuit-level drivers of craving, motivational attentional bias from drug-related stimuli, and compulsive drug seeking [3, 12, 65]. Addiction networks are well established, principally the interconnected mesocorticolimbic networks anchored in the nucleus accumbens (NAc), ventral capsule/ventral striatum (VC/VS), medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), amygdala, hippocampus, and subthalamic nucleus (STN) [28, 29]. These regions participate in reward valuation, habit formation, affect regulation, and cognitive control [28, 29, 57]. DBS has transformed care for common movement disorders and is increasingly being pursued as a therapy for refractory psychiatric illness; the relative reversibility of DBS makes it an appealing alternative to destructive procedures for some patients [17, 27].

Addiction adds treatment barriers: variable motivation, relapse risk, and family or system pressures. These factors elevate the ethical burden of consent and long-term monitoring. DBS was first explored as a potential addiction therapy in 2007 by Kuhn et al., after bilateral NAc stimulation was associated with marked remission of comorbid alcohol dependence [30].

However, DBS had been applied to addiction-relevant circuits much earlier, including the STN and VC/VS, with observations that compulsive and addictive behaviors could be either ameliorated or exacerbated depending on target, stimulation, and medication context [48, 50, 62]. Ablative procedures, particularly NAc lesioning, have been used historically in China and are still practiced to a limited extent for severe, treatment-refractory opioid dependence, though concerns persist regarding neuropsychiatric side effects and the ethics of irreversible interventions [11, 32]. In parallel to these clinical studies, controlled animal studies have provided mechanistic insights into DBS models including identifying parameter and disease-state dependencies, such as paradoxical worsening of relapse behaviors depending on timing, stimulation frequency, and dependence phenotype.

The aim of this systematic review is threefold. First, we synthesize clinical evidence for neurosurgical approaches to SUDs, while explicitly distinguishing programmable DBS from irreversible lesion/ablation ablative procedures across alcohol, opioids, stimulants, nicotine, and polysubstance use. Second, we integrate preclinical findings to clarify plausible mechanisms and inform target selection. Third, we analyze ethical and trial-design issues unique to neurosurgical interventions in addiction and propose a roadmap for future sham-controlled, circuit-guided trials.

Methods

Protocol and reporting

This review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidance. An a priori protocol specified objectives, eligibility criteria, outcomes, and analytic approach, and a PRISMA flowchart was created for the screened studies (Fig. 1).Fig. 1 PRISMA flowchart

Information sources and search strategy

We searched four databases: PubMed, PubMed Central (PMC), Embase, and Scopus. Searches were conducted from database inception through September 9th, 2025. No restrictions were applied at the search stage based on substance class; all SUDs were eligible if they involved an addictive substance and met the intervention/target criteria. Full search terms are available in Supplemental Table 1.

Eligibility criteria

Human studies were eligible if they met all of the following: (1) Population: participants with a primary diagnosis of an SUD (including alcohol, opioids/heroin, stimulants such as cocaine or methamphetamine, nicotine/tobacco, or polysubstance use); (2) Intervention: invasive neurosurgical intervention intended to treat SUD symptoms, including DBS, stereotactic lesioning/ablation, or combined procedures; (3) Target: at least one addiction-relevant target in the reward/control circuitry, including NAc, VC/VS/anterior limb of the internal capsule (ALIC), STN or medial forebrain bundle (or clearly adjacent fiber pathways intended to modulate the same network); and (4) Outcomes: reported at least one addiction-relevant efficacy or safety endpoint with extractable data, including binary/nominal outcomes (e.g., abstinent vs relapsed; adverse events), time-to-event outcomes (e.g., time to relapse), count/continuous outcomes (e.g., days using, drinks/day, quantity/frequency), or ordinal/continuous scale scores (validated or investigator-defined). Narrative outcomes were eligible only if they specified a clear status or direction of change at a defined follow-up. We included randomized trials, prospective cohorts, retrospective series, and case reports. We also included conference abstracts only if they reported original human neurosurgical SUD data with enough methodological detail to extract target, sample, and outcomes; otherwise they were excluded. We excluded studies if the neurosurgical intervention was performed for a different primary indication (e.g., movement disorder or psychiatric disorder) and SUD outcomes were not reported or not attributable; the intervention was non-invasive neuromodulation only (TMS, tDCS, etc.); or the study lacked sufficient data to confirm the target/intervention or lacked outcomes relevant to SUD. Preclinical studies were eligible if they met all of the following: (1) Model: in vivo animal studies (rodent or other) using an addiction-relevant paradigm (self-administration, conditioned place preference (CPP), extinction/reinstatement, relapse-like drinking, progressive ratio/motivation measures); (2) Intervention: DBS or invasive stimulation/lesion manipulation applied to addiction-relevant targets; (3) Outcomes: reported at least one quantitative addiction-relevant behavioral endpoint (e.g., intake, lever presses, reinstatement responding, preferences scores, breakpoints). Preclinical studies were included to support mechanistic interpretation and to summarize parameter- and state-dependent effects relevant to human trial planning. Human clinical evidence was treated as the primary evidence base for efficacy and safety. Preclinical studies were prespecified as a mechanistic-context layer and were synthesized separately; they were not used to infer clinical efficacy. Accordingly, animal findings were used to identify candidate mechanisms, target subregions, stimulation parameters, and state-dependent effects that may inform human trial design.

Study selection

All references were imported into Covidence for management of deduplication and screening. Covidence’s automated deduplication was used first, followed by manual spot checks for remaining duplicates (e.g., conference abstract vs full publications). Titles/Abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers using prespecified inclusion/exclusion criteria. Full-text review was then completed by two independent reviewers for studies advanced after title/abstract screening. Conflicts were resolved by consensus review. When multiple publications appeared to report overlapping participants, cohorts, or extended follow-up from the same center, we treated the most complete peer-reviewed report or the longest follow-up report as the primary source for cohort-level interpretation. Earlier reports, conference abstracts, or secondary analyses were retained only when they contributed non-overlapping information, such as programming details, mechanistic biomarkers, adverse events, imaging findings, or longer-term follow-up. Conference abstracts were excluded from primary interpretation when a peer-reviewed full report of the same cohort was available.

Data extraction

Extraction was performed using a standardized template and checked for consistency across reviewers. For clinical studies, we extracted study characteristics (country, design, single vs. multicenter, sample size, setting, follow-up duration, funding and conflicts), clinical characteristics (primary substance category, secondary substances, severity metrics, years of use, baseline use metrics, prior treatment failures, comorbid psychiatric/neurologic conditions, age and sex), and intervention details (surgery type, primary target, laterality, targeting approach, device manufacturer, stereotactic coordinates when reported, and post-operative imaging verification). For DBS studies, stimulation and programming variables were abstracted when available (time to programming start, number of programming sessions, stimulation mode, frequency, pulse width, amplitude, duty cycle, and whether parameters were changed over time). We also extracted trial design features (control type, randomization and allocation concealment when applicable), concurrent treatments (psychosocial care and medications), and ethics/safety reporting fields (consent safeguards and adverse event monitoring). For preclinical studies, we extracted model characteristics (species/strain/sex, housing, randomization/blinding, sample size), addiction paradigm and substance exposure protocol, surgical/stimulation details (target, stereotactic coordinates, verification), stimulation parameters and schedule, and whether pharmacologic manipulations were used.

Data synthesis

Given substantial heterogeneity in population characteristics, neurosurgical targets, stimulation parameters, follow-up duration, and outcome definitions, we performed a narrative synthesis rather than a meta-analysis. We grouped clinical results by primary substance class (alcohol, opioids/heroin, stimulants, polysubstance) and by target (NAc/VC/VS pathways vs. alternative targets such as STN or cortical nodes), then summarized patterns in efficacy signals and adverse events. Preclinical studies were synthesized separately and were used to highlight mechanistic plausibility and parameter/state dependence (e.g., stimulation timing relative to extinction/reinstatement, dependent vs. nondependent phenotypes). DBS and lesion/ablation studies were not pooled and were interpreted as distinct modalities. DBS was considered programmable and reversible at the level of stimulation delivery, whereas lesion approaches were considered irreversible interventions with distinct safety, ethical, and neuropsychological implications. We therefore summarized DBS and ablation evidence separately, then compared them only at the level of circuit rationale, endpoints, and trial-design implications.

Results

Study characteristics

47 studies (mixed clinical and preclinical) were included. Targets included NAc core/shell, VC/VS or ALIC, ACC/mPFC subregions, and STN. Clinical evidence (Table 1) consisted of one randomized study, 9 small prospective trials/pilots, and case reports (10)/series (5). Ablation studies were predominantly from NAc lesioning cohorts, with surgical targeting and hardware details summarized in Table 2. Clinical DBS programming and stimulation parameters are summarized in Table 3. Relevant clinical and preclinical study characteristics, design features, concomitant care, safety monitoring, and stimulation protocols are summarized in Tables 4, 5, 6. To make the evidence hierarchy explicit, randomized or blinded human DBS data were interpreted as the highest level of evidence, followed by prospective open-label DBS cohorts, retrospective series, single-case reports, uncontrolled lesion cohorts, and preclinical studies. Preclinical studies were considered mechanistic and translational rather than evidence of clinical efficacy. Under this framework, bilateral NAc DBS for severe alcohol use disorder represents the strongest clinical signal, while opioid/heroin DBS remains promising but lower certainty, stimulant DBS remains very sparse, and ablation studies are best viewed as historical and mechanistic comparators rather than equivalent alternatives to DBS. Clinical DBS evidence

Alcohol use disorder

Randomized evidence

The most rigorous clinical evidence comes from a randomized delayed-activation design evaluating bilateral NAc DBS in severe, treatment-refractory alcohol use disorder [1]. Across the full cohort (n = 12), abstinent days increased from 27.6% ± 11.6 at baseline to 56.0% ± 31.1 at 6 months (p = 0.014) and to 74.2% ± 31.3 at 18 months (p= 0.004) [1]. Heavy drinking days decreased from 69.6% ± 12.6 to 47.4% ± 27.6 at 6 months (p = 0.020) and to 35.5% ± 29.9 at 18 months (p= 0.031) [1]. Mean daily alcohol intake dropped from 207.4 ± 162.2 g/day to 85.3 ± 69.7 g/day at 6 months (p = 0.014) and 62.7 ± 72.5 g/day at 18 months (p= 0.037) [1]. Craving improved significantly: OCDS declined from 21.0 ± 7.0 to 10.5 ± 7.2 at 6 months (p = 0.014) and 5.9 ± 6.4 at 18 months (p= 0.008) [1]. Blinded-phase comparisons favored earlier stimulation for abstinent days and heavy drinking days (p = 0.048), and also showed lower AUQ craving (p = 0.020) and reduced anhedonia (SHAPS p = 0.028; social anhedonia p= 0.008) [1]. Safety reporting noted 38 adverse events in the first 6 months, including alcohol-relapse related hospitalizations classified as serious adverse events [1].

Prospective pilot trial evidence

In a phase I DBS trial for severe alcohol use disorder targeting the NAc, Davidson and colleagues reported clinically meaningful reductions in alcohol consumption and craving at 12 months [5]. Self-reported standard drinks/day decreased from 10.4 ± 4.0 pre-DBS to 2.7 ± 3.0 at 12 months (paired Wilcoxon p< 0.05) [5]. Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test scores declined from 35.7 ± 3 to 11.0 ± 12 (p < 0.05), craving (ACQ) from 58.3 ± 15 to 41.8 ± 17 (p< 0.05), and dependence severity (ADS) from 26.0 ± 7 to 9.0 ± 11.0 (not statistically significant) [5]. OCDS dropped from 28.7 ± 5 to 8.3 ± 8 (p < 0.05), with anxiety reductions on BAI (20.3 ± 9 to 9.3 ± 12; p< 0.05). The study also reported improvements in liver enzymes with strong model effects (ALT β = 3.40, CI 0.80–6.01, p-FDR < 0.01; AST β = 2.38, CI 0.98–3.77, p-FDR = 0.01) [5]. These clinical improvements were accompanied by neuroimaging correlates including reduced NAc metabolism on FDG-PET and changes in NAc connectivity/cue reactivity on fMRI [5, 33].

Case series and long-term outcomes

Multiple case reports and small series suggest that some individuals experience sustained reduction in craving and prolonged abstinence after NAc DBS. Kuhn et al. described one patient with severe alcohol dependence who transitioned from heavy daily consumption to near abstinence, with complete cessation after one year; carbohydrate-deficient transferrin normalized from 7.8% to 1.9% and craving/dependence scores reportedly fell below pathological thresholds [31]. Muller et al. reported early experience treating three alcohol-dependent patients with NAc DBS; two remained abstinent and one showed marked reduction [41]. Quantitatively, AUQ dropped sharply (e.g., from 53 to 8 at 6–12 months in one case) and OCDS obsession/compulsion subscores fell to 0 at follow-up in two out of three patients [41]. Longer-term follow-up of five patients treated with NAc DBS suggested durable craving suppression and functional recovery in a subset: all reported absence of craving, two remained abstinent for years, and three reduced drinking substantially with follow-up up to eight years [42]. Voges et al. reported a five-patient alcohol NAc DBS series with follow-up extending beyond three years in several cases; standardized measures again showed large reductions in craving (AUQ) and OCDS across cases, and durable abstinence in some individuals [56]. Mechanistic imaging work in an abstinent alcohol-dependent DBS patient demonstrated attenuated cue-related activation patterns and highlighted stimulation-related neuropsychiatric effects such as transient hypomania responsive to parameter adjustment [23].

Opioid and heroin use disorders

DBS cohort and pilot trial evidence

A pilot cohort study of DBS in heroin-use disorder targeting the NAc and ALIC reported sustained abstinence in a subset and large improvements in craving and quality of life among non-relapsed participants [4]. Five of eight patients remained abstinent for > 3 years, while two relapsed within the first year (one patient lost to follow up at 3 months) [4]. Among non-relapsed patients, craving visual analog scale (VAS) decreased from 7.6 ± 1.34 baseline to 1.4 ± 1.10 at 3 months and 0.8 ± 0.84 at 24 months (p< 0.001) [4]. Depression and obsessive–compulsive symptom measures improved (Hamilton depression rating scale 12.4 ± 6.23 to 3.4 ± 2.19 at 24 months; p = 0.001; Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale [OCS]; 21.4 ± 10.92 to 3.8 ± 2.17; p< 0.001) [4]. Quality-of-life summary scores increased from 504.9 ± 145.1 to 704.0 ± 73.6 at 24 months (p= 0.001) [4].

Single-case reports

Several single-case reports support possible benefits. Valencia-Alfonso et al. described heroin addiction treated with bilateral NAc DBS, reporting substantial reductions in daily heroin intake and craving depending on active contact selection; craving scores (Desire/Interest scale) dropped from 33 pre-DBS to 18 and heroin intake dropped from 0.50 g/day to 0.10 g/day under dorsal contact stimulation, whereas alternative contacts worsened craving and intake [52]. Zhou et al. reported long-term (6-year follow-up) abstinence in a heroin-dependent patient treated with NAc DBS, noting drug-free status during stimulation and continued abstinence (3.5 years) after stimulation discontinuation [68]. Zhang described a patient who maintained abstinence through an extended (9-month) blinded sham-stimulation period and subsequently underwent safe DBS explantation 2 years after successful treatment, highlighting a rarely discussed endpoint: elective device removal after sustained recovery [66]. A combined approach (NAc DBS + anterior capsulotomy) was reported in a polysubstance case with improvements across craving, mood, anxiety, sleep, and quality of life over 12 months; craving for opioids reduced from VAS 6 to 0 by 3 months and remained low, with large improvements in depression/anxiety scores over time (Hamilton Depression Rating Scale baseline 26 to 12 at 12 months, Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale baseline 29 to 11 at 12 months) [69].

Contemporary controlled feasibility trial evidence

Mahoney et al. reported an IRB/FDA-regulated single-participant trial of NAc/VC DBS for severe opioid and benzodiazepine use disorder [36]. The participant remained abstinent at both 12 weeks and 12 months [36]. Benzodiazepine craving decreased from 53.4 ± 29.5 pre-DBS to 1.0 ± 2.2 at 12 weeks (p< 0.001) with similar benefit at 12 months [36]. Heart rate variability increased from 19.2 ± 8.2 to 56.0 ± 11.7 (p< 0.001) [36]. Behavioral risk task performance improved with a trend toward significance (p= 0.06) [36].

Rezai et al. reported a multi-participant feasibility study in severe polysubstance use disorder using DBS targeting reward circuitry; four male participants were enrolled, with two maintaining complete abstinence for > 1150 and > 520 days [45]. Statistically significant craving reductions were observed in multiple participants across substances (e.g., participant 2: opioid craving F = 5.41, p = 0.036; benzodiazepine craving F = 16.91, p = 0.001; cannabis craving F = 8.77, p = 0.010; participant 3: heroin craving F = 30.03, p < 0.001; benzodiazepine craving F = 54.07, p < 0.001; cannabis craving F = 62.30, p< 0.001) [45]. Not all participants benefited; one showed nonsignificant changes (e.g., heroin craving p= 0.332) [45]. Qiu et al. reported a biomarker-guided optimization study in opioid use disorder demonstrating that personalized stimulation parameters correlated with > 50% reduction in cue-induced opioid craving (mean 6.93 vs 3.09; p = 4.32 × 10⁻⁷) and physiologic modulation of low-frequency activity (p= 0.039) [44].

Stimulant use disorders (cocaine and methamphetamine)

Cocaine

Goncalves-Ferreira et al. described DBS in a patient with severe refractory cocaine dependence, reporting improvement across objective and subjective measures [15]. Percentage of weeks free of cocaine increased from 40.9% pre-DBS to 80.5% at 6 months and 68% at 24 months; craving VAS dropped from 3.4 to 1.0 at 6 months and 0 at 24 months; negative urine screens rose from 12.5% to 66.7% at 6 months and 56.5% at 24 months [15]. Vorspan et al. reported STN DBS in a patient with crack cocaine dependence in a double-blind crossover design; STN stimulation did not clearly reduce crack craving or use and was associated with psychiatric destabilization in a context of cocaine intake [58].

Methamphetamine

Two case-based DBS experiences were reported for methamphetamine use disorder. Ge et al. described two methamphetamine-dependent patients treated with NAc DBS; one remained drug-free 1.5 years with craving VAS reduction from 5 to 0 and improved psychoticism scores (SCL-90 psychoticism 3.0 to 1.3), while a second patient relapsed when stimulation amplitude was reduced because of adverse reactions [13]. In this second patient, the right ventral DBS electrode contact deviated from the NAc region [13]. Zhang et al. reported a case of NAc plus VC DBS in methamphetamine dependence with improvements in craving and markers of dopamine system recovery: craving VAS decreased from 8 to 0 and OCS scores reduced from 29 to 11 at one year, while dopamine transporter binding increased in the caudate (+ 20.5%) and putamen (+ 25.6%) [67].

Clinical lesion/Ablation evidence

Lesion approaches are summarized separately from DBS because they differ fundamentally in reversibility, adjustability, safety profile, and ethical threshold. Whereas DBS permits post-operative programming changes and discontinuation of stimulation, lesion approaches produce permanent structural disruption of the targeted tissue or fiber pathways. Therefore, ablation findings are interpreted here as historical and mechanistic evidence for the relevance of ventral striatal circuitry, not as directly interchangeable evidence for DBS efficacy.

Ablation for alcohol dependence

Wu et al. reported stereotactic NAc ablation in 12 alcohol-dependent patients with follow-up 6–27 months; relapse occurred in 3 of 12, and standardized scales improved significantly at 6 months: Severity of alcohol dependence scale dropped from 39.08 ± 3.78 to 9.25 ± 10.52 (p < 0.01), drinking days in the last month from 21.17 ± 3.10 to 3.92 ± 7.61 (p < 0.01), and average drinks/day from 9.00 ± 2.22 to 2.25 ± 4.52 (p< 0.01) [63]. While these results suggest possible efficacy, interpretation is constrained by design limitations.

Ablation and intraoperative stimulation findings relevant to addiction

Ablative procedures targeting the NAc have been reported in multiple series, particularly for opioid dependence. Gao et al. described 28 opiate-dependent patients undergoing NAc ablation with follow-up to 15 months; relapse rates increased over time (7.7% within 1 month, 38.5% between 1 and 6 months, and 57.5% after > 6 months), though major surgical complications were reportedly uncommon [11]. Li et al. reported larger cohorts (n = 78) and methodological comparisons, noting a 4-year abstinence rate of 53.8% overall (42/78), with markedly higher abstinence rates in certain targeting/technique groups (75.0% and 76.2% in groups C and D vs. 31.3% and 28.6% in groups A and B; p< 0.005) [32]. Neuropsychological adverse effects were reported in 24.3% (18/74) of patients, though most were described as transient; no intracranial hemorrhage or infection was reported in that series [32]. Wang et al. examined reward-domain changes after NAc ablation in heroin-recovered individuals, documenting alterations in appetite/weight and sexual function measures with several statistically significant changes (e.g., increased sexual drive; higher overall satisfaction; and increased appetite and weight) [59]. Fang et al. reported intraoperative electrical stimulation responses in heroin-dependent patients, including autonomic changes and intense euphoria similar to heroin-induced euphoria associated with stimulation of the NAc and amygdala; combined stimulation strongly predicted heroin-like euphoria (OR ~ 5.4, p< 0.0001) [9]. These findings show that NAc manipulation can modify affective reward responses and may have unintended motivational consequences.

Preclinical evidence (Animal Studies)

Across 23 animal studies, DBS effects varied by substance, dependence state, stimulation frequency, timing (acquisition vs. extinction vs. reinstatement), and precise target location (core vs. shell; infralimbic vs. prelimbic vs. ACC; STN vs. striatal targets). Several consistent themes emerged.

Cocaine models

Reduction of cocaine seeking and reinstatement was frequently observed with NAc shell DBS and related circuit targets. DBS of the NAc shell was shown to attenuate cocaine priming-induced reinstatement [53] and that shell DBS may act via broader cortical-striatal modulation rather than simple local inactivation [54]. DBS in glutamatergic input regions such as the infralimbic cortex also showed reduced cocaine-primed reinstatement with statistically supported interaction effects (e.g., F = 7.876, p= 0.0263;) [18]. Chronic unilateral NAc DBS reduced relapse behavior after withdrawal with reductions of 36–48% at peak relapse time points (day 15 post-withdrawal) [22]. However, not all outcomes were uniformly beneficial: in cocaine-dependent extended-access rats, NAc shell DBS slightly increased cocaine self-administration while reducing withdrawal-associated irritability and psychomotor activation, suggesting complex motivational tradeoffs [26].

Opioid/Heroin models

Heroin/opioid relapse models showed promising effects of NAc stimulation in some paradigms. Guo et al. demonstrated that bilateral NAc core DBS attenuated cue- and heroin-induced reinstatement, with clear group differences (e.g., heroin-induced reinstatement F = 7.74, p = 0.001; DBS groups significantly reduced responding vs. controls, p< 0.05) [20]. Morphine CPP studies showed that prolonged high-frequency DBS could prevent reinstatement without impairing memory [35] and that ACC DBS inhibited morphine reward and facilitated extinction with intensity-dependent effects [10]. Low-frequency VC/VS DBS appears to strengthen extinction memory and modulate brain-derived neurotrophic factor signaling (133% increase) in corticomesolimbic regions [34].

Methamphetamine models

Methamphetamine models similarly supported relapse-reducing potential depending on stimulation pattern. Eskandari et al. reported that both high- and low-frequency (130 Hz and 10 Hz) NAc shell DBS reduced extinction time and prevented methamphetamine-primed reinstatement under specific timing conditions, with synchronous protocols often outperforming nonsynchronous stimulation [7]. Batra et al. reported that bilateral pre-operant DBS reduced methamphetamine self-administration on treatment days (p < 0.05) and produced a significant within-group reduction in relapse-session methamphetamine seeking during Pavlovian spontaneous recovery (57% decrease, p< 0.01) [2].

Alcohol models

Alcohol models highlight the risk of paradoxical effects. While some studies suggest reduced ethanol preference after NAc DBS [24] and reduced intake after NAc shell inactivation/DBS in alcohol-preferring rats (e.g., 200 µA DBS reduced ethanol consumption by 35–59% on multiple days, p< 0.0018) [61], Hadar et al. reported that NAc DBS augmented relapse-like drinking in alcohol-dependent rats and increased alcohol-induced dopamine release, suggesting that stimulation may enhance alcohol reward under dependence conditions [21]. These divergent findings support state-dependent DBS effects that may differ between dependence phenotypes.

Parameter optimization and mechanistic studies

Mechanistic/parameter-optimization work suggests DBS is not a unitary intervention and may itself be rewarding. Evans et al. demonstrated that NAc stimulation patterns influence preference and reward-seeking, with animals preferring paradigms delivering higher pulse counts, and showing stimulation-related reward signals. [8] Such results have ethical relevance, raising theoretical concerns about reinforcement or misuse potential if stimulation produces euphoria-like effects [8]. These findings also argue for a more granular approach to target selection. “NAc DBS” or “VC/VS DBS” should not be treated as anatomically uniform interventions: core versus shell, dorsal versus ventral contacts, and relative engagement of ALIC fibers may plausibly produce different effects on craving, extinction, impulsivity, mood, and adverse activation. Similarly, targets outside the NAc/VC/VS region, including STN, mPFC/infralimbic cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, medial forebrain bundle, and extended amygdala/bed nucleus of the stria terminalis pathways, may map onto different relapse mechanisms such as reward salience, habit formation, negative reinforcement, stress reactivity, or impaired cognitive control. Emerging connectomic DBS work in other neuropsychiatric and movement-disorder fields supports the concept of therapeutic “sweet spots” and adverse-effect “sour spots,” in which small differences in stimulation location or fiber engagement can correlate with beneficial versus detrimental outcomes. Future SUD DBS trials should therefore report electrode localization, volume of tissue activated/e-field models, directional contact use when available, and connectivity to prefrontal, cingulate, amygdalar, hippocampal, and thalamic nodes. This would allow efficacy and adverse events to be mapped onto circuit engagement rather than treated as target-wide effects [25].

Additional preclinical work further demonstrates that neuromodulation effects in addiction models are highly dependent on both the stimulation target and the behavioral context. Across morphine CPP paradigms, dorsal–ventral distinctions within the ventral striatum shape the magnitude of DBS-induced extinction effects, and frequency-dependent responses highlight the importance of stimulation parameters [38]. Complementary studies show that stage-specific NAc stimulation can either facilitate or disrupt extinction and reinstatement processes, showing the temporal sensitivity of these circuits [64]. Mechanistic work further reveals that mPFC-NAc pathway modulation engages molecular signaling programs such as Fibroblast Growth Factor 1/Fibroblast Growth Factor Receptor 1 that contribute to durable behavioral change [16]. Electrophysiological evidence from mPFC-driven modulation of NAc ensembles provides additional support for circuit-defined, target specific effects in morphine CPP [6]. Beyond opioid paradigms, emerging strategies such as STN stimulation in alcohol-related behaviors broaden the translational relevance of these approaches and illustrate how distinct nodes within the addiction network can be leveraged to alter maladaptive reward-seeking [55].

Risk of bias assessment and data synthesis

We did not perform a formal, tool-based risk of bias assessment because the clinical evidence base is dominated by case reports, small case series, and uncontrolled feasibility cohorts, for which standard instruments (e.g., Cochrane RoB2 for randomized trials, ROBINS-I/Newcastle–Ottawa for observational comparative studies, etc.,) are either not applicable or would generate largely uniform “high/unclear risk” ratings driven by study design rather than study conduct. In this context, assigning numerical or categorical risk-of-bias scores can give a false impression of precision and does little to discriminate between studies that differ mainly in reporting detail, follow-up intensity, and co-interventions rather than in core design features such as randomization and blinding. Instead, we used a structured qualitative appraisal aligned with the aims of this review (signal synthesis and trial-design implications rather than pooled effect estimation). Specifically, we extracted and considered study features most likely to introduce bias in neurosurgical SUD research such as patient selection and setting, concurrent treatments and monitoring intensity, outcome definition and verification, completeness and duration of follow-up, presence of blinded phases or delayed-activation designs, stimulation/programming transparency, and adverse-event ascertainment. We weighted interpretation accordingly, treating uncontrolled reports as hypothesis-generating while giving greatest inferential weight to controlled human evidence. For preclinical studies, formal scoring was similarly limited by heterogeneity in paradigms and frequent under-reporting of randomization/blinding/attrition. Animal findings were therefore used to inform mechanistic plausibility and parameter/state dependence rather than support quantitative conclusions about efficacy. This structured qualitative approach was used to avoid overstating certainty while still making differences in interpretive weight transparent across randomized, open-label, case-based, lesion, and preclinical evidence.

Discussion

The neurosurgical literature in substance use disorders is still small, heterogeneous, and uneven in methodological rigor, but provides a coherent framework. Across substances, the most consistent signals are not instant abstinence switches but shifts in intermediate processes that sit upstream of relapse: craving, cue reactivity, affective tone (including anhedonia), and, more broadly, how reward and salience are assigned. This matters because the field has often leaned on binary end points (e.g., time to first use) that are easy to operationalize but may not map cleanly onto the way circuit interventions change behavior in chronic relapsing conditions. In parallel, DBS and lesion data reinforce that ventral striatal interventions are unlikely to be behaviorally “specific” in the narrow sense; they may alter motivation and reward processing across drug and non-drug domains. The practical implication is that next-generation trials should be designed to measure mechanism-relevant dimensions and meaningful functioning, rather than focusing only on lapses and toxicology.

Alcohol use disorder

Alcohol use disorder offers the clearest reminder of why endpoints and mechanistic expectations matter. The randomized, double-blind trial of NAc stimulation remains the most rigorous dataset, and it importantly did not show separation on the primary time-to-first-drink endpoint during the blinded phase [1]. That finding should temper overly confident conclusions drawn from early open-label reports, but it also should not be misread as a simple refutation of clinical value. The same study suggests that stimulation can still matter in ways that are clinically recognizable, by shaping patterns of drinking and symptom dimensions that drive relapse risk (craving, anhedonia), even if an initial lapse is not prevented [1].

The distinction is not semantic. “First drink” is a brittle metric in a disorder where brief lapses are common, and where outcomes that patients and clinicians value, such as fewer heavy drinking episodes, more abstinent days, reduced compulsive drive, and improved affective tone, may be the more realistic first targets of circuit-based intervention. The open-label pilot, early case reports, and longer follow-up series fit comfortably with that framing: benefits, when present, tend to look like sustained reductions in consumption and compulsivity in subsets of individuals rather than uniform, immediate abstinence across cohorts [5, 31, 41, 42, 56]. The larger takeaway is that alcohol DBS studies may be better served by primary outcomes that reflect relapse vulnerability and functional recovery, with abstinence analyzed as an important but not exclusive marker of success.

Opioid use disorder and heroin dependence

The opioid literature strengthens the interpretation that DBS may operate most reliably on proximal relapse drivers, particularly craving and cue reactivity, while long-term abstinence remains more variable and context-dependent. Across cohorts and case-based reports, craving reduction is a recurrent theme, and when abstinence is achieved it often co-occurs with broader stabilization in sleep, mood, or stress physiology, which are domains that are not incidental in OUD relapse risk [4, 36, 45]. This is one reason it may be misleading to treat abstinence alone as the “true” outcome and everything else as secondary; in refractory OUD, a credible circuit intervention may first show itself by lowering the intensity and intrusiveness of craving, reducing cue-induced destabilization, and enabling engagement in recovery behavior.

A second theme in OUD is that “NAc DBS” is not one intervention. Programming, contact selection, and individual differences in acute responses appear to matter, and several reports are most valuable precisely because they illustrate that outcome can hinge on how stimulation is delivered and which portions of the VS/ALIC region are engaged [4, 52]. That observation pushes the field away from viewing DBS as a fixed dose and toward thinking of it as a titratable, personalized therapy whose effects should be verified against mechanistic readouts when possible.

In that regard, within-subject mechanistic approaches are especially instructive. They shift the evidentiary center of gravity from distal endpoints that are multiply determined (abstinence vs relapse) to measurable processes that can plausibly link stimulation to behavior (e.g., cue-reactivity signatures) [44]. Even if the clinical literature is not yet large enough to adjudicate optimal targets and parameters, these designs at least offer a path toward explainable outcomes, such as why a given setting worked for a given patient, rather than relying on post hoc narratives.

At the same time, the OUD DBS evidence base still faces the standard interpretive challenge of early neurosurgical psychiatry trials: uncontrolled protocols often include intensive monitoring, frequent clinician contact, and structured support, each of which can independently improve outcomes [36, 45]. This does not invalidate the clinical signals, but it does mean that controlled designs, standardized co-interventions, and blinded phases (where feasible) remain essential if the field is to separate stimulation-specific effects from the benefits of enhanced care.

Stimulant use disorders

The stimulant literature remains the least mature clinically, but it contributes two crucial insights that should shape the next generation of trials. First, the literature highlights that the relevant circuitry may not be identical across drugs, and that it may be premature to assume that a single VS target will generalize [14, 58]. Second, it brings the adverse-effect profile into sharp focus: stimulation-induced activation (including hypomanic symptoms under some settings) is not just a side note in stimulant populations, it can be a limiting factor and a confound, given baseline vulnerability to impulsivity, sleep disruption, and affective instability [58].

The limited evidence exploring more ventral/extended limbic targets (e.g., accumbens shell/bed nucleus of the stria terminalis) is notable because it suggests alternative mechanistic hypotheses, perhaps more directly engaging stress and negative-affect circuitry that can maintain stimulant use even when hedonic reward wanes [14]. Meanwhile, methamphetamine case reports raise provocative biological observations, such as changes in striatal dopamine transporter levels after prolonged stimulation, but remain too sparse for firm inference [13, 67]. The overarching point is that the stimulant literature argues for substance informed neurobiology: trials should be explicit about which relapse pathway is being targeted (reward salience, habitual responding, negative reinforcement, affect dysregulation), rather than treating “addiction” as a single circuit problem.

Mechanistic and ethical lessons from the ablation literature

The ablation literature serves as both precedent and caution. Mechanistically, lesion approaches are hypothesized to reduce relapse vulnerability by interrupting ventral striatal and ventral capsular pathways that assign motivational salience to drug cues, mediate reinforcement learning, and link limbic drive with frontostriatal action selection. In NAc-centered procedures, the intended effect is not simply “removal” of a craving center, but disruption of distributed mesocorticolimbic loops involving the NAc, orbitofrontal and medial prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, hippocampus, and thalamic projections. This rationale overlaps with DBS in that both modalities target reward-control circuitry; however, their biological and clinical effects may diverge. DBS may act through reversible modulation of firing patterns, oscillatory activity, synaptic plasticity, and network communication, whereas ablation produces irreversible tissue or fiber disruption and cannot be titrated after the lesion is made [11, 32, 63].

This distinction is particularly important in SUDs because ventral striatal circuits contribute to drug reward, natural reward, motivation, affect, and decision-making. Lesioning may reduce drug-related salience in some patients, but it may also alter non-drug reward processing, hedonic tone, impulsivity, sexual function, appetite, or broader motivation [59]. Therefore, ablation data should be framed as a cautionary historical comparator and as evidence that the target network is behaviorally powerful, not as evidence that irreversible procedures and DBS carry the same risk–benefit profile.

Where the ablation literature becomes particularly informative for contemporary DBS is in its documentation of non-drug behavioral and motivational changes after VS intervention [59]. That line of evidence is a reminder that manipulating these networks may shift valuation and motivation globally, not just drug salience. For DBS trials, the implication is concrete: studies should prospectively assess hedonic tone, apathy, impulsivity, and quality of life, and should track non-drug reward domains and other addictive behaviors. Without these measures, clinically meaningful tradeoffs could remain invisible, particularly in short trials focused narrowly on substance-specific endpoints.

What preclinical studies add to interpretation and trial design

The preclinical literature does not “prove” efficacy, but it materially strengthens the field’s ability to design interpretable human trials by clarifying parameter dependence and candidate mechanisms. Across substances, rodent work repeatedly shows that DBS effects depend on (1) target subregion, (2) stimulation frequency and pattern, and (3) timing relative to extinction/reinstatement or relapse-like states. For example, multiple cocaine models suggest that NAc stimulation can reduce reinstatement under some conditions, yet effects vary by whether stimulation is continuous or patterned [18, 19, 22, 26, 47, 53, 54]. Ethanol studies illustrate the same principle from the opposite direction: in certain relapse-like paradigms, NAc DBS can augment alcohol intake, highlighting that neuromodulation can plausibly be pro-relapse depending on state and context [21, 24]. This is not a minor nuance; it means that human trials must be explicit about whether stimulation is intended to support abstinence maintenance, dampen cue-reactivity during early recovery, facilitate extinction learning, reduce negative affect, or accomplish multiple goals via adaptive programming. This point deserves special emphasis for trial design. In alcohol use disorder, the strongest clinical signal comes from NAc DBS, yet preclinical alcohol-dependent models also show that stimulation can worsen relapse-like drinking under some conditions [1, 21]. Rather than weakening the rationale for DBS, this underscores the need to define the intended therapeutic state: early abstinence, cue exposure, withdrawal/negative affect, extinction learning, or relapse prevention. It also supports careful parameter titration, prospective monitoring for increased craving or use, and consideration of adaptive or state-contingent stimulation paradigms rather than assuming continuous high-frequency stimulation will be uniformly therapeutic.

Another key preclinical insight is that “reward circuitry” is not confined to the NAc. Several morphine studies targeting cortical and limbic regions (e.g., mPFC, anterior cingulate) report modulation of CPP, reinstatement, and cognitive/affective measures, suggesting that targeting cognitive control and affect regulation circuits may be an alternative or complementary route to reducing relapse vulnerability [6, 10, 16, 34, 35, 38, 64]. Importantly, these studies encourage a “network” view consistent with emerging human findings: neuromodulation may act by shifting large-scale fronto-striatal balance rather than by locally silencing a single nucleus.

Finally, preclinical work on stimulation patterning adds a practical translational level. Studies examining intermittent versus continuous paradigms, and demonstrating that stimulation itself can carry reinforcing properties depending on parameters, highlight why human trials should not treat parameter selection as an afterthought [2, 8, 55, 61]. In short, the animal literature strengthens the argument that future clinical trials should incorporate mechanistic endpoints and adaptive approaches rather than assuming one-size-fits-all continuous high-frequency stimulation.

Safety signals and ethical implications

The results emphasize that surgical and device-related complications, while not ubiquitous, are clinically meaningful and must be incorporated into consent and trial governance. Across reports, infections requiring intervention and stimulation-related neuropsychiatric changes are the recurring high-salience risks. For example, device infection necessitating explantation occurred in the alcohol DBS pilot experience [5], and compliance challenges can lead to explantation in the context of OUD feasibility work [45]. Stimulation-related activation, ranging from transient mood elevation and insomnia during programming to more concerning hypomanic symptoms, appears across multiple opioid and stimulant reports, with the crack cocaine STN case demonstrating how hypomania can derail planned blinded assessments [4, 58]. In the context of SUDs, these risks carry added ethical weight because they intersect with baseline impulsivity, psychiatric comorbidity, and the real-world harms of relapse.

The clinical relevance of these adverse events should be judged against the severity and refractoriness of the treated illness. In patients with severe, treatment-refractory SUDs and repeated overdose, medical complications, or failed evidence-based therapies, reductions in craving, heavy-use days, or cue reactivity may be meaningful even without immediate complete abstinence. However, such benefits justify invasive intervention only if they are durable, functionally meaningful, and accompanied by careful monitoring for surgical complications, device-related burden, neuropsychiatric activation, suicidality, impulsivity, apathy/anhedonia, cognitive change, and shifts in non-drug reward behavior. This benefit-risk threshold is especially high for lesion approaches because adverse motivational or neuropsychological effects cannot be reversed by changing stimulation settings or turning off a device. These considerations also affect how newer lesioning technologies should be framed. MR-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS) may reduce procedural burden by enabling incisionless, MRI-guided lesioning without craniotomy or implanted hardware, but it remains an ablative and irreversible intervention once a lesion is made. Therefore, MRgFUS changes the invasiveness profile, but it does not eliminate the ethical issues associated with permanent manipulation of reward circuitry. A recent post-search case report of bilateral NAc MRgFUS for severe AUD described reduced craving, 12-month abstinence, and no serious adverse effects, but this single case should be interpreted as feasibility-generating rather than efficacy-establishing evidence [60].

Ethically, SUD neurosurgery is distinguished not only by the usual challenges of psychiatric DBS (identity, authenticity, affective change), but also by the risk of coercion and structural vulnerability. Many individuals with severe SUD have legal, financial, and interpersonal pressures that could distort voluntariness. The ablation experience in particular emphasizes why reversibility and long-term monitoring matter: irreversible lesions in reward circuitry can plausibly affect natural motivations and thus the patient’s broader life, not simply drug use [59]. Accordingly, the strongest ethical posture for future trials includes (1) independent consent monitors, (2) explicit protections against legal or institutional coercion, (3) robust post-operative psychosocial care commitments, and (4) fairness considerations to prevent neurosurgery from becoming either an inaccessible boutique intervention or a disproportionately applied intervention in marginalized groups.

Roadmap for next-generation trials

The results suggest several practical, method-focused changes that would make these studies easier to interpret and more useful clinically. One place to start is with endpoints that fit with what DBS is actually supposed to do. If the goal is to dampen cue reactivity and compulsive drive, not guarantee perfect abstinence, then it makes sense for primary outcomes to include things like proportion of abstinent days, number of heavy-use days, validated craving/compulsitivity measures, and cue-reactivity indices, instead of treating “time to first lapse” as the only meaningful marker. The alcohol RCT makes this point clearly, a trial can miss its primary endpoint and still show secondary effects that are better aligned with the mechanism and arguably more clinically informative [1]. Another key step is to build mechanistic assessments into the design from the outset. Approaches that use electrophysiology or imaging to guide personalization, such as combining cue-reactivity tasks with intracranial signatures, should be specified prospectively rather than introduced later as explanatory add-ons [44, 52]. Along the same lines, metabolic imaging patterns linked to sustained abstinence in early OUD work point to candidate biomarkers that can be carried forward and tested directly in future trials [45]. In addition, co-interventions need to be handled more rigorously; psychosocial treatment “dose,” medication-assisted treatment, toxicology testing schedules, and relapse definitions should be prespecified and reported in a standardized way, because abstinence rates are not comparable across cohorts when these elements vary or go unreported. Finally, the field needs more sham-controlled comparison and longer follow-up when feasible. Blinding is difficult in DBS and can be undermined by stimulation side effects of obvious clinical changes, but without carefully monitored sham conditions and follow-up periods that match the chronic, relapsing nature of SUDs, it will be hard to draw firm conclusions about efficacy and durability. Next-generation trials should also incorporate newer neuromodulation technologies. Adaptive DBS is especially relevant for SUDs because craving, cue reactivity, withdrawal, stress, and relapse risk are state-dependent rather than constant. Adaptive DBS work in Parkinson disease demonstrates the feasibility of closed-loop stimulation that responds to changing neural states, and biomarker-informed OUD DBS demonstrates the feasibility of linking stimulation parameters to individualized craving circuitry [43, 44]. A current individualized adaptive DBS trial in OUD is evaluating whether invasive brain mapping can guide personalized stimulation to reduce craving and opioid use [40]. These approaches may help reduce unnecessary stimulation, avoid stimulation-induced activation, and test whether closed-loop or state-contingent stimulation is superior to continuous DBS.

Focused ultrasound technologies should be discussed separately by mechanism. MRgFUS is best conceptualized as a less invasive method for creating an irreversible lesion, whereas low-intensity focused ultrasound (LIFU) is a non-ablative neuromodulation strategy that may transiently modulate deep reward circuitry without implanted hardware. Although LIFU was outside the eligibility criteria of this invasive neurosurgical review, early SUD studies suggest feasibility and tolerability. A 2023 pilot study of bilateral NAc LIFU in four participants reported no structural MRI changes and acute craving reductions, and a 2025 open-label study in eight participants with severe OUD reported no serious device-related adverse events, sustained reductions in cue-induced opioid craving, and urine-toxicology-supported decreases in substance use over 90 days [37, 46]. These early data support LIFU as a future comparator or stepwise intervention before invasive DBS or irreversible lesioning, but larger sham-controlled trials are needed.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations. The primary limitation of this manuscript is the immaturity and heterogeneity of the underlying human evidence base. Across substances, most clinical reports are case studies or small, open-label cohorts with substantial variation in targets (and target subregions), stimulation parameters, programming intensity, follow-up duration, and concurrent treatments (psychosocial interventions and pharmacotherapy), making results difficult to compare directly and highly vulnerable to confounding. Invasive SUD trials are also particularly susceptible to non-specific effects such as enhanced monitoring, frequent clinician contact, expectancy, perioperative stabilizations, and regression to the mean, while DBS-specific challenges (e.g., stimulation-related mood/activation effects) can undermine blinding and complicate causal attribution. Outcome reporting is inconsistent, with variable relapse definitions and uneven use of objective toxicology schedules; safety reporting is similarly nonuniform and may under-capture neuropsychiatric tradeoffs. Finally, because ventral striatal/limbic interventions can plausibly affect motivation broadly, the relative undermeasurement of domains such as hedonic tone/anhedonia, apathy, impulsivity, cognition, and quality of life limits the ability to characterize benefit-risk profiles beyond substance-focused endpoints. Another major limitation is that this review’s synthesis is necessarily narrative and hypothesis-generating, rather than quantitatively definitive. Given the heterogeneity in study designs, endpoints, and reporting quality, meta-analytic pooling is not currently well-justified, and formal risk-of-bias assessment is constrained by incomplete methodological detail in many reports. Publication bias is also a concern as the literature is dominated by positive case reports and single-center experiences, and the generalizability of findings is limited by highly selected patient populations able to engage in intensive longitudinal follow-up. In addition, ablation studies and DBS trials arise from different clinical, regulatory, and ethical contexts, while preclinical models only partially capture the social, psychiatric, and environmental determinants that shape human relapse. Collectively, these limitations suggest that current conclusions support clinical plausibility and can help guide trial design, but they do not yet establish definitive efficacy, optimal targets, or programming strategies across SUD subtypes. Finally, rapidly emerging technologies such as adaptive DBS, MRgFUS lesioning, and LIFU neuromodulation are evolving faster than the current clinical evidence base. Because the formal search ended on September 9, 2025 and excluded non-invasive neuromodulation, post-search reports and LIFU studies were discussed only as future directions and were not included in the PRISMA count or primary clinical efficacy synthesis. One preprint captured within the original search was updated to its peer-reviewed publication during revision.

Conclusion

Neurosurgical modulation of reward circuitry, most commonly targeting the NAc and adjacent VC/ALIC, has produced encouraging but still preliminary signals in severe, treatment-refractory substance use disorders. Across alcohol, opioid/heroin, and stimulant use disorders, the clinical literature describes reductions in craving and, in some cases, sustained abstinence or marked decreases in consumption. However, the current evidence remains dominated by small uncontrolled studies and single-case reports, and even the strongest controlled data suggest that benefits may be more apparent on secondary measures of drinking behavior, craving, anhedonia, and cure reactivity than on time-to-relapse-style primary endpoints. Taken together, the human evidence supports clinical plausibility and trial design, but not yet definitive efficacy or generalizable target selection across SUD subtypes. DBS and lesion approaches should therefore be advanced along different evidentiary and ethical pathways. DBS warrants further study in sham-controlled, biomarker-informed, and potentially adaptive designs because it is programmable and can be modified in response to benefit or adverse effects. Lesion approaches, including newer incisionless methods such as MRgFUS, may reduce procedural burden but remain irreversible and should require especially strong evidence of durable benefit, comprehensive neuropsychological monitoring, and rigorous consent safeguards before broader clinical adoption.
