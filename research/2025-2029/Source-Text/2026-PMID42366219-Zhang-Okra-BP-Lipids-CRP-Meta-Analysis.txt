Primary Abstract:
Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Okra (Abelmoschus esculentus), a traditional plant rich in fiber, flavonoids, and antioxidants, has been suggested to improve glycemic control, lipid metabolism, and inflammation, but clinical evidence remains inconsistent. The major objective of our review was to elucidate the impact of okra supplementation on blood pressure, lipid profile, and inflammation in patients with prediabetes, T2DM, and diabetic nephropathy. Eligible studies were identified through PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus up to February 17, 2026. Pooled weighted mean differences (WMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using random-effects models. Ten studies were included. Okra supplementation significantly reduced total cholesterol (14.16 mg/dL, 95% CI: -22.39, -5.92, P < 0.001), LDL-C (-8.51 mg/dL, 95% CI: -14.92, -2.11, P = 0.009), triglycerides (-15.43 mg/dL, 95% CI: -28.67, -2.19, P = 0.022), diastolic blood pressure (-1.17 mmHg; 95% CI: -2.28 to -0.06), and CRP (-2.28 mg/dL; 95% CI: -2.70 to -1.86). No significant effects were observed for systolic blood pressure or HDL-C overall. Okra supplementation exerts beneficial effects on lipid profiles, diastolic blood pressure, and systemic inflammation in individuals with prediabetes, T2DM, and diabetic nephropathy, suggesting a potential role as an adjunctive therapy for reducing CVD risk.

Verified Round 5 Primary Facts & Critical Appraisal:
Systematic review and meta-analysis (Nutr Diabetes 2026; PMC13572387, OA full-text verified, searched through Feb 2026) pooling 10 studies in prediabetes, T2DM, and diabetic nephropathy. Showed modest resting DBP reduction (-1.17 mmHg, 95% CI -2.28 to -0.06), NULL resting SBP, lower CRP (-2.28 mg/dL), and lower TC/LDL-C/TG. Confirms no human orthostatic BP or clinical neurological symptom trials exist for okra.

Diet is recommended as the first-line management strategy for CVD [7]. Alternative treatments, such as herbal plants with antioxidant properties, help reduce stress and control diabetes [8–10]. One such natural traditional plant is okra, which has antioxidant activity [11]. Okra, from the Malvaceae family, contains flavonoids that contribute to its antioxidant effects [12]. Okra also contains soluble fiber, including gums and pectin, and other vital nutrients. Soluble fibers are successful in lowering cholesterol concentrations in the serum and reducing CVD risk [13]. Okra not only contains soluble and insoluble fiber but is also a rich source of vitamins (A, C, K, and several B-complex vitamins such as folate) and minerals (including calcium, magnesium, potassium, and iron), in addition to carbohydrates, thereby providing significant health benefits for humans [14]. A systematic review reported that okra had a positive impact on hyperlipidemia and hyperglycemia due to its antioxidant effect [16]. A pre-clinical study demonstrated that okra achieves a lipid-lowering effect and could thus be used as a cardiovascular-friendly vegetable [17]. Okra consumption, regardless of exercise training, resulted in a cardioprotective effect in rats [18]. [Materials and Methods] This study utilized a systematic review combined with meta-analysis to assess the effects of okra supplementation on blood pressure, lipid profiles, and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels in individuals with T2DM. The research methodology was guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) framework to ensure methodological rigor and transparency. Although this review was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO, all procedures strictly adhered to PRISMA standards, employing predefined eligibility criteria and analytical strategies to reduce potential bias and strengthen the reliability of the findings [24]. A comprehensive literature search was conducted across multiple electronic databases, including Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed/Medline and Google scholar for studies available until February 17, 2026. The search strategy incorporated a blend of Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) and non-MeSH keywords: (“Okra” OR “Abelmoschus esculentus”) AND (“Randomized controlled study” OR “Clinical Trials” OR “Intervention Studies” OR “intervention” OR “controlled trial” OR “trial” OR “randomized” OR “placebo” OR “random” OR “assignment”) (see Supplementary Table 1). Additionally, we searched trial registries, including ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, to identify ongoing or unpublished studies. Reference lists of relevant systematic reviews were also manually screened to capture any studies that may have been missed during the database and registry searches. Grey literature sources such as dissertations and conference proceedings were not included, as our focus was on peer-reviewed studies to ensure methodological rigor and reliability of the findings.
