Background

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder after Alzheimer’s disease. Hereditary causes account for 10–15% of cases but present considerably more complex clinical manifestations than idiopathic cases. It is therefore imperative to enhance clinicians’ recognition of hereditary forms.

Methods

This study enrolled 29 patients with early‑onset or familial parkinsonism. 17 cases were identified with reportable genetic findings using PD-targeted or movement disorders gene panels. Clinical data and genetic data were collected.

Results

Among 17 patients, PARK2/PRKN (n = 9) and PARK8/LRRK2 (n = 5) were predominant subtypes, with two cases of PARK14/PLA2G6, and one case of PARK18/EIF4G1. Clinically, bradykinesia affected 17/17 patients, rigidity 15/17, tremor 13/17, and dystonia 7/17 (including one oculogyric crisis). Hyperreflexia occurred in 6/17, cognitive impairment in 2/17, pyramidal signs in 2/17, ataxic dysarthria 1/17, and pes cavus in 1/17. Diurnal fluctuation and sleep benefit occurred exclusively in PARK2/PRKN and PARK14/PLA2G6 patients. Among patients with PRKN, 7/9 exhibited diurnal fluctuations: five had exon rearrangements, one had a splicing mutation, and one had a homozygous variant. Conversely, the two PARK2/PRKN patients without diurnal fluctuations showed no exon deletions/duplications. PARK18/EIF4G1 mutations may manifest incomplete penetrance.

Conclusion

Among PARK2/PRKN patients in our cohort, a potential association between diurnal fluctuation and exon rearrangements may exist, although this observation requires cautious interpretation. Furthermore, these variants appear to cluster within the N-terminal region of the protein (the start of the RING1 domain and preceding regions). Beyond the common PARK2/PRKN subtype, PARK14/PLA2G6 may also be linked to diurnal fluctuation and sleep benefit.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder after Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [1], affecting approximately 1% of individuals aged over 60 years and 4% of those over 85 years worldwide [2,3]. The clinical diagnosis of PD is primarily based on a combination of typical motor and nonmotor symptoms, including bradykinesia, tremor and rigidity, anosmia, sleep disorders, and autonomic abnormalities [4,5]. However, the low initial diagnostic accuracy of 58% severely hinders early intervention and disease management [6]. While most PD cases are sporadic, approximately 10%–15% of all PD patients and 25% of early-onset PD (EOPD) cases have a clear familial genetic background, indicating that genetic variation is a core pathogenic factor for PD, especially for early-onset and atypical PD subtypes [7,8].

Genetic studies have confirmed that multiple pathogenic genes with different inheritance patterns are closely associated with PD occurrence, and significant genotype-phenotype heterogeneity widely exists in genetic PD [9]. Existing studies have demonstrated that different PD gene mutations correspond to distinct clinical manifestations, disease progression patterns, and neuropathological features. Over 90% of genetically-related phenotypes are complex or atypical, with dystonia, abnormal cognition, pyramidal signs, neuropsychiatric disorders, abnormal imaging, and abnormal eye movements [10]. For instance, LRRK2 mutations usually induce typical idiopathic PD-like phenotypes [11], while SNCA mutations cause highly variable clinical spectrums ranging from typical EOPD to dementia with Lewy bodies [9]. Mutations in PRKN and PINK1 mostly lead to slowly progressive EOPD, whereas other recessive gene variants tend to cause severe atypical parkinsonism with complex clinical phenotypes [9]. In the Chinese population, the mutational spectrum of EOPD is dominated by PRKN, GBA, and LRRK2 mutations [12–14].

Marked diurnal fluctuation and sleep benefit, alongside a marked and sustained response to low-dose levodopa, are considered hallmark features of dopa-responsive dystonia (DRD) [15]. However, Yamamura et al. first described autosomal recessive familial cases presenting with early-onset parkinsonism accompanied by prominent diurnal fluctuation and sleep benefit as early as 1973 [16]. Symptoms are relatively mild in the morning and deteriorate in the late afternoon or evening, a pattern that differs from classic PD, in which symptoms worsen in the morning. Sleep benefit is a relatively common phenomenon: previous studies have reported that approximately 45% of patients exhibit sleep benefit after waking [17]. Although this phenomenon has been studied for decades, and multiple factors have been proposed to correlate with sleep benefit in earlier investigations, these associations remain controversial [18].

Against this background, the present study enrolled a cohort of 17 Chinese patients with early-onset or familial parkinsonism and conducted a retrospective observational study. The core research objectives were as follows: (1) to clarify the genetic subtype distribution and summarize the phenotypic characteristics of the enrolled patients; (2) to screen and identify previously unreported PD pathogenic variants in the Chinese population; (3) to further explore the genotype–phenotype correlations of hereditary parkinsonism in this local cohort. This study aims to supplement the clinical genetic data of PD in the Chinese population and provide additional observational evidence for genotype–phenotype correlations, especially for the phenotypes of diurnal fluctuation and sleep benefit.

Methods

Patients

Patients with clinically diagnosed early‑onset or familial parkinsonism who underwent genetic testing at the Department of Neurology, the Hebei Medical University Third Hospital between January 1, 2016 and June 30, 2025, and who had not received anti‑parkinsonian treatment were retrospectively enrolled. Demographic and clinical data, including sex, age, family history, past medical history (medication use and exposure to heavy metals or other toxic substances), current illness, neurological examination (performed by at least two specialist neurologists), routine laboratory tests and cranial MRI, were extracted from the electronic medical record system. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hebei Medical University Third Hospital (ethics approval number: W2025-058-1). The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardians.

Inclusion criteria: Patients presenting with parkinsonism should fulfill one of the following conditions: age at onset ≤50 years, or age at onset ≥50 years with a positive family history (i.e., at least one first or second degree relative diagnosed with Parkinson’s disease or manifesting parkinsonism), together with a favorable/partial response to levodopa benserazide (≥15% improvement in MDS UPDRS III motor score) [19].

Based on the 2015 MDS‑PD diagnostic criteria [20], parkinsonism is defined as bradykinesia, in combination with either rest tremor, rigidity, or both. Bradykinesia is defined as slowness of movement and decrement in amplitude or speed (or progressive hesitations/halts) as movements are continued. Bradykinesia can be evaluated by using finger tapping (MDS-UPDRS 3.4), hand movements (3.5), pronation-supination movements (3.6), toe tapping (3.7), and foot tapping (3.8). Rigidity refers to “leadpipe” resistance. Rest tremor refers to a 4- to 6-Hz tremor in the fully resting limb, which is suppressed during movement initiation.

Exclusion criteria: Patients with identifiable etiologies underlying secondary parkinsonism were excluded, including those with exposure to specific medications (e.g., chlorpromazine, haloperidol, risperidone, olanzapine, metoclopramide, flunarizine, cinnarizine); toxin exposure (e.g., carbon monoxide, manganese, pesticides, organic solvents); recurrent stroke; infections (e.g., encephalitis, syphilis, HIV); inflammatory demyelinating disorders; and metabolic disorders (e.g., thyroid or parathyroid dysfunction, Wilson’s disease).

Gene testing

Two distinct sequencing panels were adopted in this study: a comprehensive PD-targeted panel (covering next-generation sequencing (NGS) for point mutations, MLPA, and repeat expansion screening) and a movement disorder (MD) panel (integrating NGS, capture-based CNV (Cap-CNV) analysis, and repeat expansion screening). The MD panel provides broader gene coverage. Based on the PD-targeted sequencing framework, it additionally includes genes responsible for hereditary spastic paraplegia, ataxia, peripheral neuropathy, leukoencephalopathy, and other related neurological disorders. The gene lists for the PD-targeted panel and the MD panel are provided in S1 Table and S2 Table, respectively.

DNA Library Preparation: Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples obtained from patients and their parents using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Standard libraries were constructed with the DNA Sample Prep Reagent Set (MyGenostics, Beijing, China) and included end repair, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. The constructed libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Enrichment and Sequencing of Targeted Genes: Amplified DNA libraries were captured using the targeted gene capture panel (MyGenostics Inc., Beijing, China). Biotinylated 100-bp capture probes were designed to tile across all coding exons plus 50 bp flanking regions of target genes. Capture was performed per the manufacturer’s protocol. Raw sequencing was performed using paired-end reads of 2 × 150 bp. The mean sequencing depth for target regions was at least 300×, and the proportions of target bases covered at ≥10× and ≥20 × were at least 98% and 96%, respectively. Bioinformatics Analysis: Raw sequencing data were stored in FASTQ format. MGI sequencing adapters and low-quality reads (<80 bp) were trimmed using Cutadapt (http://code.google.com/p/cutadapt/). Clean reads were mapped to the UCSC hg19 human reference genome using the BWA module within Sentieon software (https://www.sentieon.com/). Duplicate reads were removed, and base quality recalibration was performed with Sentieon. Base recalibration adjusts base quality scores to better reflect the true probability of read-to-reference mismatches. Mapped reads were used for variant calling. SNPs and InDels were detected using Sentieon; variant data were converted to VCF format. Variants were annotated with ANNOVAR (http://annovar.openbioinformatics.org/en/latest/) against multiple databases, including the 1000 Genomes Project, ESP6500, dbSNP, ExAC, an in-house MyGenostics database, and HGMD. Functional predictions were generated using SIFT, PolyPhen-2, MutationTaster, and GERP++. Variant Selection: Benign and likely benign variants were excluded per American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines [21]. For quality control, retained variants were required to meet MutCount ≥10 and MutRatio ≥0.3; variants with insufficient sequencing support were discarded. Variants annotated as pathogenic in HGMD, ClinVar, and the MyGenostics internal case database were retained. Variants present in public population databases (1000G, ESP6500) and our in-house healthy control database were excluded. Synonymous variants lacking pathogenic evidence in HGMD were excluded, as were noncoding variants without HGMD-documented pathogenicity. Priority was assigned to exonic and splice-site variants. Population allele frequency thresholds were applied according to inheritance patterns: MAF ≤ 0.02 for recessive disorders and MAF ≤ 0.001 for dominant disorders. Remaining variants were further curated by integrating disease inheritance patterns and patients’ clinical phenotypes to identify candidate pathogenic variants. Sanger sequencing was performed to validate all candidate pathogenic variants.

Results

A total of 29 cases meeting enrollment criteria were collected, with 17 demonstrating positive genetic findings and 12 negative. The negative group had a mean age at onset of 36.6 years; two patients were under 20 years, while seven were over 40 years old. Among 17 patients, PARK2/PRKN (n = 9) and PARK8/LRRK2 (n = 5) were predominant, with two cases of PARK14/PLA2G6, and one case of PARK18/EIF4G1 (Clinical and genetic data of the 17 patients are summarized in Table 1 and Table 2). No GBA mutations were detected despite their reported high frequency in Asian populations [12]. Clinically, bradykinesia affected 17/17 patients, rigidity 15/17, tremor 13/17, and dystonia 7/17 (including one oculogyric crisis). Hyperreflexia occurred in 6/17, cognitive impairment in 2/17, pyramidal signs in 2/17, ataxic dysarthria 1/17, and pes cavus in 1/17. Three patients with combined tremor had both resting and postural tremor (Patient 5, 6, and 17).

Discussion

Diurnal fluctuation and sleep benefit in hereditary parkinsonism

Sleep benefit in PD denotes improved motor symptoms upon awakening after a medication-free night. Yamamura et al. (1973) [24] first described ‘paralysis agitans of early onset with marked diurnal fluctuation’. Subsequent reports identified similar features predominantly in juvenile PD (JPD, onset ≤21 years), frequently associated with PRKN, PINK1, and DJ-1 mutations [12]. Sleep benefit occurs in up to 55% of such patients, lasting several hours and sometimes following daytime naps [25,26]. Although proposed several decades ago, this phenomenon remains difficult to investigate, with its true prevalence and mechanisms still unclear. Physiological explanations have been hypothesized, favoring replenishment of dopaminergic stores in nigral terminals during sleep [25–28]. Traditionally, diurnal fluctuation, sleep benefit, and a dramatic, sustained levodopa response are hallmark features of DRD [15,29]. Adult-onset DRD may present with focal dystonia and parkinsonism [29]. Thus, distinguishing JPD from DRD based on atypical symptoms can be challenging. In DRD, diurnal fluctuation is prominent in early childhood but diminishes during the second decade, attributed to tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) short half-life and circadian oscillation of tyrosine hydroxylase activity at dopaminergic terminals in younger patients (activity decreases during wakefulness and increases during rest) [30,31]. However, patients with PRKN in the study who exhibited significant diurnal fluctuation persisted into their third or fourth decades. In a study of 43 autosomal recessive-EOPD patients, marked diurnal fluctuation occurred in 41 cases (95.3%), absent only in the two with onset after age 40, while most patients reported even 30–40 years post-onset [32]. Additionally, parkinsonism with diurnal fluctuation is also documented in other hereditary disorders, including ataxia telangiectasia, spinocerebellar ataxia type (SCA) 3, hereditary spastic paraplegia type 8 and type 11 [33–36].

Interestingly, in the study, 5 of 7 PARK2/PRKN patients exhibiting diurnal fluctuation carried exon rearrangement mutations. In contrast, no exon rearrangements were detected in patients without such fluctuations. Furthermore, these variants were predominantly clustered in the N-terminal region of the protein (the start of the RING1 domain and preceding regions). The core functions of the N-terminal region of the Parkin protein up to the RING1 domain-including the Ubl domain, linker region, and RING0-are to maintain an autoinhibited resting state, mediate PINK1-dependent activation, and act as a molecular switch regulating E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. The RING1 domain and the subsequent C-terminal domains, including IBR, REP, and RING2, constitute the catalytic core of the Parkin protein. It is possible that such phenotypic heterogeneity is attributable to differential functional impairment. Accordingly, large-scale studies are still needed to perform comprehensive statistical analyses.

In a separate study reported by Yamamura et al. [32], they investigated 95.3% of their cohort exhibited diurnal fluctuations. Among 37 patients from 16 families who underwent genetic testing, no pathogenic variants were identified in 16 patients from 5 families, while exon rearrangements were detected in the remaining patients. These findings may suggest a potential association between diurnal fluctuation and PRKN exon rearrangements, although this observation requires cautious interpretation.

DRD exhibits distinct gender predilection compared with other disorders, with an incidence two to three times higher in females than males. Among cases of hereditary parkinsonism with diurnal fluctuation, reported sex differences are inconsistent: one study identified sleep benefit to be more prevalent in males (65%) than females (42%) [28]. Both Zhong Rui et al. [18] and Yamamura et al. [32] detected no significant sex differences in PD patients with diurnal fluctuation. Nevertheless, the meta-analysis by Zhong Rui et al. demonstrated lower sleep efficiency in patients presenting sleep benefit, a finding that challenges the classical hypothesis of dopaminergic store replenishment within nigral terminals during sleep. However, most participants included in that meta-analysis had sporadic PD, and several received relevant pharmacotherapy. Thus, the influencing factors and mechanisms underlying sleep benefit in untreated hereditary parkinsonism warrant further investigation.

PARK2/PRKN

PRKN is the most common cause of autosomal recessive early-onset parkinsonism [37,38]. Bradykinesia is the most common sign, followed by tremor (especially in the legs), rigidity, dystonia, and postural instability [39]. Atypical features such as anterocollis, spasticity, and upper motor neuron signs, as well as cognitive decline, each occur in only about 3% of cases [39]. Although bradykinesia is the most common initial sign of PRKN- associated parkinsonism, one report found that 42% of patients reported dystonia as an initial sign [40]. In the PARK2/PRKN patients of this study, pyramidal signs, cognitive decline, pes cavus and ataxic dysarthria were observed. PRKN mutations have been reported in some cases of progressive supranuclear palsy, restless legs syndrome, and essential tremor [41]. Notably, one patient with a heterozygous PRKN variant (likely pathogenic), who was excluded from the final cohort of this study, exhibited isolated postural tremor responsive to alcohol, a feature consistent with essential tremor. His mother had similar symptoms and harbored the identical pathogenic variant. Nevertheless, a causal link between PRKN variants and essential tremor cannot be established based on this single case. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether this phenotype represents a preclinical or early stage of PD, which warrants long-term clinical follow-up.

Notably, although PRKN mutations were traditionally presumed to follow autosomal recessive inheritance, increasing evidence suggested that heterozygous mutations might also be pathogenic or increase susceptibility to typical late-onset PD [42–44]. Patient 9, with a heterozygous variant that has previously been reported to be associated with PD [45], exhibited a complex parkinsonism phenotype with a partial response to levodopa. Additionally, whole-exome sequencing performed previously at an external hospital revealed no additional pathogenic variants in this patient. Although autosomal dominant inheritance has been considered, this case warrants further exploration and validation.

PARK8/LRRK2

While the motor symptoms of LRRK2-related parkinsonism are largely indistinguishable from sporadic PD, significant differences exist in age at onset (AAO), disease penetrance, and neuropathology [8]. Neuropathology ranges from nigrostriatal degeneration with variable Lewy body and Alzheimer’s pathology to absence of these features or atypical findings [8]. LRRK2 carriers also typically exhibit better olfactory function, less frequent rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (RBD), and diverse atypical features including orthostatic hypotension, dementia, and hallucinations [3]. LRRK2 parkinsonism generally progresses slower. In this study, all PARK8 patients presented with IPD except one case accompanied by cognitive decline. One patient had an onset age of 59 years; genetic testing was prompted by a family history of PD. Otherwise, patients at this age would usually not be considered for genetic screening.

In summary, our findings may advance the understanding of this disorder. Among PARK2/PRKN patients in our cohort, a potential association between diurnal fluctuation and exon rearrangements may exist, although this observation requires cautious interpretation. Furthermore, these variants appear to cluster within the N-terminal region of the protein (the start of the RING1 domain and preceding regions). Beyond the common PARK2/PRKN subtype, PARK14/PLA2G6 may also be linked to diurnal fluctuation and sleep benefit. Nevertheless, several limitations of this study should be noted. As a single-center retrospective investigation with a small sample size, selection bias may be present, especially concerning missed GBA variant detection. Moreover, a subset of patients was analyzed using a movement disorder gene panel. While the panel contained cap-CNV analysis, it is less sensitive than MLPA for large GBA rearrangements, potentially contributing to undetected GBA variants. In addition, diurnal fluctuations and sleep benefit were assessed via patient self-report without quantitative measurements.
