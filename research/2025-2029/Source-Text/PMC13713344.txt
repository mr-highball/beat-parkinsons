Background

Anxiety and depressive symptoms are common, and dietary patterns have been proposed as a modifiable influence on them. The majority of the evidence rests on composite adherence indices such as the Mediterranean Diet Adherence Screener (MEDAS), which sums its components as if they all contributed equally and addresses depression more often than anxiety. Whether individual dietary components relate to symptoms independently of the composite index has rarely been tested, and rarely alongside social media use, a further exposure proposed to shape both diet and mood.

Methods

An anonymous online survey in English, Spanish, and Russian was completed by 115 adults aged 18 to 65 years with no previous diagnosis of mental illness, comprising a 14-item modified Mediterranean Diet Score (mMEDS), the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), and questions on daily time spent on Instagram, Facebook, and Twitter/X. Four primary hypotheses tested the associations of olive oil intake and red and processed meat intake with each HADS subscale; all other comparisons were exploratory. The Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied within each family. Effect sizes are reported with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals. The primary comparisons were additionally adjusted for sex, age band, and educational level.

Results

All four primary estimates lay in the hypothesized direction. Higher olive oil intake was associated with lower anxiety scores (r = −0.249, 95% CI − 0.448 to −0.050, p = 0.021), and restricted red and processed meat intake was associated with lower anxiety and depression scores after adjustment (β = −2.12, p = 0.021; β = −1.62, p = 0.032). None survived correction for multiple testing. The mMEDS total did not appear to be related to either subscale (ρ = −0.045 and −0.141), while item-level associations differed in direction across food groups.

Conclusion

Component-level signals appeared where the composite showed none, which may indicate that summing adherence items can obscure relationships running in opposite directions between foods. For studies using MEDAS-type indices, testing components alongside the total score could be worthwhile. Olive oil quantity and red and processed meat intake may be candidates for confirmatory work.

Introduction

Anxiety disorders and major depressive disorders collectively affect an estimated 970 million individuals worldwide (1). Together, they represent one of the most prevalent forms of mental illness (2). Personal consequences of suffering from anxiety or depression include impaired occupational functioning, relationship difficulties, an elevated risk of physical comorbidity, and premature mortality (3). Both conditions follow a relapsing–remitting course in a substantial proportion of patients (4). Overall, 68% of patients with depression do not reach remission after a first-line course of antidepressant treatment. One-third of individuals with depression fail to achieve an adequate response to first-line pharmacological treatments (5, 6). Depression ranks as the fourth leading cause of the global burden of disease (7). Anxiety disorders impose comparable disability-adjusted costs (8).

Diet is increasingly recognized as an important factor influencing mental health (9). The growing burden of anxiety and depression has increased interest in lifestyle factors that may improve mental health (10). However, the association between adherence to healthy diet patterns, such as the Mediterranean diet, and anxiety and depression has been paradoxical. Several research groups have reported an association between adherence to Mediterranean-style dietary patterns and lower rates of depression and anxiety (11, 12). However, these results are not always consistent, with other studies showing weak or indirectly mediated associations (13, 14).

The conflicting findings reported in previous studies may be related to variability across social, ethnic, educational, sex, and age groups, as well as differences in physical activity and other lifestyle factors. One potentially important factor that is often overlooked is social media use (15). Meta-analyses have shown that greater problematic social media use is associated with higher levels of anxiety and depression, particularly among younger adults (16–18). However, whether extensive social media use may shape individual food choices, and hence anxiety or depression, is still unknown.

To address these limitations, we conducted a cross-sectional pilot study using an international online survey assessing individual Mediterranean diet component adherence using the validated 14-item PREDIMED instrument; anxiety and depression using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale; and social media use across Instagram, Facebook, and Twitter/X. We applied several statistical methods to try to untangle the complex network of dietary and social media choices that might be correlated with anxiety and depression.

Methods

Study design and participants

Data were collected through an anonymous, cross-sectional online survey administered between 30 December 2023 and 25 January 2024 using Google Forms. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 to 65 years old without a previous diagnosis of mental illness. Recruitment was by convenience sampling: the authors and their students distributed the survey link through personal, institutional, and social media networks. The survey was conducted in English, Spanish, and Russian. The instruments were taken from their published English versions, and the Spanish and Russian versions were translated from these by native speakers of each language. Responses were harmonized to a single English label set before scoring. All three language versions are provided in Supplementary Material S1.

The country or region of residence was not recorded in the questionnaire. Since recruitment used open distribution of a survey link, the number of individuals who viewed the invitation is unknown, and a response rate cannot be calculated. No personal identifiers or IP addresses were collected, so no formal duplicate-prevention mechanism was applied. All 115 responses received were retained; no participant was excluded from the study. Where a participant did not answer an individual item, that participant was excluded pairwise from the comparisons involving that item only, and the number of participants contributing to each comparison is reported in the corresponding table. Both HADS subscales were complete for all 115 participants. Social media usage was reported by 98 participants for Instagram, 98 for Facebook, and 97 for Twitter/X. This was a pilot study using a convenience sample, and the final sample size of 115 participants reflects the number of respondents obtained during the recruitment window. No a priori sample size calculation was performed, and the analyses are accordingly presented as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Precision is conveyed by reporting a confidence interval for every estimate.

Ethics

The study was approved by the, Institute of Scientific Personnel Training of the National Academy of Sciences. Approval was granted in August 2021 for the research program under which this survey was conducted. All participants provided informed consent before beginning the survey by signing an informed consent sheet presented at the start of the questionnaire. No personal identifiers or IP addresses were collected, and data handling conformed to the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) requirements.

Dietary assessment

Diet was assessed using a modified 14-item Mediterranean Diet Score (mMEDS), adapted from the PREDIMED Mediterranean Diet Adherence Screener (MEDAS) (19, 20). The items cover the following: olive oil as the main cooking fat; daily olive oil consumption of four or more tablespoons; homemade vegetable sauces at least twice weekly; four or more daily servings of vegetables; three or more daily servings of fruit; nuts at least three times weekly; fish at least three times weekly; legumes at least three times weekly; preference for poultry over red meat; fewer than two weekly servings of red or processed meat; less than one daily tablespoon of butter, margarine, or cream; two or fewer weekly servings of processed snacks; fewer than one daily can of sugary beverages; and consumption of more than seven glasses of wine per week. Each item was scored 1 for adherence and 0 for non-adherence, giving a total score range of 0 to 14. No item was reverse-coded. Three items were administered at thresholds specific to this survey: vegetable intake of four or more servings daily, red and processed meat intake of fewer than two servings weekly, and wine intake of more than seven glasses weekly. The remaining 11 items applied the original criteria. This composite is referred to throughout as the mMEDS and is not compared with published MEDAS distributions (Figure 2).

Psychological assessment

Anxiety and depressive symptoms were assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) (21), a 14-item instrument comprising a 7-item anxiety subscale and a 7-item depression subscale, each item scored 0 to 3 and each subscale ranging from 0 to 21. Scores were classified as normal (0–7), borderline (8–10), or abnormal (11–21). Internal consistency in this sample was acceptable for both subscales (Cronbach’s α = 0.83, 95% CI 0.77 to 0.87 for anxiety; α = 0.72, 95% CI 0.59 to 0.81 for depression, bootstrap intervals from 5,000 resamples) (Figure 3).

Side-by-side histograms display the distribution of participants' HADS anxiety (left, blue) and depression (right, orange) subscale scores from zero to twenty-one, with dashed lines marking clinical cutoffs at scores of eight and eleven.

Social media assessment

Participants first indicated whether they used social media. Those who responded were asked to estimate their average daily time spent on Instagram, Facebook, and Twitter/X by selecting one of the five fixed response categories: 10 min or less, 30 min, 50 min, 70 min, and 90 min or more. These were pre-specified categories rather than free-text estimates, and they were assigned values of 10, 30, 50, 70, and 90 min for the analysis. The scale was censored at both ends, and no zero-min option was offered, so non-use of an individual platform was not separately identifiable. Only Instagram, Facebook, and Twitter/X were assessed (Figure 4).

Hypotheses

Four primary hypotheses were specified, covering two dietary components for which prior evidence exists, each tested with both HADS subscales. The evidence base addresses both anxiety and depressive symptoms, and hypotheses were specified for both outcomes accordingly:

H1. Daily consumption of four or more tablespoons of olive oil is associated with lower HADS anxiety scores.

H2. Daily consumption of four or more tablespoons of olive oil is associated with lower HADS depression scores.

H3. Consumption of fewer than two weekly servings of red or processed meat is associated with lower HADS anxiety scores.

H4. Consumption of fewer than two weekly servings of red or processed meat is associated with lower HADS depression scores.

Statistical analysis

Continuous scores were compared between the adherent and non-adherent groups using the Mann–Whitney U test, with group-specific sample sizes, medians, and interquartile ranges reported alongside the U statistic. Effect size is expressed as the rank–biserial correlation, calculated as r = 2 U/(n₁n₂) – 1, with U computed for the adherent group, so that a negative value indicates lower outcome scores among adherent participants. Confidence intervals were obtained by bootstrap resampling within the groups (5,000 resamples). Comparisons across three or more groups used the Kruskal–Wallis test and were reported with the H statistic, degrees of freedom, and epsilon-squared. Associations between continuous measures used Spearman’s rank correlation. Associations between social media time and the binary adherence items were tested using the Mann–Whitney U test, comparing the distribution of reported daily minutes between adherent and non-adherent participants. As rank-based methods depend only on the ordering of the response categories, the specific minute values assigned do not affect the results. One hypothesis family was defined per outcome variable, and the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate within each family, with both unadjusted and adjusted values reported for every comparison. For the four primary comparisons, multivariable linear regression models were fitted with the HADS subscale score as the dependent variable and the dietary item, sex, age band, and educational level as independent variables. Analyses were conducted in Python 3 using pandas, SciPy, and statsmodels. Missing responses were handled by pairwise exclusion.

Results

Sample characteristics

Of the 115 participants, 84 (73.0%) were women, and 31 (27.0%) were men. Sixty-five participants (56.5%) were aged 18–25 years, with the remaining distributed across the 26–35 (n = 18), 36–45 (n = 18), 46–55 (n = 6), and 56–65 (n = 8) bands. Ninety-four participants (81.7%) held a university degree or higher, 13 (11.3%) had completed vocational training, and 8 (7.0%) had completed secondary studies.

The mMEDS total score had a mean of 7.88 (SD 2.33), a median of 8 (IQR 6–10), and a range of 2–14. HADS anxiety had a mean of 7.84 (SD 4.41), a median of 7 (IQR 5–10), and a range of 0–19; HADS depression had a mean of 5.41 (SD 3.62), a median of 5 (IQR 2–7), and a range of 0–19. Sixty-eight participants (59.1%) fell in the normal range for anxiety, 20 (17.4%) in the borderline range, and 27 (23.5%) in the abnormal range; for depression, the corresponding figures were 89 (77.4%), 15 (13.0%), and 11 (9.6%). Both subscales were complete for all 115 participants (Table 1).

Participants adherent to the olive oil quantity item had a median HADS anxiety score of 6 (IQR 4–10) compared with a score of 8 (IQR 6–11) among non-adherent participants (U = 1241.0, p = 0.021, q = 0.084, r = −0.249, 95% CI − 0.448 to −0.050). The same item compared with depression had medians of 5 (IQR 2–7) and 5 (IQR 3–7), respectively (p = 0.346, q = 0.346, r = −0.102, 95% CI − 0.307 to 0.109).

Participants adherent to the red and processed meat item had a median anxiety score of 6 (IQR 4–10) compared with a score of 9 (IQR 5–12) (p = 0.061, q = 0.110, r = −0.218, 95% CI − 0.443 to 0.019) among non-adherent participants, and those adherent to that item had a median depression score of 5 (IQR 2–7) compared with a score of 6 (IQR 4–7) (p = 0.083, q = 0.110, r = −0.201) among non-adherent participants.

In multivariable models adjusted for sex, age band, and educational level, three of the four associations reached nominal significance: olive oil and anxiety (β = −1.86, 95% CI − 3.54 to −0.18, p = 0.031), red and processed meat and anxiety (β = −2.12, 95% CI − 3.91 to −0.32, p = 0.021), and red and processed meat and depression (β = −1.62, 95% CI − 3.09 to −0.14, p = 0.032). Olive oil and depression did not reach nominal significance (β = −0.57, 95% CI − 1.98 to 0.83, p = 0.422). All models explained less than 10% of the variance in the outcome (R2 0.054 to 0.095).

Overall adherence, tested as a single summary exposure, was not associated with either subscale (mMEDS with anxiety ρ = −0.045, 95% CI − 0.239 to 0.151, p = 0.63; and depression ρ = −0.141, 95% CI − 0.316 to 0.046, p = 0.13). An 11-item score restricted to items administered as originally specified gave the same results (ρ = −0.047, p = 0.62 and ρ = −0.097, p = 0.30, respectively), as did a 13-item score excluding only the vegetable item (ρ = −0.066, p = 0.48 and ρ = −0.126, p = 0.18).

Exploratory comparisons: dietary items and psychological symptoms

Across the 12 remaining dietary items tested against each HADS subscale, no comparison reached p < 0.05 before correction; the smallest unadjusted values were 0.120 for anxiety and 0.146 for depression (Table 2).

Among demographic comparisons, women reported higher anxiety scores than men (U = 899.0, p = 0.011, r = −0.310), with no corresponding difference in depression (p = 0.466). Neither age band nor educational level was associated with either subscale (age band and anxiety H(4) = 0.355, p = 0.986; age band and depression H(4) = 2.098, p = 0.718; education and anxiety H(2) = 0.012, p = 0.994; education and depression H(2) = 2.222, p = 0.329; epsilon-squared below 0.01 throughout).

Exploratory comparisons: dietary items and social media use

For Instagram, adherence to the vegetable item was associated with lower reported use (median 30 min, IQR 10–70, compared with 50 min, IQR 30–90; p = 0.008, q = 0.109, r = −0.317), whereas adherence to the olive oil cooking-fat item was associated with higher reported use (median 50 compared with 30 min; p = 0.042, q = 0.247, r = +0.323). For Facebook, adherence to the sugary beverages item was associated with lower reported use (p = 0.001, q = 0.017, r = −0.310). This was the only association in the study to survive correction. Discussion

In this exploratory pilot survey of 115 adults, all 4 primary point estimates lay in the hypothesized direction, and 3 of the 4 appeared to reach nominal significance once sex, age band, and educational level were accounted for, although none survived correction for multiple testing. The confidence intervals were found to be wide, and the adjusted models explained less than a tenth of the variance in symptom scores, so these associations may be compatible with the small effects described in larger studies rather than constituting independent evidence of them.

The results are reported for both subscales. Previous studies have examined the relation between dietary components and depressive symptoms (22–27): A prospective cohort of 12,059 Spanish graduates reported a weak inverse association between olive oil consumption and incident depression (22), a randomized trial in 73 patients with major depression reported symptom improvement following daily extra-virgin olive oil (23), and a meta-analysis of 17 studies covering 241,738 participants reported an association between red and processed meat intake and depression (25). In this study, restricted red and processed meat intake appeared to be associated with lower depression scores once sex, age band, and educational level were accounted for (β = −1.62, 95% CI − 3.09 to −0.14, p = 0.032), which could be consistent with that body of work, although the unadjusted comparison did not reach nominal significance (p = 0.083). Olive oil intake did not appear to be associated with depression scores (p = 0.346 unadjusted, p = 0.422 adjusted). Neither comparison survived correction for multiple testing, and the confidence intervals remained wide enough that effects of the magnitude reported in those larger studies cannot be excluded.

The closest published analog to our design is a randomized trial in adults with severe obesity that used the HADS as its outcome measure and reported reductions in both anxiety and depressive symptoms following daily extra-virgin olive oil (24). The direction of our olive oil estimates appears to match, while the magnitude does not, which might be expected: An intervention trial in a clinical population and a cross-sectional survey of a young, healthy, highly educated convenience sample are not comparable designs.

Associations between dietary items and social media use were found to be heterogeneous in direction. Adherence to the vegetable item appeared to be associated with lower reported Instagram use, while adherence to the olive oil cooking-fat item appeared to be associated with higher use, and overall adherence did not appear to be related to use of any platform. Reports in the literature are similarly mixed: Social media use has been associated with skipping breakfast, greater consumption of unhealthy snacks and sugar-sweetened beverages, and lower fruit and vegetable intake (28, 29), while a study of nine platforms in Saudi adults found that daily Snapchat use was associated with poorer fruit and vegetable intake but daily LinkedIn and WhatsApp use were associated with a healthier diet (30), and social media exposure has also been used deliberately to increase vegetable consumption (31). These reports may suggest that the relationship between platform use and dietary behavior could depend on the platform, the content encountered, and the food group examined rather than on time spent online alone. Our observations appear to be consistent with this heterogeneity, although its source cannot be identified here, since content exposure was not assessed.

The single association surviving correction—between adherence to the sugary beverages item and lower reported Facebook use—may warrant cautious interpretation despite its adjusted value. Group medians were identical, and the difference appeared to lie in an upper tail of heavier users among non-adherent participants, so it might be better described as a difference in the distribution of heavy use than as a difference in typical use.

Inflammatory and neuroprotective pathways have been proposed as mechanisms linking olive oil intake to mental health, and an olive oil-rich diet has been reported to lower C-reactive protein and interleukin-6 compared with low-fat diets (32). No biological markers were measured in this study, and this background is offered as context for the associations observed rather than as an explanation of them. Similarly, adherence to the Mediterranean diet has been reported to depend on economic circumstances, social connectedness (33), perceived stress (34), physical activity, body weight, and meal timing (35, 36); none of these variables were measured in our sample, and they are summarized here for background purposes only.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. The sample was small, predominantly female (73.0%), young (56.5% were aged 18 to 25), highly educated (81.7% held a university degree or higher), and recruited through the authors’ networks, which may introduce selection bias and could limit generalizability to the wider adult population. Adjustment was possible only for sex, age band, and educational level, and so residual confounding by unmeasured lifestyle, socioeconomic, and clinical factors may remain; the adjusted models explained less than 10% of the variance in symptom scores. The cross-sectional design does not allow inferences about direction, and reverse causation is possible, in which anxiety symptoms might shape dietary choices and patterns of social media use rather than the reverse.

Dietary intake and social media use were self-reported. Three adherence items were administered at thresholds specific to this survey, and the composite score was reported as a modified score; sensitivity analyses using an 11-item score restricted to the items administered at the original thresholds returned the same results, and the analyses reported above were conducted at the item level. Social media use was captured using five fixed time categories for Instagram, Facebook, and Twitter/X, censored at both ends of its range and without a zero-minute option; for Facebook and Twitter/X, the lowest category was the modal response, so the distributions for these platforms are concentrated at the lower end of the scale. Video-based platforms were not assessed. The Spanish and Russian instruments were translated from the published English versions by native speakers, and the language of completion was not recorded, so measurement equivalence across versions was not assessed; internal consistency in the pooled sample was acceptable for both subscales. Country or region of residence was not recorded.

Numerous comparisons were performed, so the likelihood of false-positive findings may be elevated; associations that did not survive correction are reported as exploratory. The study should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Conclusion

Olive oil intake and restricted red and processed meat intake each appeared to be associated with lower anxiety scores, in the direction prior evidence would predict, and both associations appeared to hold after adjustment for sex, age band, and educational level. The effects are small and did not survive correction for multiple testing, and the study is accordingly reported as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

One further observation may be of more general interest. Overall adherence, tested as a summary exposure, did not appear to be related to symptom scores or to social media use, whereas several item-level associations did, and these differed across food groups, running in opposite directions for different items. A composite score sums those components as though they acted alike, and in this sample, that summation returned a null result while its constituents did not. This could be a reason to test dietary components individually rather than to rely on total adherence scores as the exposure of interest, and it might apply whether the specific associations reported here replicate.

Olive oil quantity and red and processed meat intake may be priorities for confirmatory testing in adequately powered samples, with both HADS subscales as outcomes.
