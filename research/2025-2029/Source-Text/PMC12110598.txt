Understanding the risk and protective factors associated with Parkinson’s disease (PD) is crucial for improving outcomes for patients, individuals at risk, healthcare providers, and healthcare systems. Studying these factors not only enhances our knowledge of the disease but also aids in developing effective prevention, management, and treatment strategies. This paper reviews the key risk and protective factors associated with PD, with a particular focus on the biological mechanisms underlying these factors. Risk factors include genetic mutations, racial predispositions, and environmental exposures, all of which contribute to an increased likelihood of developing PD or accelerating its progression. Conversely, protective factors, such as regular physical exercise, adherence to a Mediterranean diet, and higher urate levels, have the potential to reduce inflammation and support mitochondrial function, thereby mitigating the risk of disease. However, identifying and validating these factors presents significant challenges. These challenges include the absence of reliable biomarkers, intricate interactions between genetic and environmental components, and clinical heterogeneity observed in patients with PD. These barriers complicate the establishment of clear causal relationships and hinder the development of targeted preventive strategies. To overcome these challenges, we propose several solutions and recommendations. Understanding the mechanisms underlying risk factors may inform future research aimed at developing standardized and more accurate biomarkers for PD, facilitating earlier diagnosis and improved monitoring of disease progression. Additionally, we offer actionable recommendations for PD prevention and management tailored to healthy individuals, patients diagnosed with PD, and healthcare systems. These strategies aim to improve clinical outcomes, enhance the quality of life, and optimize healthcare delivery for PD.

1. Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by motor disabilities, making it the second leading cause of such disabilities in adults after stroke [1]. While PD itself is not typically the direct cause of death, it increases the mortality risk due to complications such as falls, pneumonia, and cardiovascular issues. Recent studies have shown a clear increase in PD-related mortality, with U.S. rates increasing by 63% from 1999 to 2019, and global trends reflecting similar patterns [2,3]. The disease is characterized by classical motor manifestations such as bradykinesia, rigidity, and gait disorders, which fluctuate as the disease progresses [1]. At its core, PD involves the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, leading to a decrease in dopamine levels in the striatum and the formation of intracellular aggregates, known as Lewy bodies [1]. The etiology of the disease involves both genetic and environmental factors, which are crucial for understanding and developing effective treatments and prevention strategies [1,4,5,6,7,8]. Genetic research has identified risk factors, such as mutations in genes like LRRK2 and GBA1, which are common in patients with PD, suggesting a genetic predisposition to the disease. These mutations vary across populations, offering the potential for more tailored treatments [5]. Environmental factors, including pesticides, heavy metals, and air pollution, may contribute to oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroinflammation, which are key mechanisms of PD progression [9]. Lifestyle factors may also play a protective role. Regular exercise, especially high-intensity aerobic activity, preserve dopamine-producing neurons and slows symptom progression [10]. Diets rich in antioxidants, polyphenols, and polyunsaturated fatty acids may reduce PD risk, and cognitive and social engagement has been linked to protection against cognitive decline in patients with PD, emphasizing the importance of mental stimulation [11]. Recent studies have also focused on the gut–brain axis and microbiome dysbiosis, offering new therapeutic possibilities [12,13].

This review aims to provide a comprehensive and integrative analysis of the risk and protective factors associated with PD, with a specific emphasis on the underlying biological mechanisms that drive these interactions. Unlike many existing reviews that primarily focus on epidemiological associations [6,14,15], this paper delves into the molecular and genetic basis of these factors, highlighting how they contribute to the pathogenesis or prevention of PD. Additionally, the review highlights the current challenges in identifying and validating these factors and proposes forward-looking solutions, including the development of novel biomarkers, particularly in the context of personalized medicine. What sets this review apart from other similar papers is its multidimensional approach. Beyond synthesizing scientific evidence, it bridges the gap between research and practice by offering actionable, stakeholder-specific recommendations tailored to healthy individuals, patients, and healthcare providers. It also places a strong emphasis on education and awareness, often neglected yet crucial components of disease prevention and management. By making complex scientific concepts accessible and relevant to a broad audience, this review not only advances academic understanding but also supports real-world strategies to reduce the global burden of PD.

2. Molecular Mechanisms of PD

The molecular mechanisms underlying PD involve a multifaceted and interconnected network characterized by the progressive degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta. The pathogenesis of PD results from the complex interplay of genetic, environmental, and cellular factors that collectively contribute to neurodegeneration. Key factors implicated in the progression of PD include α-synuclein aggregation, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, ferroptosis, and gut dysbiosis, all of which contribute to neuronal damage (Figure 1). A hallmark of PD pathology is the aggregation of misfolded alpha-synuclein (α-syn) proteins, which form intracellular inclusions known as Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites. These aggregates disrupt normal cellular processes, particularly by impairing mitochondrial function and integrity. Mitochondrial dysfunction results in compromised ATP production and enhanced oxidative stress, initiating a deleterious feedback loop that exacerbates neuronal injuries. This dysfunction is further compounded by impaired protein clearance mechanisms, including the ubiquitin-proteasome system and autophagy-lysosomal pathways, which lead to the accumulation of damaged proteins and organelles. Neuroinflammation is another critical feature in the progression of PD. Misfolded α-synuclein triggers the activation of microglia and astrocytes, resulting in the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. While these responses may be protective in the early stages, sustained inflammation can contribute to neuronal injury and accelerate disease progression. Recent research has identified additional mechanisms, such as gut dysbiosis and ferroptosis, that play significant roles in PD pathophysiology. Alterations in gut microbiota composition have been shown to influence systemic inflammation and neuronal health, implicating a potential gut–brain axis in PD. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation, has been increasingly recognized as a mechanism contributing to neuronal loss in PD. Genetic studies have further elucidated the molecular underpinnings of PD, identifying mutations in genes such as LRRK2, PINK1, and Parkin, which are associated with familial forms of the disease. These findings provide valuable insights into mitochondrial dynamics and cellular quality-control mechanisms. 2.1. α-Synuclein Aggregation

α-Synuclein aggregation is considered one of the earliest and most central events in PD pathogenesis. Under pathological conditions, α-synuclein, a presynaptic neuronal protein, misfolds and aggregates into insoluble fibrils, forming Lewy bodies, which are a hallmark of PD [16]. These aggregates disrupt neuronal function and are believed to propagate from cell to cell, contributing to the spread of the pathology. Recent studies have shown that α-synuclein aggregates can be transmitted to neighboring cells, seeding further aggregation in healthy neurons. Cell-to-cell transmission is a critical factor in the spread of PD pathology [17]. 2.2. Mitochondrial Dysfunction

Mitochondrial dysfunction plays a pivotal role in PD pathogenesis. Impairment of mitochondrial function, particularly complex I of the electron transport chain, leads to reduced ATP production and increased generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [19]. This dysfunction is exacerbated by the interaction between α-synuclein aggregates and mitochondria. Recent research has highlighted a generalized reduction in mitochondrial quality-control proteins in dopaminergic neurons from patients with PD. This includes a decrease in proteins such as PINK1, Parkin, and mitochondrial chaperones, which are crucial for maintaining mitochondrial proteostasis and function [19]. 2.3. Oxidative Stress

Oxidative stress occurs when there is an imbalance between the production of ROS and the body’s ability to detoxify these reactive intermediates or repair the resulting damage. In PD, elevated ROS levels damage lipids, proteins, and DNA, exacerbating neuronal injury. 2.4. Neuroinflammation

Neuroinflammation in PD involves the activation of microglia and other immune cells in response to neuronal damage and α-synuclein aggregation. This chronic inflammation further damages neurons and promotes disease progression. Recent studies have shown that microglial activation is not only a response to neuronal damage but also contributes to the progression of PD [20]. Activated microglia can release exosomes containing α-synuclein, which may propagate neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration by affecting distant dopaminergic neurons. 2.5. Ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death that is driven by lipid peroxidation. In PD, iron accumulation in the substantia nigra enhances oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, leading to ferroptotic death of the dopaminergic neurons. Recent research has linked α-synuclein aggregation to ferroptosis [21]. Misfolded α-synuclein disrupts cellular iron homeostasis and promotes lipid peroxidation, thereby facilitating ferroptosis. 2.6. Gut Dysbiosis

Emerging evidence suggests that gut dysbiosis, an imbalance in gut microbiota composition, may precede central nervous system involvement in PD. The gut–brain axis allows the potential propagation of misfolded α-synuclein from the enteric nervous system to the brain via the vagus nerve. Studies have shown that individuals with PD exhibit significant alterations in gut microbiota composition compared to healthy controls. There is a notable depletion of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria and enrichment of pro-inflammatory bacteria [22]. Gut dysbiosis contributes to PD pathology by

Facilitating the propagation of α-synuclein aggregates from the gut to the brain.

Activating systemic immune responses and promoting neuroinflammation.

Altering the production of neurotransmitters and metabolites that influence brain function.

3. Risk Factors in PD

Risk factors are conditions that increase the likelihood of disease development. These risk factors can be genetic, environmental, or lifestyle-related and may be modifiable (e.g., smoking, diet) or non-modifiable (e.g., age, family history). The risk factors for PD include age (more common in those over 60 years), genetics (family history), environmental toxins (e.g., pesticides), and head injuries (Figure 2). Disease progression is slower in those diagnosed at a younger age, while severe motor symptoms, cognitive decline, and poor response to treatment suggest faster progression. Non-motor symptoms like depression and sleep issues also affect the disease course.

3.1. Demographic and Genetic Factors

Demographic and genetic factors are key risk factors for PD, as they significantly impact the disease’s onset, progression, and response to treatment. An understanding of these factors is essential for enhancing diagnosis, improving treatment outcomes, and predicting disease trajectories, thereby enabling the customization of interventions to meet individual patient needs (Table 2).

3.1.1. Age

Age is the most significant risk factor for PD, with a median age of onset of 60 years [1,7]. Several mechanisms explain this association. Aging is linked to neuronal loss, with approximately 30% of elderly non-PD individuals experiencing degeneration of the substantia nigra (SN) [23]. This neuronal loss heightens the sensitivity of SN neurons to mitochondrial dysfunction and protein degradation impairment [7]. PD symptoms manifest when 50–60% of the SN neurons are lost. Aging also contributes to increased oxidative stress, which damages DNA, proteins, and lipids, accelerating the death of dopaminergic neurons. Iron accumulation with age generates free radicals via the Fenton reaction, exacerbating oxidative stress and neurodegeneration [24]. Furthermore, aging reduces the efficiency of autophagy and the ubiquitin-proteasome system, processes critical for protein quality control, thereby facilitating the accumulation of misfolded proteins like α-synuclein, a hallmark of PD [25]. Mitochondrial dysfunction due to aging further diminishes energy production and increases oxidative damage. While PD predominantly affects older individuals, about 5% of cases occur in those under 50 years of age, often due to genetic mutations [7,26].

3.1.2. Sex

PD is approximately twice as prevalent in men as in women [26]. The protective effects of female sex hormones, such as estrogen, may explain this disparity. Estrogen has neuroprotective roles, including upregulating neurotrophic factors, enhancing dopamine synthesis, reducing inflammation, and preventing α-synuclein aggregation and Lewy body formation [27]. Reduced lifetime estrogen exposure, as seen in early menopause or oophorectomy, correlates with a higher PD risk [27,28]. Sex-specific genetic mechanisms also influence the risk of developing PD. Variants like the LRRK2 G2019S mutation exhibit a higher prevalence in women and sex-dependent phenotypic differences [27]. Urate levels, an endogenous antioxidant, are associated with a reduced PD risk and slower progression, particularly in men [28]. Differences in the immune response and neuroinflammation may further contribute to the higher incidence of PD in men [27].

3.1.3. Racial and Ethnic Groups

The prevalence of PD varies across racial and ethnic groups, with Caucasians and Hispanics having a higher risk than Black and Asian populations [26,29]. Black individuals show approximately half the PD prevalence of White individuals [30]. This disparity is attributed to genetic, biological, and socioeconomic factors. Genetic predispositions vary among ethnicities, with certain mutations being specific to certain populations. For instance, the LRRK2 G2019S variant is prevalent in Europe, North Africa, and the United States but is rare in Japan, where the LRRK2 p.R1628P and p.G2385R variants are more common [31]. Ancestry-related gene expression differences also influence the risk of PD. Ancestry-associated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) account for up to 27% of PD heritability and are often related to immune responses and vascular functions [32]. Environmental and socioeconomic factors intersect with genetic predisposition and influence PD progression. Stress, healthcare access, and comorbidities play critical roles in exacerbating these disparities [33]. Racial differences in immune response and neuroinflammation, evident in ancestry-associated gene expression patterns, may further explain the variability in PD risk and progression [32].

3.1.4. Family History of PD

A family history of PD is a significant risk factor, with 15–25% of individuals with PD reporting familial connections and 10–20% having a first-degree relative with the condition [34,35]. Individuals with a family history are up to three times more likely to develop PD, often presenting with an earlier onset of symptoms [4,35]. Interestingly, a familial history of melanoma is also associated with an increased risk of PD [36]. Familial PD accounts for approximately 15% of cases, with the remaining 85% being sporadic [37].

3.1.5. Genetic Contributions

Approximately 5% of PD cases result from mutations in specific genes, with over 200 PD-related genes identified to date [38]. The key genetic contributors include:

SNCA (Alpha-Synuclein): The first gene found to have a mutation with an impact on PD was the SNCA gene, which provides instructions for making alpha-synuclein (α-Syn), a protein involved in communication between neurons. In the brains of patients with PD, this protein accumulates in clumps known as Lewy bodies, which can lead to the death of many cell types (e.g., dopaminergic neurons, cortical neurons, and the enteric nervous system) and the development of PD [39]. Mutations in the SNCA gene are observed in patients with early-onset PD and rapid disease progression (mean age of onset, 46 years) [5]. It is common to observe cognitive decline in individuals with the SNCA gene mutation [39].

LRRK2 (Leucine-Rich Repeat Kinase 2): Mutations in the LRRK2 gene represent the most common genetic cause of familial PD and also contribute to an increased risk of sporadic PD [40]. The LRRK2 gene encodes dardarin, a multifunctional protein involved in key cellular processes, including autophagy regulation, oxidative stress response, inflammation, and cytoskeletal dynamics [41]. Dysregulation of LRRK2 activity impairs synaptic function and alters dopamine metabolism, resulting in dopamine-induced neurotoxicity in dopaminergic neurons [42]. LRRK2 mutations are the principal cause of autosomal dominant PD, a hereditary form of the disease in which a single copy of the mutated gene is sufficient to trigger pathogenesis [43]. Clinically, autosomal dominant PD mirrors sporadic PD in terms of motor symptoms—such as bradykinesia, tremor, rigidity, and postural instability—but tends to present at an earlier age and follows a more rapid progression. To date, over 50 distinct pathogenic mutations have been identified in the LRRK2 gene [44].

GBA1 (Glucosylceramidase Beta): Mutations in the GBA1 gene, which encodes the lysosomal enzyme glucocerebrosidase (GCase), are associated with both familial and sporadic forms of PD. GCase functions as a lysosomal hydrolase that catalyzes the breakdown of glucosylceramide into glucose and ceramide, which is a critical step in lipid metabolism and lysosomal homeostasis [44]. Impaired GCase activity due to GBA1 mutations leads to lysosomal dysfunction and contributes to the accumulation of alpha-synuclein, a hallmark of PD pathology. GBA1 is recognized as a major genetic risk factor for PD, with mutations estimated to affect approximately 7–10% of patients with PD [44,45,46].

PARK7 (DJ-1): The PARK7 gene encodes DJ-1, a multifunctional protein involved in protecting neuronal cells against oxidative stress. DJ-1 exerts several neuroprotective roles, functioning as a transcriptional regulator, antioxidant, and molecular chaperone [47]. It is expressed in various cell types in the central nervous system, including microglia, astrocytes, and neurons [48]. DJ-1 plays a key role in mitigating oxidative stress and modulating neuroinflammatory pathways; thus, dysfunction of PARK7/DJ-1 may impair microglial immune responses and contribute to the onset and progression of PD [48]. Mutations in PARK7 are linked to autosomal recessive early-onset PD, accounting for less than 1% of cases, typically with an onset before the age of 50 years [47]. In addition to its role in familial PD, reduced expression or oxidative modification of DJ-1 has also been observed in idiopathic PD, suggesting a broader involvement in disease pathology [49]. DJ-1 protein levels can be measured in the CSF, particularly during the early stages of the disease, highlighting its potential utility as a biomarker for early PD detection and as a therapeutic target aimed at reducing neuroinflammation and promoting neuronal survival [5,49].

PINK1 (PTEN-induced kinase 1): Mutations in PINK1 impair mitochondrial function and autophagy, leading to hereditary, early-onset PD [50,51,52,53,54]. Emerging evidence suggests that PINK1 deficiency may impair adult neurogenesis of dopaminergic neurons, thereby promoting neurodegeneration associated with PD [53]. Therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring mitochondrial function—such as enhancing nucleotide metabolism—have been proposed as potential approaches to mitigate neurodegenerative processes in PD [54].

PRKN (Parkin): The PRKN gene encodes the Parkin protein, which plays a critical role in maintaining cellular homeostasis by regulating lysosomal activity and facilitating the degradation of damaged or excess proteins. Loss-of-function mutations in PRKN disrupt the ubiquitin-proteasome system, leading to the accumulation of toxic protein aggregates in neurons and subsequent dopaminergic cell loss in the substantia nigra [55]. PRKN mutations have been strongly implicated not only in the early-onset of PD—accounting for approximately 10–20% of cases with onset between ages 40 and 50, and up to 42% of cases with onset at or before age 20—but also in the broader pathogenesis and progression of the disease [56,57,58,59].

UCHL1 (Ubiquitin C-Terminal Hydrolase L1): The UCHL1 gene encodes the deubiquitinating enzyme UCHL1, which is predominantly expressed in neurons and plays a key role in the degradation of misfolded or accumulated proteins via the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) [60]. A rare variant of UCHL1 has been associated with UPS dysfunction, leading to the accumulation and aggregation of neuronal proteins, particularly alpha-synuclein, a hallmark of PD pathology [61]. Additionally, reduced levels of UCHL1 have been reported in individuals with PD compared to healthy controls [62], and these levels show a significant correlation with motor symptom severity, as assessed by the MDS-UPDRS III [63]. Given its detectability in CSF and blood, UCHL1 is considered a promising candidate biomarker for the diagnosis and monitoring of PD progression.

Double Mutation Carriers (“Double Hit”) in PD: Some patients with PD may carry mutations in more than one PD-associated gene, representing a “double hit” genetic risk factor. Notably, individuals with both the LRRK2 G2019S mutation and either the GBA1 E326K or L444P variant have been identified [46,64]. In such cases, the onset of motor symptoms tends to occur at an earlier age than those harboring only a single mutation in LRRK2 or GBA1 [65]. Regarding motor features, studies suggest that the presence of LRRK2, GBA1, or combined LRRK2-GBA1 mutations does not significantly influence the initial motor presentation, such as tremors [65,66]. However, disease progression, as measured by the Hoehn and Yahr (HY) scale, is reported to be more rapid in patients with GBA1-associated PD and LRRK2-GBA1 dual mutation carriers than in those with mutation-negative PD (MNPD) [65]. Beyond motor symptoms, these genetic variants may also differentially influence non-motor symptoms. For example, patients with GBA1-associated PD have shown a higher prevalence of non-motor features, such as dementia, probable REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD), and psychosis, in contrast to those with LRRK2-PD or MNPD [65]. Both RBD and psychosis are recognized as risk factors for the development of dementia in patients with PD [67,68]. Interestingly, despite carrying risk variants in both LRRK2 and GBA1, dual mutation carriers (LRRK2-GBA1-PD) tend to exhibit a lower frequency of RBD and psychosis than patients with single GBA1 mutations, suggesting a complex and possibly modulating interaction between these genetic factors [65].

3.1.6. Gene–Environment Interactions in PD

Gene–environment interactions in PD represent a crucial paradigm for understanding disease etiology, where genetic susceptibility factors converge with environmental exposures to influence disease risk and progression [69]. These interactions manifest through multiple biological mechanisms, including oxidative stress, protein aggregation and neuroinflammation [9,70]. Recent epidemiological studies have demonstrated that environmental factors, such as pesticide exposure, can significantly modify disease risk in individuals with specific genetic variants, particularly in genes like SNCA and LRRK2 [71,72]. This interaction occurs through various cellular pathways, including mitochondrial function, protein degradation, and dopamine metabolism. Understanding these interactions has profound implications for both disease prevention and treatment strategies.

3.2. Education and Intelligence

Surprisingly, a high level of education (9 or more years) and a higher cognitive capacity in early adulthood, measured by intelligence (IQ), are associated with a higher risk of developing PD, particularly among men [73,74,75]. While the reasons for this link remain unclear, several theories have been proposed. Firstly, lifestyle differences may play a role. Research indicates that individuals with higher IQs or more education often have lower cholesterol levels [76], which are associated with a higher risk of PD [77,78]. This relationship between IQ, education, and cholesterol levels may also be influenced by socioeconomic status and lifestyle habits [76]. Additionally, individuals with higher IQs or education levels are less likely to smoke [79,80], and as previously noted, smoking is considered to be protective against PD. Occupational differences may also contribute; people with higher education levels often engage in jobs that require less physical activity, potentially increasing the risk of PD [73]. Conversely, adults with lower education levels are more likely to smoke, possibly due to stress or targeted tobacco advertising [75]. However, higher education is associated with a greater cognitive reserve, which may influence cognitive performance in PD and potentially delay its progression [81]. 3.3. Environmental and Occupational Factors

The risk and progression of PD are influenced by various environmental, occupational, socioeconomic, and geographic factors. These factors interact through complex mechanisms, including oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, genetic predisposition, and disrupted cellular processes (Table 4).

3.3.1. Exposure to Herbicides and Pesticides

Exposure to herbicides and pesticides, such as paraquat, is strongly associated with an increased risk of PD [82,83]. Agricultural workers and individuals living in rural areas face heightened exposure through direct contact or environmental contamination. These chemicals induce oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, which are critical processes in dopaminergic neuron degeneration, and have been linked to genetic alterations that elevate susceptibility to PD [84].

3.3.2. Heavy Metal Exposure

Heavy metals, including mercury, manganese, and lead, disrupt redox homeostasis, leading to excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and impaired antioxidant defense systems [85]. This oxidative stress contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction and the aggregation of alpha-synuclein, forming Lewy bodies—a hallmark of PD [86]. Metals also trigger neuroinflammation by activating inflammasomes and pro-inflammatory cytokines, exacerbating neuronal loss [87,88]. Additionally, heavy metals cross the blood-brain barrier, accumulate in the brain, and affect gut–brain communication via microbiome disruption, further amplifying the risk of PD [88,89].

3.3.3. Industrial Chemicals

Occupational exposure to industrial solvents, such as trichloroethylene, is associated with PD development [85,90]. These solvents can impair autophagy, lysosomal function, and mitochondrial integrity, leading to neuronal dysfunction [90]. Genetic factors combined with solvent exposure may synergistically increase susceptibility to PD.

3.3.4. Urban and Rural Living

Urban living, characterized by higher air pollution levels, poses a significant risk of PD [91]. Pollutants contribute to neurotoxicity, systemic inflammation, and genetic interactions that predispose individuals to PD [9,92,93]. Particulate matter in polluted air has been shown to directly damage neurons and exacerbate oxidative stress, highlighting the importance of mitigating pollution exposure in urban areas. In contrast, people residing in rural areas, those working on farms, and those exposed to chemical (e.g., pesticides and herbicides) are more prone to head injuries [94], infections [95], soil-borne pathogens [95], and consumption of unwell water, which may be causally connected to PD [96].

3.3.5. Occupational and Workplace Factors

Certain occupations, particularly in agriculture [97], mining, and industries involving heavy metals [98] or solvents (such as trichloroethylene) [90], elevate the risk of PD [97]. Agricultural workers are exposed to pesticides and herbicides, while miners encounter high levels of metals like manganese and lead. Professions requiring high cognitive engagement, such as healthcare [99] and teaching [100], may also be linked to PD, potentially due to a combination of environmental exposures and genetic predispositions that affect key cellular processes [101]. Individuals who engage in contact sports, such as boxing, American football, and soccer, are at a heightened risk of experiencing head trauma, particularly repeated head trauma, which increases their susceptibility to developing neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer’s disease and PD [102].

3.3.6. Socioeconomic Status

Lower socioeconomic status (SES), often defined by income and education levels, is correlated with a higher prevalence of PD. Limited access to healthcare among lower SES groups can delay diagnosis and treatment, thereby accelerating disease progression [103,104]. Conversely, higher SES is associated with lifestyle factors such as physical activity and dietary antioxidant intake, which reduce PD risk. Environmental exposures, such as pollutants or occupational hazards, are often more prevalent in lower SES populations, further contributing to the onset and severity of PD.

3.4. Lifestyle Factors

Lifestyle factors significantly influence the development of PD [105]. Factors such as physical activity, diet, smoking, and alcohol consumption have been shown to affect the risk of developing PD (Table 5). A thorough understanding of these lifestyle factors is essential for the prevention, early detection, and management of PD.

3.4.1. Body Mass Index (BMI)

The relationship between BMI and PD is contentious. Some studies have reported no significant link between obesity or prolonged sedentary behavior and PD risk [106]. Conversely, other research suggests that abdominal obesity and increased waist circumference may elevate PD risk [107,108]. These effects may be mediated by elevated inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress, and obesity-related metabolic disturbances like insulin resistance and dyslipidemia, which contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal damage in PD [107]. Additionally, obesity-induced changes in the gut microbiome may exacerbate inflammation and oxidative stress, further influencing PD progression. Unintentional weight loss is a common feature of PD, affecting approximately 60% of patients, with an average loss of 3–6 kg during disease progression [12]. This weight loss correlates with poor prognosis and a diminished quality of life [12,109].

3.4.2. Alcohol Consumption

The relationship between alcohol consumption and PD risk remains inconsistent across studies. While some research has not identified any significant link between alcohol and PD [110], other studies suggest a protective effect of moderate alcohol consumption against motor function decline [111,112]. Acute alcohol intake may temporarily alleviate motor symptoms such as tremors and bradykinesia by increasing dopamine release in specific brain regions. However, heavy and prolonged alcohol use can lead to persistent reductions in dopamine levels, exacerbating PD symptoms [113]. Neuroimaging studies have revealed that alcohol intake is negatively associated with brain structure and connectivity changes, potentially accelerating PD progression [114]. Additionally, alcohol can interfere with the efficacy of medications like levodopa, a precursor of dopamine, by impairing its absorption and effectiveness. This can intensify tremors and other motor symptoms. Furthermore, alcohol consumption disrupts sleep patterns, compounding the existing sleep disturbances commonly experienced by individuals with PD.

3.4.3. Dietary Habits and Nutrition

The consumption of dairy products has been associated with an increased risk of developing PD [115], particularly among men [116,117]. Potential contributing factors include milk proteins, such as casein and lactalbumin, which may lower serum urate levels. Since urate has protective effects against PD, this reduction could increase vulnerability [116]. Additionally, contaminants like pesticides in dairy products may play a role [118]. While these associations do not establish causality, they suggest a potential risk link. Diets rich in pro-inflammatory foods, such as red meat, refined sugars, and trans fats, may promote systemic inflammation, further increasing the risk of PD. Similarly, carbonated beverages and cold cuts are also linked to a higher risk of PD [119]. Nutrient deficiencies, including omega-3 fatty acids, vitamin D, B vitamins, and coenzyme Q10, can impair neuronal health and mitochondrial function, potentially accelerating neurodegeneration and worsening PD symptoms [120]. Moreover, high-protein diets may interfere with the absorption of levodopa, the primary medication for managing PD symptoms.

3.4.4. Sedentary Lifestyle

Physical inactivity and a sedentary lifestyle are associated with the development and progression of PD. Sedentary behavior worsens non-motor symptoms, such as cognitive impairment, depression, and poor sleep quality, significantly reducing the quality of life of individuals with PD [105,121]. Physical inactivity is also correlated with increased depression and cognitive decline in patients with PD, suggesting that reducing sedentary time could help alleviate these symptoms. Furthermore, dysregulated genes identified in patients with PD overlap with those affected by sedentary behavior, particularly in cellular pathways linked to PD progression. Network-based research underscores the impact of lifestyle factors on significant genetic and molecular pathways, highlighting potential therapeutic targets for slowing PD progression.

3.5.1. Diabetes

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) significantly impacts the development and progression of PD [122,123,124]. T2DM exacerbates motor symptoms (e.g., instability and mobility issues), non-motor symptoms, cognitive impairments (e.g., slower thinking and attention deficits), and accelerates disease progression [125,126,127,128]. The neurovascular burden associated with T2DM, such as white matter hyperintensities, contributes to these effects [129,130]. T2DM is thus considered an independent factor negatively influencing PD outcomes [130,131].

3.5.2. Hypertension

Hypertension may increase the risk of PD due to hypertensive vasculopathy in regions such as the basal ganglia, brain stem, and thalamus [132]. It also contributes to cardiovascular diseases and cerebral small vessel diseases, leading to white matter hyperintensities that may exacerbate PD progression [133,134,135]. Early diagnosis and management of hypertension can mitigate the risk and progression of PD.

3.5.3. Cholesterol and Triglycerides

Patients with PD often exhibit lower levels of cholesterol and triglycerides than healthy controls [136,137]. Studies have reported consistently reduced total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglyceride levels in patients with PD up to 20 years before diagnosis [138]. This reduction is associated with increased oxidative stress and alpha-synuclein aggregation in dopaminergic neurons [139]. High plasma LDL-C levels and total cholesterol levels have been linked to slower PD progression and improved motor function [140,141], while low HDL-C levels are associated with a higher PD risk [142]. However, findings on lipid levels remain inconsistent, likely due to confounding factors such as obesity, diabetes, lifestyle, and genetics [141,143,144,145].

3.6. Inflammation, Immunity, and the Gut–Brain Axis

Inflammatory factors, immune dysfunction, and gut–brain axis dysbiosis contribute to neurodegeneration and progression in PD [95]. Chronic inflammation and microglial activation release harmful cytokines and oxidative molecules, while dysbiosis disrupts gut function, triggering brain inflammation and neuronal damage. These factors may influence PD onset, as early symptoms often precede the central nervous system changes. A summary of their impact on PD is presented in Table 7. Understanding these mechanisms is key to developing therapies that can slow disease progression.

3.6.1. Viral Infections

Viral infections play a significant role in the development and progression of PD by contributing to chronic neuroinflammation. Neuroinflammation is triggered when viruses activate the immune response, particularly through the activation of microglia, which release pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. These molecules can cross the blood-brain barrier and cause neuronal cell death [146,147]. Furthermore, certain viruses, such as influenza and Epstein-Barr viruses, have been found to promote the aggregation of alpha-synuclein, a protein critical to the pathology of PD [148]. In addition, the activation of Toll-like receptors (TLRs), particularly TLR4, by viral proteins leads to heightened immune responses and further neuroinflammation, ultimately causing neuronal damage [149]. Viral RNA can also trigger microglial activation through receptors like Mac-1, resulting in the production of reactive oxygen species that contribute to neuronal injury [150]. Epidemiological studies have identified specific viruses, including cytomegalovirus (CMV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), and hepatitis C virus, as factors that may induce chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation, potentially increasing the risk of PD development [146].

3.6.2. Neuroinflammation and Chronic Inflammatory Diseases

Inflammation is a normal immune response designed to protect the body; however, when it becomes chronic, it can lead to tissue damage and contribute to the development of diseases like PD. Chronic inflammation in PD is driven by cytokines, reactive oxygen species, and chemokines produced by various immune cells, including endothelial cells, microglia, astrocytes, and peripheral immune cells. Inflammation can manifest in two forms: acute, which has a rapid onset and short duration with localized symptoms, and chronic, which persists over time and leads to tissue damage and an increased risk of disease. In PD, markers of inflammation, such as CRP, TNF-α, and IL-6, have been linked to the disease and may serve as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets [151,152]. The gut–brain axis, wherein inflammation in the gastrointestinal tract, influenced by the gut microbiota, can exacerbate neuroinflammation, further promoting PD progression, is also a key factor [153]. Additionally, genetic factors like LRRK2 mutations and environmental exposures such as toxins or infections play a role in driving neuroinflammation in PD [154].

3.6.3. Gut Dysbiosis

Gut dysbiosis, an imbalance in the gut microbiota, is increasingly recognized as a critical factor in PD pathogenesis. The gut–brain axis, a bidirectional communication system between the gut and the central nervous system, is essential for maintaining neurological health. Dysbiosis can disrupt this axis, leading to neuroinflammation and misfolding of alpha-synuclein, a protein closely associated with PD pathology [154,155]. Altered gut microbiota can produce pathogenic metabolites that create a pro-inflammatory environment, exacerbating neuroinflammation and promoting PD progression [155,156]. Several biological pathways connect gut dysbiosis to the progression of PD. Dysbiosis may lead to the overproduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and interleukins, which can cross the blood-brain barrier and induce neuroinflammation [157]. Additionally, the gut microbiota influences immune responses through toll-like receptors, and dysregulation of these receptors can contribute to alpha-synuclein pathology [157]. Moreover, the gut–brain axis, which relies on neuroendocrine signals and direct neural pathways, can be disrupted by dysbiosis, thereby exacerbating PD progression [158,159].

3.6.4. Chronic Constipation

Chronic constipation is a prevalent non-motor symptom that often precedes motor manifestations of PD. This condition is linked to gut dysbiosis and increased intestinal permeability, which may result in systemic inflammation and activation of the enteric nervous system. These changes can trigger alpha-synuclein misfolding in the gut, potentially propagating to the brain via the vagus nerve and contributing to PD development [156]. The early occurrence of gastrointestinal symptoms, including constipation, supports the hypothesis that PD pathology may originate in the gut years before motor symptoms appear [158].

3.7. Neurological and Psychological Factors

Neurological and psychological factors can increase the risk and progression of PD by affecting neural circuits, neuroinflammation, and promoting oxidative stress, which damages neurons. Impaired dopamine regulation, which is common in both neurological and psychological disorders, may also contribute to the early-onset and worsening of PD symptoms. Table 8 provides a summary of the mechanisms underlying the neurological and psychological factors of PD.

3.7.1. Traumatic Brain Injury

Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) is a significant risk factor for PD, with biological mechanisms including inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and protein accumulation. TBI promotes the upregulation of proteins such as amyloid precursor protein (APP), alpha-synuclein, hyper-phosphorylated Tau, and TAR DNA-binding protein 43, all of which are linked to PD pathology [165]. Acute neuroinflammation and catecholamine dysfunction triggered by TBI further contribute to PD progression [166]. The inflammatory response involves microglia and astrocyte activation, which can become detrimental if prolonged, accelerating neurodegeneration [165]. Notably, U.S. veterans with TBI have a 56% increased risk of PD, with the risk escalating with injury severity [165].

3.7.2. Sleep Disorders

REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) is an important early indicator of PD. Studies have demonstrated that individuals with RBD are at an increased risk of developing PD or other synucleinopathies such as dementia with Lewy bodies [167]. The relationship between RBD and PD is believed to stem from the accumulation of misfolded alpha-synuclein proteins in brain regions responsible for regulating REM sleep, leading to the formation of Lewy bodies, a characteristic feature of PD. RBD is marked by the absence of muscle atonia during REM sleep, causing dream enactment, and often precedes the onset of motor symptoms in PD by several years [168]. Beyond RBD, sleep fragmentation and difficulties with sleep maintenance are frequently observed in patients with PD, contributing to the worsening of cognitive decline and other non-motor symptoms [167]. As both a risk factor and an early sign of PD progression, RBD reflects the underlying neurodegenerative changes associated with this disease.

3.7.3. Loss of Smell (Anosmia)

Anosmia, or loss of smell, is an early non-motor symptom of PD that often precedes motor symptoms by years or even decades. It is linked to the accumulation of alpha-synuclein in the olfactory bulb and related regions, which are among the first areas affected by PD [169]. This pathology begins in the olfactory bulb and related regions, such as the anterior olfactory nucleus, which are among the first areas affected by PD. Reduced integrity of the substantia nigra, a characteristic feature of PD, has also been observed in individuals with unexplained smell loss, further supporting the association between anosmia and PD. This suggests that anosmia not only signals PD risk but may also serve as an early marker of the disease [170]. The disruption of olfactory function by alpha-synuclein pathology may contribute to the progression of neurodegeneration in other brain regions.

3.7.4. Chronic Stress

Psychological and emotional health significantly influence both the risk and progression of PD. The progression of PD is also closely linked to various biological mechanisms influenced by psychological stress and mood disorders. Stress can accelerate the damage to dopamine-producing neurons, worsen motor symptoms like bradykinesia, motor blocking, and tremors, and reduce the effectiveness of dopaminergic medications [171]. The mesocortical and mesolimbic dopaminergic pathways, which are crucial for mood regulation, are disrupted in patients with PD, contributing to the high prevalence of depression and anxiety in these patients [172,173]. Chronic stress is a significant risk factor for PD, as it exacerbates the loss of dopamine-producing neurons. Animal models suggest that prolonged stress accelerates dopaminergic cell death, underscoring the potential benefits of stress reduction interventions [174,175].

3.7.5. Depression and Anxiety

Mental health conditions, such as depression and anxiety, can serve as early indicators during the prodromal phase of PD, although their presence does not necessarily predict the development of the disease. Research shows that individuals under 50 years of age with mental health disorders, including schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, insomnia, depression, and anxiety, are at a heightened risk of PD, particularly when other environmental or contributory factors are present [176,177]. Depression and anxiety, prevalent non-motor symptoms of PD, may also precede the onset of motor symptoms and are increasingly recognized as integral to the disease’s pathophysiology. The biological mechanisms linking stress, depression, and anxiety to PD include activation of the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and sympathetic nervous system (SNS), elevated levels of glucocorticoids and catecholamines leading to neuronal injury, increased pro-inflammatory cytokines contributing to neurodegeneration, and stress-induced vulnerability of dopamine neurons via glucocorticoid receptor activity. Chronic stress and mood disorders not only exacerbate both motor and non-motor symptoms but also synergize with other factors to increase neuronal vulnerability, potentially accelerating the progression of PD. Therefore, addressing stress and mood disorders in patients with PD is crucial for slowing disease progression and improving their quality of life [178].

3.8. Hormonal and Other Health Conditions

Hormonal factors influence the onset, progression, and severity of PD. Imbalances in hormones such as thyroid and cortisol can affect neuronal health and exacerbate symptoms. Understanding these factors is essential for developing gender-specific therapies and improving disease management. A summary of the hormonal risk factors for PD is presented in Table 9.

3.8.1. Estrogen and Its Neuroprotective Role

Estrogen plays a significant role in the risk and progression of PD by influencing key processes involved in dopaminergic function, including dopamine synthesis, metabolism, and transport. It is believed to have neuroprotective effects, potentially shielding dopaminergic neurons from degeneration. Animal models support this, showing that estrogen may reduce inflammation by interacting with the brain’s renin-angiotensin system and counteracting oxidative stress, which can damage neurons. Through these mechanisms, estrogen helps protect the brain’s dopaminergic system, potentially slowing PD progression [179].

3.8.2. Postmenopausal Women and PD Risk

Postmenopausal women, particularly those not undergoing hormone replacement therapy (HRT), are at an increased risk of developing PD. This heightened risk is linked to reduced lifetime exposure to endogenous estrogens, either due to early menopause or a shorter fertile lifespan. The absence of estrogen supplementation through HRT after menopause further exacerbates this risk [180,181].

3.8.3. Hormone Replacement Therapy

The association between hormone replacement therapy (HRT) and PD risk is multifaceted. While estrogen alone may offer protective effects, long-term use of combined estrogen-progesterone therapy appears to increase the risk of developing PD [179,182]. Estrogen replacement therapy (ERT) can help alleviate symptoms and slow the progression of PD in women, especially in the early stages, before L-dopa therapy begins [183]. The lower incidence of PD in women than in men may be partly attributed to the protective influence of endogenous estrogen. Estrogen is thought to protect against neurodegeneration by reducing oxidative stress, regulating dopamine metabolism, and improving mitochondrial function, which may help preserve dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, a region that is progressively affected by PD [184].

3.8.4. Hyperuricemia

Uric acid, with its antioxidant properties, helps reduce oxidative stress, which is a key factor in neurodegeneration in PD. The relationship between uric acid levels and PD risk is complex. Elevated uric acid levels are associated with a reduced risk of PD due to their neuroprotective effects. However, hyperuricemia is also linked to cognitive impairment and conditions like gout, which may exacerbate neurodegeneration [185]. Additionally, co-existing conditions, such as cerebrovascular injury, can amplify the effects of hyperuricemia, increasing the risk of cognitive decline and accelerating PD progression [186].

3.9. Medications

Certain antibiotics, calcium channel blockers (for treating high blood pressure and heart conditions), certain anti-diabetic medications (such as rosiglitazone and pioglitazone), and statins (for treating high cholesterol levels) are associated with an increased risk of PD [6,187,188]. A summary of the medications associated with an increased risk of PD is presented in Table 10.

3.9.1. β2-Adrenoceptor Antagonists (e.g., Propranolol)

Propranolol, a β2-adrenoceptor antagonist, is associated with an increased risk of PD. β2-adrenoceptors play a critical role in regulating metabolic functions and dopaminergic activity, both of which are essential for neuroprotection. Chronic use of propranolol may contribute to the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, which is a hallmark of PD. The β2-adrenoceptor blockade can exacerbate motor symptoms and accelerate PD progression by reducing dopaminergic signaling. Additionally, the modulation of adrenergic activity may disrupt the already compromised balance of neurotransmitters, further impairing the dopaminergic systems [189].

3.9.2. Diabetes Medications

Metformin, a widely used diabetes medication, has been linked to an increased risk of PD due to its influence on various physiological processes. It can contribute to insulin dysregulation, leading to neuroinflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction, and may also alter synaptic plasticity, implicating neurodegeneration pathways that are common to both diabetes and PD. In contrast, other diabetes treatments, such as glucagon-like peptide-1 agonists (GLP1a), may offer protective effects against PD progression by modulating insulin signaling pathways [190,191].

3.9.3. Antibiotics

Macrolides and lincosamides, classes of antibiotics, have been associated with an increased risk of PD, likely due to their disruptive effects on the gut microbiota. Alterations in microbial composition may exacerbate neuroinflammation and promote the aggregation of α-synuclein, a protein implicated in PD pathogenesis. In contrast, emerging therapies, such as prasinezumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting aggregated α-synuclein, show potential in slowing motor progression in rapidly advancing PD cases [70,192].

3.9.4. Psychological Medications

Psychotropic medications, including antidepressants and anxiolytics, are associated with an increased risk of PD in older populations. These drugs can impact dopaminergic systems, with antipsychotics blocking dopamine receptors, which may increase vulnerability to PD [193].

3.9.5. Cancer Medications

Although direct evidence is limited, certain chemotherapeutic agents and cancer medications may elevate the risk of PD through mechanisms such as inducing oxidative stress, which generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) and exacerbates neuronal damage, and causes mitochondrial dysfunction, which impairs cellular energy production, a known contributor to PD pathology [194].

3.9.6. Epigenetic Modifications

Certain medications can modify epigenetic markers, thereby influencing the gene expression associated with PD. Prolonged use of these drugs may induce changes in DNA methylation, a modification commonly observed in patients with PD [195]. Epigenetic alterations, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and hydroxy methylation, are integral to the molecular mechanisms underlying PD, facilitating the interaction between genetic and environmental factors that contribute to disease onset and progression [49].

3.10. Comorbid Conditions

Other neurodegenerative or chronic diseases may increase the risk or worsen the progression of the disease.

4. Prevent Factors in PD

Preventive factors in PD focus on strategies aimed at reducing the risk of or delaying the onset of this progressive neurodegenerative disorder (Figure 3). Research indicates that lifestyle choices, nutrition, and occupational factors may play a role in lowering the risk of developing PD. Identifying and understanding these factors can guide the development of strategies that not only delay PD onset but also potentially reduce its overall prevalence. By incorporating these preventive measures, individuals may lower their risk of developing PD or slow its progression.

4.1. Lifestyle Factors

Lifestyle factors play a crucial role in both preventing the onset of PD and slowing its progression. Research indicates that specific lifestyle habits can have protective effects on brain health, potentially reducing the risk of developing PD, delaying its onset, and mitigating its progression and symptoms. A summary of the lifestyle factors that may help prevent PD is provided in Table 11.

4.1.1. Physical Activity

Engaging in the highest level of physical activity and exercise has the advantage of decreasing the incidence of PD by up to 21% [196]. It is possible that the onset of PD could be delayed by a significant amount of physical activity, as it may slow down the disease’s pathological processes [197], particularly in individuals who are more physically active during their middle years [198]. During the prodromal phase of PD, individuals may experience non-motor symptoms such as sleep disorders and constipation, along with subtle motor signs like rigidity, balance impairment, and tremor, leading to a likely decrease in physical activity during this period [197]. Patients in the early stages of PD who have consistently engaged in physical activity and exercise over a long period may notice a slower decline in gait stability, activities of daily living, and processing speed [199]. Additionally, participating in physical activity and exercise can improve non-motor symptoms like depression, apathy, and postural instability, while also reducing motor symptoms by reducing inflammation in individuals with PD [200]. Consequently, maintaining a high level of physical activity and exercise could stimulate interest in potential therapeutic targets for PD, ultimately contributing to an improved quality of life for individuals with this condition. Vigorous physical activity and reduced sitting time have neuroprotective effects, including improved gait stability, daily living activities, and processing speed [68]. Exercise has been shown to alleviate non-motor symptoms, such as depression, apathy, and postural instability, while reducing motor symptoms through anti-inflammatory mechanisms [69]. Engaging in regular physical activity may also delay the onset of PD and contribute to a better quality of life.

4.1.2. High-Quality Sleep

High-quality sleep plays a crucial role in reducing the risk of PD through several interconnected biological mechanisms. One key factor is the promotion of efficient protein clearance, particularly through the glymphatic system, which becomes more active during sleep and clears toxic proteins like α-synuclein that contribute to PD. Sleep also regulates neuroinflammation by reducing the activation of immune cells, such as microglia and astrocytes, which can exacerbate neural injuries [201]. Additionally, good sleep helps mitigate oxidative stress by reducing the production of reactive oxygen species and supporting the body’s antioxidant systems, both of which are critical for protecting brain cells from damage. Furthermore, maintaining regular circadian rhythms through high-quality sleep is vital for dopamine regulation, which is essential for the prevention of PD [202]. Disruption of circadian rhythms can reduce dopamine levels and increase the risk of PD. Sleep also improves sleep architecture, reduces fragmentation, and enhances restorative sleep, which supports overall brain health [202]. These combined mechanisms highlight the neuroprotective role of sleep, suggesting that interventions to improve sleep quality may help delay or reduce the onset of PD [203].

4.1.3. Social Support

Social support plays a crucial role in reducing the risk of PD and impacts both psychological and physical health. Studies have shown that strong social networks are linked to better mental health, reducing depression, anxiety, and stress, which are key risk factors for PD [204]. These connections may lower the likelihood of developing the disease. Social support also helps to slow PD progression through several mechanisms. It encourages physical activity, which has been shown to improve motor and cognitive functions in patients with PD by enhancing cerebral mechanisms and cardiovascular fitness [205,206]. Additionally, the psychological benefits of social support, such as increased positivity and reduced distress, can improve disease management and treatment adherence [204]. Furthermore, access to information through social networks provides patients with strategies, like dietary changes, that may help slow disease progression, such as modifications that influence the gut microbiome [207].

4.1.4. Cognitive Engagement

Cognitive engagement, such as participation in mentally stimulating activities, may help reduce the risk of developing PD and its associated cognitive decline. One mechanism through which cognitive engagement may protect against PD is by enhancing neuroplasticity, the brain’s ability to reorganize and form new neural connections. This process, stimulated by engaging in complex tasks, may build a cognitive reserve, potentially delaying the onset of cognitive symptoms in PD [208]. Additionally, cognitive engagement may support neurotransmitter systems crucial for PD, such as dopamine, serotonin, noradrenaline, and acetylcholine, which are implicated in cognitive processes and are affected by PD. Engaging in mental activities may help maintain these systems, potentially slowing the cognitive decline [209]. Moreover, cognitive engagement may influence mitochondrial function, reducing oxidative stress and improving energy metabolism in neurons, which could slow cognitive deterioration [210]. It may also interact with genetic factors, such as variations in the APOE and GBA genes, which are linked to cognitive decline in PD and may potentially mitigate their effects [211]. Cognitive engagement can also help slow PD progression by building cognitive reserves, allowing individuals to compensate for neural damage and delay cognitive symptoms [212]. Furthermore, cognitive activities are often associated with increased physical activity, which has been shown to enhance brain function and structure, leading to improved cognitive outcomes in patients with PD [213]. Cognitive engagement may also help reduce anxiety, indirectly supporting cognitive health and slowing disease progression [213].

4.1.5. Smoking

It is undeniable that smoking tobacco cigarettes is detrimental to our health. Several studies have shown that cigarette smoking may reduce the risk of developing PD [214], with smokers being only half as likely as non-smokers to develop PD [215]. A significant inverse dose-response correlation has been discovered in relation to PD risk: the greater the duration and frequency of smoking among individuals, the lower their chances of developing PD [9]. Consequently, smoking has been linked to a later age at onset of PD [4]. The potential protective effect of smoking on PD may be due to the presence of nicotine in tobacco smoke, which may trigger the release of dopamine, a neurotransmitter associated with PD [214]. Moreover, nicotine has the ability to block the formation of toxins by directly impacting enzyme functions, such as MAO-B [216]. Cigarette smoking has also been observed to diminish the aggregation of alpha-synuclein in cell cultures, suggesting a potential slowdown in the development of PD [217]. Although cigarette smoking may have some protective effects in patients with PD, it is directly linked to increased issues with drooling, swallowing, and freezing in patients with PD [10]. Patients with PD who smoker are more likely to experience non-motor symptoms, such as unexplained pain, feelings of sadness, and memory problems [10]. Moreover, while there may not be a strong connection between smoking and the mortality of PD, it is clear that individuals with PD who smoke are at a higher risk of developing smoking-related cancers like lung cancer, cardiovascular disease, and respiratory issues [218].

4.2. Dietary and Nutritional Factors

Dietary and nutritional factors are crucial in the context of PD, as they can influence both the risk of developing the disease and the management of its symptoms. Proper nutrition supports brain health and neuroprotection, reduces inflammation, promotes gut health, and maintains overall well-being, all of which are vital for managing neurodegenerative conditions like PD. Additionally, interactions between medications and dietary factors are an important consideration in treatment. A summary of dietary and nutritional factors that may prevent PD is provided in Table 12.

4.2.1. Coffee

Drinking coffee may help prevent the onset of PD by ~30% [220,221,222]. Additionally, consuming coffee is linked to a decreased likelihood of developing PD and slowing its advancement [223]. A recent study reported a strong correlation between the duration of coffee consumption and age at onset of PD, as well as a relatively low association between the number of cups of coffee and age at onset of PD [220]. The potential explanation for the protective effect of coffee against PD may be attributed to caffeine. This is supported by the fact that black tea, which contains a lower amount of caffeine, demonstrated a more moderate association with the onset age of PD [220]. There is a paradox regarding the effects of caffeine on motor and non-motor symptoms among patients with PD. On the one hand, research on drug-naive, early-stage PD patients has shown that the severity of non-motor symptoms related to mood and cognition has an inverse relationship with coffee consumption [224]. Additionally, some research has indicated that individuals who consume coffee exhibit lower tremor scores than those who do not, with a correlation observed between the severity of tremors and the amount of coffee consumed [225]. In addition, coffee was found to have a protective effect against motor function decline, mortality, and cognitive decline in patients with PD [113]. However, some studies have shown that there is no meaningful connection between the dosage and duration of coffee consumption and the presence of motor or non-motor symptoms in patients with early-stage PD [10]. There are multiple reasons why coffee may offer protection against the development of PD. One reason is that a component of coffee acts as an adenosine A2A receptor antagonist, leading to decreased α-synuclein aggregation in SynT-Synphilin-1 neuroglioma cells [226]. Additionally, coffee consumption not only exhibits anti-inflammatory properties but also mitigates the risk of chronic diseases like diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular disease, all of which indirectly influence the development and progression of PD [227].

4.2.2. Tea

Multiple studies and meta-analyses have consistently shown an inverse relationship between tea consumption and the risk of developing PD. Increased tea consumption is associated with a reduced risk of developing PD [228], with a potential decrease in PD risk of 26% [229]. While most studies have focused on the preventive effects of tea consumption, emerging evidence suggests that tea may also have the potential to slow down PD progression. A study highlighted the role of tea drinking in modifying the progression of idiopathic REM sleep behavior disorder (iRBD) to α-synucleinopathies, including PD [230]. The study found that tea drinking was associated with a decreased risk of phenoconversion from iRBD to PD, suggesting a protective role of tea in the early stages of neurodegenerative disease progression [230]. Tea is rich in polyphenols, especially catechins like EGCG, which possess significant neuroprotective properties. These compounds reduce oxidative stress, a major factor in PD pathogenesis, by scavenging free radicals and enhancing antioxidant defense [229]. Polyphenols also modulate signaling pathways and metal chelation, which are crucial for reducing neuronal damage. Studies on black tea have consistently shown a significant inverse association between black tea consumption and the risk of PD. The Singapore Chinese Health Study found that black tea consumption was associated with a reduced risk of PD, independent of caffeine intake or tobacco smoking [231]. This protective effect is thought to be due to the presence of arubigins and theaflavins, which are formed during the oxidation of catechins in black tea. While green tea is rich in polyphenols, particularly catechins like epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), its association with reduced PD risk in human populations remains inconclusive [231]. Some studies have not found a significant association between green tea consumption and reduced PD risk [231]. However, green tea polyphenols (GTPs) have demonstrated protective effects against dopaminergic neuron degeneration in preclinical models, suggesting their potential neuroprotective properties [231]. A study in Japan found that the intake of Japanese and Chinese teas was significantly inversely associated with the risk of PD, supporting the notion that various types of tea are beneficial [232]. Theanine, an amino acid found in tea, can inhibit glutamate receptors and regulate extracellular glutamine concentrations, providing neuroprotective effects by reducing excitotoxicity [229].

4.2.3. Diet Habits

Dietary habits can have both negative and positive impacts on PD. A dietary regimen characterized by elevated consumption of fruits, fish, vegetables, whole grains, nuts, legumes, and poultry, coupled with reduced intake of saturated fats and moderate alcohol consumption, may serve as a protective factor against PD [233]. The relationship between nutrition and PD is complex, involving various dietary patterns and specific nutrients that may influence the risk and progression of the disease. The Mediterranean diet, which is rich in fruits, vegetables, nuts, and fish, has been associated with a reduced risk of PD and slower disease progression. This diet is thought to exert its effects through anti-inflammatory and antioxidant mechanisms, which help mitigate oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, key factors in PD pathogenesis [234,235].

4.2.4. Vitamins

Longitudinal studies have indicated that a persistent deficiency in vitamin D is associated with a higher likelihood of developing PD [236]. The increased risk may be due to the chronic lack of vitamin D, which can result in the depletion of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra region and ultimately contribute to the onset of PD [236]. Moreover, previous studies have shown that there are seasonal and sunlight-related impacts on dopamine transporter (DAT) expression in both individuals without any health issues and those in the early stages of PD [237]. It has been suggested that dietary vitamin E may play a role in protecting against PD, indicating that incorporating vitamin E-rich foods into one’s diet could be an immediate option for reducing the risk of PD [238]. Patients with PD have been found to have lower levels of vitamin B12 than healthy controls, which could potentially be linked to the onset of early gait instability and neuropathy [239]. Additionally, it was observed that patients with PD who did not experience dementia had elevated levels of vitamin B12 compared to cognitively healthy patients with PD, suggesting that a higher vitamin B12 level might reduce the risk of developing dementia in the future [240]. Similarly, adequate intake of vitamins, such as B6 and folate, may support neurological health and reduce PD risk by lowering homocysteine levels, which are linked to neurodegeneration [241]. Minerals like magnesium and zinc are also important, as they are involved in numerous enzymatic processes that support neuronal function and may help slow PD progression [241].

4.2.5. Calcium

The role of calcium in PD is less clear, but it is essential for various cellular processes, including neurotransmitter release and neuronal excitability. Dysregulation of calcium homeostasis contributes to neuronal death, a hallmark of PD. Therefore, maintaining adequate calcium levels through diet may support neuronal health and potentially slow the disease progression [241].

4.2.6. Gut–Brain Axis

Emerging research has highlighted the gut–brain axis as a significant factor in PD. The gut microbiota can influence neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration, suggesting that dietary interventions that promote a healthy gut microbiome may impact PD risk and progression. Diets rich in fiber and probiotics may enhance gut health and, consequently, brain health, offering a potential therapeutic avenue for PD [242].

4.2.7. Uric Acid (Urate Levels)

Recent studies have highlighted the potential of uric acid (UA) in reducing the risk of PD. A 1 mg/dL increase in serum urate is associated with a 6% reduction in PD risk [243]. As a potent endogenous antioxidant, UA accounts for 60–70% of the antioxidant activity in human plasma [244], and its properties are especially relevant in PD, where oxidative stress is a key factor in neuronal damage [245]. UA mitigates oxidative stress by scavenging free radicals and reactive oxygen species (ROS) in neuronal cells. It also inhibits lipid peroxidation and protects cellular components from damage. Mitochondrial dysfunction is central to PD, and UA’s ability to protect mitochondrial integrity may help preserve neuronal function [245]. Additionally, UA may reduce inflammatory damage to neurons by modulating the inflammatory pathways activated in PD [245]. It also aids in maintaining calcium homeostasis, a critical factor in neuronal function, as disruptions in calcium signaling are implicated in PD pathogenesis. One proposed mechanism of UA’s neuroprotective effect of UA is the activation of Nrf2, a transcription factor that regulates antioxidant proteins, offering protection against oxidative damage in PD. Research comparing UA levels in patients with PD and healthy controls shows significantly lower serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) UA levels in patients with PD, suggesting that factors beyond purine metabolism, such as age, sex, and weight, may influence UA levels [246]. Higher serum urate levels are associated with a reduced PD risk, supporting UA’s potential neuroprotective effects of UA [243]. Despite these findings, some studies have reported no significant correlation between high UA levels and reduced PD risk, and the role of UA in PD progression remains debated [246]. Additionally, the causality of low UA levels in PD remains under discussion [246].

4.3. Medications and Medical Factors

Medications and medical factors are vital for preventing or slowing PD progression. For example, anti-inflammatory drugs can reduce neuroinflammation, which is associated with PD development and potentially slows disease progression. Neuroprotective medications help preserve neurons by neutralizing free radicals and supporting mitochondrial function. Table 13 provides an overview of the medications and medical factors, along with their respective mechanisms, in the prevention and slowing of PD progression.

4.3.1. Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs)

NSAIDs have been explored for their potential neuroprotective effects in PD due to their ability to inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, particularly COX-2, which is upregulated in PD [247]. By inhibiting COX-2, NSAIDs reduce the production of pro-inflammatory prostaglandins, potentially mitigating neuroinflammation and slowing neurodegeneration [247]. NSAIDs, such as aspirin and ibuprofen, may also decrease elevated cytokines like TNF-α and IL-1β, which contribute to neuroinflammation and neuronal death in PD [248]. Aspirin and Ibuprofen have been shown to effectively reduce the risk of PD and postpone the age of onset of PD by as much as 5 years [220,249]. It is possible that the anti-inflammatory effects of aspirin and ibuprofen play a role in decreasing PD risk [250]. Furthermore, aspirin has been shown to boost the production of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), an enzyme that controls the rate of dopamine (DA) synthesis. This leads to an elevation in the level of DA found in dopaminergic neurons [251]. Additionally, aspirin and ibuprofen have the potential to decrease the likelihood of LRRK2 mutations, indicating that anti-inflammatory medications could serve as beneficial treatments for modifying the progression of LRRK2-PD [252]. Epidemiological studies suggest that regular NSAID use is associated with a reduced risk of developing PD, although the results are inconsistent across studies. Experimental models have also demonstrated the protective effects of NSAIDs against neurotoxin-induced dopaminergic neuron degeneration. However, long-term NSAID use is associated with gastrointestinal and cardiovascular risks, warranting careful consideration in PD treatment.

4.3.2. Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)

CCBs have been studied for their potential to reduce the risk and progression of PD by modulating calcium ion flows, which is crucial for neurotransmitter release and neuronal health. In dopaminergic neurons, particularly those in the substantia nigra, CCBs help regulate calcium influx, which is critical given the vulnerability of these neurons to calcium-induced stress due to their pacemaking activity [253]. L-type calcium channels, such as those with CaV1.3 subunits, contribute to mitochondrial oxidative stress, a key factor in PD pathogenesis. Recent research also suggests that Cav2.3 channels may play a role in regulating neuronal viability in the substantia nigra, potentially offering neuroprotective benefits [254]. While a systematic review and meta-analysis found that CCB use was associated with a significantly reduced risk of PD (relative risk: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.62–0.99) [255], other studies, such as a large prospective cohort study, found no significant association [256]. Additionally, a clinical trial with isradipine, a CCB, did not show significant effects on slowing early-stage PD progression [255].

4.3.3. Statins

Statins, primarily known for their cholesterol-lowering effects, have been investigated for their neuroprotective potential in PD due to their anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, and neuroprotective properties. By suppressing pro-inflammatory molecules and microglial activation, statins help to reduce neuroinflammation in PD. These effects are mediated through the inhibition of the mevalonate pathway, which is crucial for synthesizing inflammatory mediators. Statins also inhibit oxidative stress, a major factor in PD pathogenesis, and potentially protect dopaminergic neurons from degeneration. Furthermore, statins can act as ligands for PPARα, a regulator of mitochondrial function, which is often compromised in PD [257]. Evidence suggests that statins, particularly simvastatin, due to its ability to cross the blood-brain barrier, may slow PD progression by attenuating α-synuclein aggregation [257]. A systematic review and meta-analysis suggested that atorvastatin may reduce PD risk, attributed to its potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidative effects [258]. However, further randomized controlled trials are required to confirm these findings.

4.3.4. α1-Adrenergic Antagonists

α1-Adrenergic antagonists, such as terazosin, doxazosin, and alfuzosin, primarily used to treat benign prostatic hyperplasia, have shown potential neuroprotective effects in PD by enhancing glycolysis and energy metabolism, which are impaired in PD [259]. These drugs activate PGK1, the first ATP-generating enzyme in glycolysis, thereby increasing ATP production and potentially counteracting the energy deficits observed in PD. Moreover, α1-adrenoceptor antagonists may modulate the locus coeruleus, a key noradrenergic center affected in PD, potentially influencing dopamine release and providing neuroprotection against PD. A cohort study involving Danish and U.S. health registries found that users of terazosin, doxazosin, and alfuzosin had a significantly lower risk of developing PD than users of tamsulosin, which does not enhance glycolysis. The hazard ratios for developing PD were 0.88 in the Danish cohort and 0.63 in the U.S. cohort, indicating a reduced risk associated with these drugs [259].

4.4. Occupational Factors

Certain occupational categories, particularly those involving high levels of physical activity, have been shown to significantly reduce the risk of developing PD. Occupations such as engineering, production work (including machine operators and fabricators), metalworking, and construction or extractive work (e.g., mining and oil well drilling) have been associated with a lower PD risk [260]. Research has highlighted that jobs requiring significant physical exertion are linked to a decreased risk of PD, consistent with the well-established protective effects of regular physical exercise (Table 14). Occupations that demand consistent physical activity may confer neuroprotective benefits similar to those of structured exercise regimens. Multiple studies have also demonstrated a strong association between artistic occupations and a reduced risk of PD. For instance, one study indicated that men engaged in artistic professions later in life had a significantly lower risk of developing PD [261]. This protective effect is thought to be related to the dopaminergic activity required for creativity, which may be better preserved in individuals working in the artistic fields [261]. A case-control study conducted in Japan found that professional or technical occupations were inversely related to the risk of PD, particularly in men. This suggests that careers requiring high levels of cognitive engagement and technical skills may have a protective effect against the disease [262]. Interestingly, certain farming activities are associated with a lower risk of developing PD. A study of French farm managers revealed that activities such as gardening, landscaping, reforestation, small animal farming, and horse-related tasks (e.g., training, dressage, and riding) were associated with a reduced PD risk [263]. This finding is particularly notable, given that farming has typically been associated with an increased PD risk due to pesticide exposure. Occupations that involve physical activity and cognitive engagement may enhance neuroplasticity and promote overall brain health. Such activities are thought to reduce the risk of neurodegenerative diseases like PD, by maintaining robust neural networks and fostering neuronal resilience. Physical activity, a key component of many protective occupations, has been linked to improved mitochondrial function and reduced oxidative stress, both of which are crucial for maintaining neuronal health and potentially slowing neurodegenerative processes. Additionally, occupations that involve regular physical activity or cognitive stimulation may increase the production of neurotrophic factors, such as brain-derived neurotrophic factors (BDNF), which support neuronal survival and function. The production of BDNF may provide further protection against PD. Some occupational activities may also contribute to reducing neuroinflammation, a key factor in PD pathogenesis. This effect could be mediated through the direct anti-inflammatory effects of physical activity or indirect mechanisms involving occupational exposures that modulate inflammatory pathways. Furthermore, occupations that promote physical activity may enhance autophagy, a cellular process crucial for removing damaged proteins and organelles. Efficient autophagy is particularly important for preventing the accumulation of α-synuclein, a hallmark of PD pathology.

4.5. Therapeutic Implications

The state of the gut microbiota significantly influences PD progression by maintaining chronic inflammation and facilitating the spread of misfolded proteins from the gut to the brain. Emerging therapeutic strategies targeting gut dysbiosis aim to restore the microbiota balance and potentially slow PD progression. These strategies include probiotics and dietary interventions, which have shown promise in preclinical studies [154,156].

5. Challenges and Solutions in Identifying Risk and Protective Factors for PD

Identifying risk and protective factors for PD is a complex task due to the multifactorial nature of the disease and the intricate interplay between genetic, environmental, and behavioral factors. PD is influenced by a combination of genetic predisposition, environmental exposure, and lifestyle behaviors [263]. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified over 200 genes associated with PD [19]. However, the genetic landscape is further complicated by rare variants, which are difficult to replicate across studies due to their low frequency [264]. Additionally, the interactions between genetic factors and environmental exposures, such as pesticides and industrial chemicals, remain poorly understood, making it challenging to identify common molecular pathways and therapeutic targets [265]. PD is a heterogeneous disorder with multiple subtypes, each potentially governed by distinct molecular mechanisms [265]. This heterogeneity complicates the identification of universal risk or protective factors, as findings relevant to one subtype may not apply to others. Variability in disease progression, shaped by genetic diversity and environmental influences, underscores the need for personalized research approaches and consideration of population-specific genetic architectures [31].

Several methodological challenges hinder progress in identifying the risk and protective factors for PD. Cross-sectional studies, commonly used in PD research, fail to capture the temporal relationship between exposure and disease onset. While longitudinal studies are more informative, they are resource-intensive and are conducted less frequently [15]. Confounding factors, such as lifestyle differences, regional variations in environmental exposures, and genetic predispositions, can obscure true associations common [266]. Many studies also face issues like recall bias in case-control designs and difficulties in establishing causality in observational research. Furthermore, inconsistencies across studies—stemming from variations in study design, sample size, and methodologies—impede the development of clear preventive guidelines. For example, while some studies have identified smoking and coffee consumption as protective factors, others have not observed significant associations [266]. Recent advancements in research methodologies offer promising avenues for understanding the etiology of PD. Techniques such as polygenic risk scores, epigenetic analyses, and Mendelian randomization (MR) provide new opportunities to explore genetic and environmental interactions [265]. However, these approaches require validation and standardization to ensure their broad applicability. The lack of comprehensive data integrating genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors further limits the holistic understanding of PD risk [266].

To address these challenges, standardizing research methodologies is essential. The implementation of uniform protocols for data collection, clinical assessments, and environmental exposure reporting can reduce inconsistencies across studies [267]. Multi-omics approaches, which integrate genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics with artificial intelligence, can uncover patterns that traditional methods might miss, aiding in the identification of biomarkers and elucidating PD’s molecular underpinnings. Advanced trial designs, including adaptive trials and studies targeting pre-symptomatic stages, can improve our understanding of risk factors and protective measures before significant neuronal damage occurs. Incorporating digital tools and telehealth into research can provide continuous monitoring and overcome the limitations associated with traditional clinical endpoints [15].

Large-scale collaborative initiatives are critical for overcoming data fragmentation and leveraging diverse expertise. Establishing centers of excellence, such as those focusing on complex disease genetics (e.g., University of Helsinki), and fostering international collaborations can accelerate data sharing and discoveries [267]. Emerging technologies, including next-generation sequencing (NGS), medical digital twins, and blockchain-based data management systems, offer promising pathways for enhancing data integration, ensuring privacy, and enabling personalized research strategies. By addressing these challenges and embracing innovative solutions, the field can progress toward a more comprehensive understanding of PD etiology, ultimately enabling more effective prevention and therapeutic interventions. 6. Integrating Risk Factors into Biomarker Discovery

PD biomarkers fall into five main types: clinical (motor and non-motor symptoms), imaging (e.g., dopamine transporter scans), biochemical (e.g., α-synuclein in CSF or blood), genetic (e.g., LRRK2 and GBA mutations), and digital (data from wearables and apps) [268,269]. Their accuracy varies: imaging biomarkers such as DAT-SPECT are most reliable for diagnosis, especially early on; biochemical and clinical markers have moderate to limited accuracy; genetic biomarkers help assess risk but not diagnosis; and digital markers show potential for monitoring but require further validation [270,271]. Integrating known PD risk factors into biomarker research may improve early detection, diagnostic precision, and tailored treatment strategies [270].

6.1. Age-Related Molecular Biomarkers

Age is the most prominent risk factor for PD; however, chronological age fails to capture the heterogeneity of biological aging processes. Molecular markers reflecting oxidative stress, such as advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) damage, may serve as proxies for age-related neuronal vulnerability. Additionally, the application of epigenetic clocks in PD-relevant tissues offers a compelling avenue for quantifying accelerated biological aging, potentially identifying individuals at heightened risk prior to clinical onset.

6.2. Sex-Specific Biological Signatures

Sex-based differences in the prevalence and progression of PD suggest a role for sex hormones and sex-linked molecular pathways in disease susceptibility. For example, reduced estrogen receptor expression and altered testosterone-to-estradiol ratios may serve as sex-specific biomarkers, particularly relevant to the increased risk observed in postmenopausal women and the modulatory effects of hormone replacement therapy. Conversely, the male predominance in PD incidence may be related to androgenic influence or sex-specific gene regulation. Transcriptomic profiling stratified by sex could uncover the molecular pathways underpinning the differential risk of neuroprotection.

6.3. Metabolic and Lifestyle-Derived Biomarkers

Lifestyle factors, including diet, physical activity, and smoking, influence the risk of PD by modulating systemic metabolism and chronic inflammation. Metabolomic profiling, encompassing lipidomics and oxidative stress markers (e.g., malondialdehyde and isoprostanes), may elucidate the metabolic signatures linked to prodromal or progressive PD. Furthermore, circulating levels of adipokines, such as leptin and adiponectin, which modulate both metabolic and inflammatory pathways, may bridge systemic metabolic health and neurodegenerative processes, representing potential biomarkers at this intersection.

6.4. Environmental Exposure and Toxicological Biomarkers

Environmental exposure, particularly to pesticides and industrial metals, is strongly associated with an elevated risk of PD. Biomarkers, such as urinary pesticide metabolites and blood concentrations of heavy metals (e.g., manganese and iron), offer a quantitative measure of toxic burden. These exposures are known to elevate oxidative stress biomarkers, including 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) and malondialdehyde (MDA), which correlate with neurodegenerative changes in patients with PD. Moreover, toxins like paraquat and rotenone induce α-synuclein aggregation, a key pathological hallmark of PD, which can be detected through real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC) or quantification of oligomeric species in the CSF or plasma. Environmental exposures may also elicit neuroinflammatory responses, as reflected by elevated levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) in biofluids. A composite biomarker strategy that integrates environmental toxin levels with DNA adducts or inflammatory signatures could serve as an early detection and risk stratification tool.

6.5. Metabolic Dysregulation and Endocrine Markers

Systemic metabolic dysfunction, including insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and type 2 diabetes mellitus, has been increasingly implicated in PD pathogenesis. Biomarkers such as glycated α-synuclein, insulin signaling intermediates, and advanced glycation end-products may reflect neurotoxic metabolic states. Furthermore, lipidomic profiles may reveal dysregulated lipid metabolism in PD, offering an additional layer of disease-specific metabolic markers for PD.

6.6. Neuroinflammation and the Gut–Brain Axis

Emerging evidence implicates neuroinflammation and gut microbiota dysbiosis in the etiology of PD. Pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), markers of microglial activation, and intestinal permeability indicators (e.g., zonulin, lipopolysaccharide-binding protein) are candidate biomarkers for the inflammatory component of PD. Moreover, alterations in short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) profiles and microbe-derived metabolites provide insights into gut–brain interactions and may serve as early, non-invasive biomarkers.

6.7. Pharmacogenomics and Epigenetic Modifications

The growing field of pharmacogenomics offers an opportunity to tailor PD treatment regimens. L-DOPA metabolite profiles and dopamine pathway genotyping may predict therapeutic efficacy and adverse event susceptibility. Epigenetic markers, including DNA methylation patterns, histone post-translational modifications, and non-coding RNAs, can illuminate gene–environment interactions and disease trajectories. For example, aberrant methylation of PD-associated loci may serve as early indicators of disease predisposition and progression. Notably, certain oncologic therapies may modulate neuroinflammatory or neuroprotective pathways, warranting investigation into their impact on PD risk using biomarkers, such as NF-κB activation or neurotrophin levels.

6.8. Population-Specific Genetic Risk Biomarkers

Ethnic and geographic variability in genetic susceptibility to PD underscores the importance of population-specific biomarker discovery. For instance, GBA mutations, prevalent in 5–10% of PD patients of Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry, represent a well-validated genetic risk marker for PD. Similarly, the LRRK2 p.G2385R variant, associated with increased PD risk in East Asian populations, and PLA2G6 mutations observed in early-onset PD in Eastern China support the development of ancestry-informed biomarker strategies. Epigenetic variation across racial groups—such as differential DNA methylation patterns—may further refine risk assessment and facilitate the identification of population-tailored diagnostic tools.

7. Raising Awareness in PD

Raising awareness of PD is crucial for understanding its mechanisms, risk factors, and factors influencing its onset and progression. For healthcare providers, staying informed about PD helps in better management of risk factors like genetics, age, and environmental influences. Highlighting preventive measures, such as regular physical activity, balanced nutrition, and early detection, is essential to reduce risks and improve patient outcomes. Raising awareness among professionals and patients can lead to more effective management strategies, ultimately enhancing the quality of life and minimizing the impact of the disease. Table 16 outlines recommendations for the prevention and management of PD in healthy individuals, individuals diagnosed with PD, and healthcare systems.

7.1. Demographic and Genetic Factors

Age: Aging is a major risk factor for PD, especially among individuals older than 60 years of age. Regular screenings (e.g., cognitive assessments and dopamine transporter scans) for neurodegenerative markers are crucial for early detection. Healthy individuals older than 60 years of age should consider these preventive measures. Patients with PD should undergo regular check-ups to monitor disease progression. Health systems should prioritize neurodegenerative screening in older adults for early intervention.

Sex: Men are at a higher risk for developing PD. Public health campaigns should target men, focusing on both motor and non-motor symptoms. Healthy men should engage in regular screenings, while those diagnosed with PD require tailored care for gender-specific symptoms.

Race and Ethnicity: Certain racial and ethnic groups have a higher PD risk. Targeted health programs and screenings can improve early diagnosis and access to treatment. High-risk populations should participate in community-based screenings, and health systems should implement targeted interventions.

Family History and Genetics: A family history of PD increases the risk. Genetic counseling and predictive testing (e.g., LRRK2 mutations) can help monitor and personalize preventive care. Genetically predisposed individuals may benefit from targeted therapies like LRRK2 inhibitors. Health systems should provide genetic counseling and predictive testing for at-risk families.

7.2. Cognitive and Educational Factors

Engaging in intellectually stimulating activities (e.g., reading and learning new skills) can reduce the risk of PD progression and support cognitive health. Patients with PD should incorporate cognitive therapy into their daily routine. Healthcare providers should encourage lifelong cognitive engagement and brain health-focused educational programs.

7.3. Environmental and Occupational Risks

Toxins: Exposure to pesticides, heavy metals, and industrial chemicals increases the risk of PD. Workers should wear protective gear and seek safer alternatives to reduce exposure. Regular health check-ups and screenings are vital for individuals exposed to toxins. Health systems should mandate safety regulations and offer regular screening for at-risk workers.

Air Pollution: Living in high-pollution areas increases the risk. Urban residents should use air purifiers and wear masks. Public health initiatives should emphasize the connection between air pollution and PD risk and advocate cleaner air policies.

Socioeconomic Disparities: Limited healthcare access increases the risk of PD in underserved populations. Expanding preventive screening and improving access to care in these communities are essential. Health systems should provide affordable screening and treatment for underserved populations.

7.4. Lifestyle and Health Habits

Nutrition and Diet: A diet rich in antioxidants, healthy fats, and anti-inflammatory nutrients (e.g., the Mediterranean diet) supports brain health. Patients with PD should focus on neuroprotective foods (e.g., omega-3s and vitamin E). Health systems should encourage nutritional counseling for individuals at risk and those with PD.

Physical Activity: Regular exercise, especially aerobic activities and balance-enhancing exercises, can reduce the risk of PD. Patients with PD should follow personalized exercise plans to manage their symptoms. Health systems should promote physical activity and integrate physical therapy into PD management.

Alcohol Consumption: Excessive alcohol intake may contribute to neurotoxicity. Healthy individuals should limit their alcohol intake to protect their brain health. Patients with PD should monitor their alcohol consumption to avoid interactions with medications. Health systems should promote healthy drinking habits and provide resources for reducing alcohol consumption.

Body Weight and Metabolic Health: Maintaining a healthy weight through diet and exercise reduces the risk of neurodegeneration. Patients with PD should monitor their BMI and metabolic health to prevent disease progression. Health systems should prioritize metabolic health screening in at-risk individuals and patients with PD.

7.5. Metabolic and Systemic Health Conditions

Diabetes and Hypertension: Managing blood sugar and blood pressure can reduce the risk of PD. Patients with PD should closely monitor these conditions, as they may exacerbate neurological decline. Healthcare providers should emphasize the management of diabetes and hypertension in PD care.

Cholesterol and Triglycerides: Maintaining balanced lipid levels through diet and medication can reduce inflammation and protect brain health. Patients with PD should regularly monitor their lipid levels. Health systems should incorporate lipid screening into PD management and preventive care.

Metabolic Syndrome: Addressing metabolic syndrome through weight management and a healthy lifestyle reduces the risk of neurodegeneration. Patients with PD should focus on improving their diet and weight management to reduce systemic inflammation. Health systems should offer metabolic screening for at-risk populations.

7.6. Inflammatory and Immune-Related Factors

Neuroinflammation and Chronic Diseases: Anti-inflammatory treatments (e.g., NSAIDs and curcumin) may help mitigate PD-related inflammation. Management of chronic inflammatory diseases (e.g., arthritis) can support neurological health. Vaccination can also reduce the risk of infection. Patients with PD should consider anti-inflammatory treatments and manage chronic conditions. Health systems should integrate care for chronic inflammatory diseases and promote vaccination programs.

7.7. Neurological and Psychological Factors

Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI): High-risk occupations should enforce safety measures (e.g., helmet use and concussion monitoring) and provide annual neurocognitive assessments.

Sleep Disorders: Screening for sleep disturbances (e.g., REM sleep behavior disorder) is important, as these are linked to increased PD risk. Treatment options like melatonin or CPAP therapy can improve sleep quality.

Loss of Smell (Anosmia): Anosmia is an early symptom of PD. Routine smell tests during neurological assessments can help detect high-risk individuals.

Stress, Depression, and Anxiety: Chronic stress and mental health issues can accelerate neurodegeneration in the brain. Psychological support (e.g., CBT) is essential. Early intervention for depression and anxiety can improve the quality of life and may slow PD progression.

7.8. Gut–Brain Axis and Microbiome Health

Maintaining gut health through a balanced diet rich in prebiotics and probiotics is essential. Patients with PD should work with healthcare providers to monitor their gut health and explore probiotic therapies. Health systems should facilitate collaboration between neurologists and gastroenterologists to explore the gut–brain connections in PD care.

7.9. Hormonal Factors and Other Health Conditions

Estrogen and Neuroprotection: Postmenopausal women may benefit from hormone replacement therapy (HRT) for neuroprotection. Regular hormonal evaluation should guide treatment.

Uric Acid Levels: Managing uric acid levels through diet and hydration may help lower the risk of neurodegenerative diseases. Patients with PD should monitor their uric acid levels to reduce neurodegeneration. Health systems should offer regular evaluations of HRT and monitor uric acid levels in patients with PD.

7.10. Medications and Emerging Treatments

Neuroprotective Drug Strategies: Emerging treatments like GLP-1 receptor agonists may reduce PD risk. Healthy individuals at risk should discuss these treatment options with their healthcare providers. Patients with PD should explore these drugs and monitor the long-term effects of medication. Health systems should support research on neuroprotective drugs and monitor the long-term effects of medication on PD care.

8. Conclusions

This paper has provided a comprehensive overview of the key risk and prevalent factors that highlight the considerable heterogeneity and complexity underlying the neuropathogenesis of PD. It is likely that multiple factors, with varying degrees of risk, interact within genetically predisposed individuals during critical stages of neurodegeneration, ultimately leading to the clinical manifestation of PD. For each risk and prevalent factor, we discussed the molecular mechanisms involved, which may help identify the interactive effects of multiple risk factors. Additionally, we explored the evolving understanding of risk and prevalence factors in PD, acknowledging that as a complex neurological disorder, new risk factors—particularly those related to genetics—are likely to be uncovered in the future. Furthermore, we highlighted several protective factors that may mitigate the development of PD or slow its progression, such as physical activity levels. These factors warrant increased attention from individuals and health systems alike to raise awareness and improve public health initiatives within our communities.
