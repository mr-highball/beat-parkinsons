Abstract

Parkinson’s disease (PD), the second most common neurodegenerative disorder, poses a significant challenge to elderly health. Its diagnosis currently relies primarily on clinical symptoms, which often proves inadequate for differentiating PD from other forms of parkinsonism, particularly in early disease stages. Although positron emission tomography (PET) has offered compelling evidence supporting the differential diagnosis of PD, its high cost and radiation exposure limit its widespread use. Consequently, there is a pressing need for more accessible, convenient, non-invasive, and safe diagnostic approaches. The “swallow tail sign” (STS)—an MRI-based biomarker characterized by a hyperintensity in the posterolateral substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc), surrounded by hypointense areas—has emerged as a highly promising adjunct tool. This biomarker holds potential not only to enhance diagnostic certainty in distinguishing PD from similar neurodegenerative disorders but also to improve the understanding of its pathophysiology. In this review, we outline the anatomical basis of the STS and the pathological changes in PD leading to its disappearance. We further summarize its applications across various magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques and evaluate its value in identifying PD and differentiating it from other parkinsonian syndromes. With significant advancements in ultra-high-field MRI (≥ 7 T) and artificial intelligence (AI), combined with other MRI biomarkers, the value of STS will be further explored in the future.

Key Points

Question Despite its diagnostic potential, the biological basis, imaging interpretation, and clinical utility of the STS remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation in parkinsonism.

Findings The normal STS appears as dorsolateral nigral hyperintensity on susceptibility-sensitive MRI, while its attenuation or loss in PD supports cautious adjunctive diagnostic use.

Critical Relevance Statement This review critically evaluates the biological basis, MRI interpretation, diagnostic limitations, and evolving multimodal and AI-based assessment of the STS, guiding radiologists toward its standardized and cautious adjunct use in the clinical imaging assessment of parkinsonism.

Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD), the second most common neurodegenerative disorder, is characterized clinically by bradykinesia, rigidity, rest tremor, and postural instability, while its key pathological feature is the degeneration and loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra (SN) [1, 2]. Over the past generation, largely driven by population aging, the global burden of PD has more than doubled, with current estimates suggesting that approximately 11.8 million individuals are affected worldwide [3]. Currently, the diagnosis of PD relies primarily on clinical evaluation. According to the Movement Disorder Society clinical diagnostic criteria, the diagnostic process first requires the confirmation of parkinsonism, defined by bradykinesia plus one of resting tremor and rigidity, or both, and then applying some specific criteria to determine if the syndrome is attributable to PD [4]. However, from a clinical perspective, using these specific criteria to accurately distinguish PD from other conditions that cause similar symptoms remains a major challenge, especially in the early stages. The differential diagnosis relies heavily on the clinician’s experience to exclude other conditions, such as atypical parkinsonian syndromes (AP) (e.g., multiple system atrophy (MSA), progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), corticobasal degeneration (CBD)), vascular parkinsonism, Lewy body dementia, and essential tremor (ET).

Recent advances in positron emission tomography (PET) and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) have provided substantial evidence to support the diagnosis of PD and its differentiation from other parkinsonian disorders. Several functional imaging techniques serve as valuable tools for evaluating nigrostriatal dopaminergic integrity, including dopamine transporter (DAT), vesicular monoamine transporter (VMAT), and iodine123-2β-carbomethoxy-3β-(4-iodophenyl)-N-(3-fluoropropyl)-nortropane (123I-FP-CIT) SPECT [5–7]. Additionally, 18F-FDG PET technology clearly reveals the disease-specific patterns of regional glucose metabolism [8, 9]. Novel radiotracers such as 18F-SPAL-T-06 and 18F-F0502B, which specifically target the α-synuclein protein, an important pathological hallmark of PD within the nigrostriatal pathway, have shown promising potential [10, 11]. These imaging modalities help support the diagnosis of PD and facilitate its differentiation from other parkinsonian disorders. However, the potential radiation risks associated with injecting radiotracers and the high cost of nuclear medicine imaging limit their widespread clinical use. Moreover, extensive research indicates that it lacks sufficient reliability to differentiate between PD and APs or to rule out alternative diagnoses, such as cerebrovascular disease [12]. In contrast, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is more accessible, convenient, non-invasive, and safe, making MRI biomarkers for PD an increasingly compelling research focus. The three main diagnostic approaches for PD are illustrated in Fig. 1.Fig. 1 The three main diagnostic methods of PD

In 2014, Schwarz et al first described the “swallow tail sign” (STS) on standard axial high-resolution 3-T susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI). This sign is characterized by a hyperintensity in the posterolateral one-third of the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc), which is surrounded by relatively hypointense areas, creating an appearance reminiscent of a swallow’s tail. They proposed that its absence could serve as a triage marker supporting the distinction between degenerative parkinsonism and non-degenerative mimics. Furthermore, within workflows aimed at distinguishing PD from AP, it could act as a valuable adjunct—particularly in cases where STS alone may be insufficient—as part of a broader multimodal imaging panel [13]. As a simple qualitative imaging biomarker for PD, the STS has subsequently become the focus of extensive and ongoing research.

Against this background, this review synthesizes current knowledge on STS, organized into several key sections: First, we outline the anatomical basis of the STS and the pathological changes in PD that lead to its loss. Subsequently, we review the existing literature to summarize the applications of STS across different MRI modalities and to evaluate its usefulness in identifying PD and distinguishing it from other parkinsonian disorders. With ongoing advancements in imaging technology, such as higher resolution and optimized parameters, as well as the significant progress in machine learning (ML), STS may play an increasingly vital role in enhancing the accuracy of PD diagnosis and differentiation from other parkinsonian syndromes.

Materials and methods

To survey the published articles, we conducted a thorough literature search in PubMed up to November 2025, using terms including “PD”, “parkinson’s disease”, “parkinson”, “atypical parkinsonism”, “parkinsonism”, “swallow tail”, “swallow tail sign”, “N1 sign”, “nigrosome-1 sign”, “dorsolateral nigral hyperintensity”. The inclusion criteria were clinical retrospective or prospective studies focused on STS, and the exclusion criteria included reviews, meta-analyses, or other literature types without original data. Study selection was based on screening the titles and abstracts according to the prespecified criteria, and to minimize the risk of omitting potentially relevant literature, full texts were retrieved and assessed when the eligibility was unclear. After carefully reviewing the retrieved literature, a total of 52 articles were ultimately kept that were directly related to STS.

Structural basis of the STS and its mechanism-based alterations

Normal structural basis of the STS

SN, located in the ventral tegmentum of the midbrain, is anatomically divided into the pars reticulata (SNr) and the pars compacta. The SNr is located in the ventral part of the SN, mainly composed of GABAergic neurons, while the SNc is located in the dorsal part, mainly composed of dopaminergic neurons that are rich in neuromelanin (NM) [14].

In 1999, Damier et al. first described that the SNc is further subdivided into nigrosomes and the nigral matrix according to specific immunohistochemical staining characteristics [15]. The nigrosomes were mainly composed of five calcium-binding protein-negative regions containing dopaminergic neurons, designated as Nigrosome-1 (N1) to Nigrosome-5 (N5). Approximately 60% of all dopaminergic neurons in the SN are located within the nigral matrix, while the remaining 40% are distributed across the five nigrosomes. Among these, Damier et al identified N1, the largest nigrosome—situated in the ventrolateral region of the SN—as the most severely affected in PD, exhibiting a dramatic loss of up to 98% of its dopaminergic neurons. Additionally, the degree of neuronal loss is ranked in the following order: N1 > N2 > N4 > N3 > N5 [16] (Fig. 2).Fig. 2 Anatomical subdivisions of the SN at three representative axial levels of the midbrain. In T2-weighted images (T2WI) and SWI of healthy individuals, the N1 showed hyperintensity, surrounded by the adjacent hypointense SN and medial lemniscus, forming a distinctive “swallow tail” [13]. Historically, the hyperintense inner region of STS has been conventionally attributed to N1, so the STS is also known as “N1 sign” [17]. For research purposes, it is also preferable to use the descriptive term “dorsolateral nigral hyperintensity” to describe STS [18]. However, a 2022 study by Brammerloh et al, combining 7-T in vivo MRI, 7-T postmortem MRI, three-dimensional block-face imaging, and immunohistochemistry, revealed that the STS only partially overlaps with N1, which is thinner, longer, and extends beyond the STS in both the anteroposterior and superoinferior dimensions [19]. Nevertheless, the non-equivalence between STS and N1 does not compromise the value of STS as a biomarker for PD. Therefore, for clarity and consistency, the term STS will be used uniformly throughout the remainder of this review. However, a more precise investigation into the relationship between MRI characteristics and the anatomy of the SN may pave the way for improved diagnosis and disease monitoring in PD in the future.

Mechanisms underlying the loss of the STS in PD

When using MRI to evaluate PD, NM and brain iron content serve as important imaging biomarkers, offering valuable pathophysiological insights into the onset and progression of PD and other neurodegenerative disorders. The literature consistently supports the view that, in patients with PD, NM levels decrease and iron content increases in the SNc [20–22].

NM is an intraneuronal pigment formed through the auto-oxidation of catecholamines. It is predominantly found in specific brainstem structures such as the SNc, ventral tegmental area, and locus ceruleus. Furthermore, NM acts as a metal ion chelator capable of binding iron. As a result, in the SNc of healthy individuals, a portion of iron exists in the form of NM–Fe complexes. In PD patients, however, the chelating function of NM is impaired, leading to the release of iron from these complexes and a consequent increase in free iron levels [23]. The loss of dopaminergic neurons in SNc causes NM to enter a depigmented state, which is thought to facilitate the deposition of its previously bound iron and other metals into adjacent brain tissues [24]. The released free iron, which is highly reactive, generates free radicals in the surrounding region via the Fenton reaction, promoting oxidative stress and other related reactions. These processes, in turn, contribute to the degeneration and loss of dopaminergic neurons in the SN, and the progressive depletion of these neurons further drives the pathogenesis and progression of the disease. Moreover, dysregulated levels of ferrous iron can drive neuroinflammation through various neurodegenerative processes [23].

Taken together, the disappearance of the STS on MRI mainly results from the following mechanism: the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in N1 region leads to reduced NM content and an accumulation of paramagnetic free iron (Fig. 3). Possible causes underlying the loss of the STS in healthy people

However, the absence of the STS is not unique to patients, as it can also occur in the healthy controls (HCs), as illustrated in Fig. 4. First, a postmortem 7-T SWI-MRI study revealed that the disappearance of STS is attributable not only to the iron deposition in brain parenchyma but also to the presence of microvessels containing iron or deoxyhemoglobin. At ultra-high field strengths, both iron deposition and microvascular structures collectively contribute to the hypointensity in the N1 region and its surroundings. Such microvascular features within the midbrain dopaminergic system may lead to false-positive interpretations of STS changes [25]. Second, studies have shown that approximately 19% of HCs exhibit an absence of STS, likely due to individual anatomical variations or physiological iron deposition associated with normal aging [26, 27]. Third, inter-individual differences in the content and molecular composition of NM within the SNc may lead to incomplete hyperintense visualization of STS, resulting in its absence in some HCs [28]. Recent progress in STS imaging

Iron-sensitive susceptibility imaging

SWI

The appearance and disappearance of STS is closely related to the level of iron content within the N1 region, making iron-sensitive susceptibility imaging a key radiological technique for its evaluation. Although conventional T2WI can reflect iron deposition, its sensitivity is limited [29]. Studies have indicated that SWI is currently a highly sensitive MRI sequence for assessing iron deposition in neurodegenerative diseases [30, 31]. SWI exploits magnetic susceptibility differences between tissues and is particularly sensitive to substances that induce local magnetic field inhomogeneities, such as iron and calcium. Its technical principles include the use of long echo times, three-dimensional gradient echo acquisition, and post-processing of phase information, which together enable the generation of high-contrast images that significantly enhance the detection of susceptibility-sensitive substances and distinguish between paramagnetic (e.g., iron) and diamagnetic (e.g., calcium) materials. Evolving from simple two-dimensional T2*-weighted sequences to three-dimensional sequences, SWI has improved spatial resolution and enhanced sensitivity and contrast [29, 31].

The STS can be visualized on both T2WI [32] and T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery sequences (T2-FLAIR) [33]. However, compared with T2WI and T2-FLAIR, SWI offers a higher contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) and a clearer depiction of STS anatomy, enabling more distinct visualization of structures such as N1 [29, 34] (Fig. 5). Compared to single-echo SWI, multi-echo SWI can further improve the CNR, attributed to increased signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Moreover, studies indicate that there is no significant difference in CNR measurements between 20-channel and 64-channel coils for visualizing anatomical details of STS, suggesting that SWI performance in STS visualization may not be affected by the number of coil channels [34]. However, slice thickness is critical for diagnostic accuracy. One study indicated that when using a clinical 3-T SWI sequence with a slice thickness of 3 mm, STS had limited diagnostic value in distinguishing PD from AP, likely due to partial volume effects from excessive slice thickness, which hindered accurate identification of the N1 [35]. When the slice thickness was reduced from 2 mm to 1.5–1.6 mm, accuracy improved from 67% to 84%. However, the use of SWI to assess the STS for PD diagnosis remains controversial. The study by Sotoudeh et al reported that visual assessment of STS absence on SWI demonstrated extremely high diagnostic value for PD, with reported sensitivity of approximately 100% and specificity of about 95% [30]. A recent study by Xing et al showed that in clinically uncertain parkinsonian syndromes, evaluating the absence of STS using high-quality 3-T SWI yielded excellent diagnostic performance, which shows 91% sensitivity, 87% specificity, 90% accuracy, and a positive predictive value as high as 95%. Its diagnostic value was comparable to the current gold standard, 123I-FP-CIT, suggesting its potential as an effective clinical triage tool [37]. A study shows that functional PET imaging (6-[18F]FDOPA) still holds a diagnostic advantage over STS evaluation [38]. A systematic review found that the absence of STS had high diagnostic accuracy in distinguishing patients with parkinsonism from those with non-degenerative movement disorders, with an overall sensitivity ranging from 73% to 100% and specificity from 75% to 100% [39]. Additionally, a meta-analysis indicated that in non-degenerative parkinsonian disorders, such as vascular or drug-induced parkinsonism, the absence of STS was observed in only 21.7% of cases, suggesting that the absence of STS is more likely to indicate a neurodegenerative etiology [40]. Furthermore, the absence of STS shows potential in identifying individuals with rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (RBD) who are at high risk of conversion to PD [39, 41, 42].

Nevertheless, Prasuhn et al reported contrasting findings, observing no statistically significant difference in STS absence between PD and HC and noting low diagnostic performance (unilateral sensitivity 37%, bilateral sensitivity only 9%; unilateral specificity 78%, with an overall area under the ROC curve of only 0.5). They concluded that simple binary visual assessment of STS provides limited value for diagnosing PD in routine clinical practice [43]. Therefore, when using STS for diagnostic evaluation of PD, it is important to pay attention to the assessment method. As described by Schwarz et al the most reliable method currently recognized is the evaluation using a 6-point visibility scale for each hemi-mesencephalon [44].

Importantly, several neurological disorders may mimic STS abnormalities and limit its diagnostic specificity, which should be considered during image interpretation. Kim et al suggested that STS abnormalities alone cannot serve as a reliable biomarker for diagnosing idiopathic PD and may only indicate degeneration of N1 [45]. A meta-analysis revealed that STS disappearance occurred in 89.4% of AP, making it difficult to distinguish PD from these overlapping conditions [40]. Large-scale 3-T SWI studies have demonstrated that loss of nigral hyperintensity can be observed in PD, MSA, and PSP, reflecting overlapping abnormalities in N1 visualization and limiting its ability to differentiate parkinsonian disorders [18, 40, 46, 47]. In contrast, CBD generally preserves the nigrosome structure, although overlap may still occur in individual cases [48].

Therefore, STS should be regarded as an indicator of N1 degeneration rather than a disease-specific biomarker. Careful consideration of potential mimickers is essential in clinical interpretation. Additional MRI findings may aid in the differential diagnosis of APs. Pontine and cerebellar atrophy accompanied by the “hot cross bun sign” are characteristic imaging features of the MSA cerebellar variant (MSA-C), whereas the “putaminal rim sign” is typically observed in patients with MSA parkinsonian variant (MSA-P). In contrast, PSP is characterized by selective midbrain atrophy with the axial “Mickey Mouse sign” and sagittal “hummingbird sign” (Fig. 6) [12, 49]. Moreover, other neurodegenerative and non-neurodegenerative disorders may also present with STS abnormalities. For example, studies have reported that the absence of STS on SWI has considerable diagnostic value in distinguishing dementia with Lewy bodies from other types of dementia, with sensitivity and specificity reaching 82% and 79%, respectively [50, 51]. Furthermore, visual assessment of STS has been proposed as a potential marker of nigral neurodegeneration in α-synucleinopathies, with reported sensitivity and specificity of 85% and 82% [52]. STS absence has also been reported in multiple sclerosis [53] and even neurosyphilis [54], possibly related to iron deposition or structural alterations within the SN, further underscoring its lack of disease specificity. Non-degenerative parkinsonian disorders, including vascular parkinsonism and drug-induced parkinsonism, usually show preserved visibility of the STS, however, occasional STS abnormalities have been reported, further emphasizing that STS loss is not disease-specific [39, 45].

In addition to PD diagnosis, studies have also investigated the clinical manifestation, progression, and treatment of STS and PD. Studies have confirmed that abnormal STS is commonly observed contralateral to the affected limb [55]. However, in clinical practice, abnormal STS occasionally appears on the ipsilateral side as well. Some researchers suggest that in the context of severe neuronal loss in N1, the dopaminergic neurons in the remaining smaller clusters may take on greater significance, potentially accounting for the lack of concordance with clinical asymmetry observed in certain patients [5]. Studies have reported that in PD patients, the absence of STS is not associated with Hoehn-Yahr stage (p = 0.189) or with medication treatment (p = 0.833), indicating that STS cannot be used for “staging assessment” or “treatment monitoring”. The presence of STS is unaffected by Parkinson’s medication, suggesting that STS reflects irreversible structural pathology in the SN rather than reversible functional states [56, 57].

Optimized iron-sensitive imaging

Traditional SWI processing employs a mask on the high-pass filtered phase images, which can enhance the visibility of veins and iron but is geometry-dependent, potentially compromising susceptibility contrast and reducing STS recognition effectiveness [58, 59]. Furthermore, a key limitation of STS is that it is fundamentally a qualitative imaging marker, rendering its evaluation susceptible to human subjectivity and devoid of quantitative information. To address these limitations, magnetic-sensitive imaging technology has undergone significant evolution.

Quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) directly calculates the susceptibility values within tissue through complex post-processing algorithms, enabling precise localization and quantitative measurement of iron content [29]. Its primary advantage lies in the fact that the final quantitative results are theoretically independent of geometry shape, echo time, or field strength, providing a more objective quantitative metric [29]. It provides a complementary strategy by directly quantifying magnetic susceptibility alterations linked to iron deposition. Nevertheless, quantitative techniques often necessitate dedicated acquisition protocols, specialized post-processing workflows, and prolonged analysis times, which may hinder their routine clinical adoption. Therefore, rather than replacing STS, QSM is expected to play a synergistic role. QSM reveals that in individuals with prodromal PD manifestations, progressively increasing nigral iron deposition can be detected as the disease advances [60]. Abnormalities in QSM are already present in the early stages of the disease. However, no significant further progression is observed between early and mid-to-late stages, suggesting that these changes may reach a “plateau” early in the disease course, making it a sensitive early diagnostic biomarker, but may not be an ideal indicator for tracking long-term disease progression [57].

Susceptibility map-weighted imaging (SMWI) and true susceptibility-weighted imaging (tSWI) are techniques that combine the advantages of both QSM and SWI. Specifically, they utilize QSM data—particularly based on the high-pass filtered phase data—to generate a susceptibility mask, replacing the traditional phase mask used in SWI [23]. These approaches effectively eliminate geometric dependency, allowing prominent visualization of veins (and iron deposits) independent of their orientation. Moreover, the interference from iron signals in NM-rich regions of the SNc is better avoided, leading to a clearer and more consistent visualization of STS [23].

For STS imaging, researchers have proposed specific optimization protocols. Compared to conventional SWI, SMWI provides higher CNR and reduces partial volume effects [17]. A study suggests that in the early diagnosis and screening of PD, SMWI combined with refined scoring systems could position STS as a highly promising first-line imaging biomarker capable of replacing PET, with a sensitivity of 94.59% and a specificity of 92.49% [61].

As for tSWI, it enables more reliable visualization of the STS via a stable and rapid (< 6 min) imaging protocol that adopts finalized optimal parameters, including a resolution of 0.67 mm × 0.67 mm × 1.34 mm, a flip angle of 9°, echo times of 11 ms and 20 ms, and an acquisition aligned with the Anterior Commissure - Posterior Commissure line. By integrating the SNR of magnitude images with the contrast of QSM, tSWI delivers images that stand as the most effective approach for STS visualization [62].

Additionally, an innovative fast-imaging technique combining Echo Planar Imaging (EPI) with SWI has successfully shifted STS detection from expensive, scarce, and time-consuming high-field MRI to the widely accessible 1.5 T magnetic field. This method achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.98 for STS detection, highlighting its significant clinical potential for promoting early and accessible diagnosis of PD [63].

Neuromelanin-sensitive MRI (NM-MRI)

NM-MRI and iron-sensitive imaging exhibit a complementary relationship, jointly confirming abnormalities in the SN from the dual perspectives of NM and iron content, providing multidimensional imaging evidence for PD diagnosis. Studies indicate that the overlaying of iron and NM boundaries helps readers make a more informed decision about the presence of STS [12]. NM signals show significant correlations with multiple MRI parameters: it shows a negative correlation with the CNR of QSM (Rho = –0.60), a positive correlation with the CNR of T2WI (Rho = 0.65), and a negative correlation with T1-weighted signal [34]. By utilizing techniques such as magnetization transfer contrast, NM-MRI produces high signal intensity in T1-weighted images within NM-rich regions, enabling early detection of NM loss [23].

Reduced volume or contrast of NM in the SN is a common finding in NM-MRI studies of PD patients. Studies have demonstrated that the NM distribution visualized by NM-MRI corresponds to the dopaminergic territory identified on PET imaging, and its signal intensity correlates with dopamine release capacity and disease severity in PD [64, 65]. However, studies have indicated that this technique achieves only 60–80% sensitivity in distinguishing PD patients from HC, performing no better than DAT [66]. In contrast, other research has reported a pooled sensitivity of 89% (95% confidence interval: 86%–92%) and a pooled specificity of 83% (95% CI: 76%–88%) for NM-MRI in diagnosing PD[67]. Therefore, the diagnostic value of NM-MRI in PD, as well as the diagnostic utility of the STS within NM-MRI, still require further exploration using larger sample sizes and more standardized, optimized scanning protocols.

Ultra-high-field strengths of 7-T MRI and above

Imaging of the N1 was first introduced at a field strength of 7 T, subsequently demonstrated at a field strength of 3 T, and came to be known as the “STS” [13, 27]. Ultra-high field MRI demonstrates significant advantages in the visualization and assessment of STS, due to its superior spatial resolution and SNR [68, 69] (Fig. 7). To standardize and improve the reproducibility of N1 assessment, a study based on 7 T has developed a probabilistic atlas of the N1 region corresponding to the STS, precisely pinpointing the most probable locations of STS [70]. In the diagnosis of PD, 7-T MRI demonstrates excellent performance in detecting the absence of the STS. Compared to the 3-T SWAN sequence, 7-T SWI achieves higher diagnostic sensitivity (7 T 93% vs 3 T 79%), specificity (7 T 100% vs 3 T 94%), and accuracy (7 T 96% vs 3 T 86%) for STS loss [71]. A meta-analysis indicates that combining 3 T and 7 T data yields a sensitivity of 97.7% and a specificity of 94.6% for diagnosing PD with STS [40]. In idiopathic RBD patients, STS loss detected on 3-T MRI increases from 63% to 87% on 7-T MRI, consistent with DAT reduction, indicating the higher potential value of 7 T in screening during the prodromal stage of the disease [72].

Furthermore, ultra-high-field imaging can better reveal the complexity of the STS. A study using 7-T SWI has shown that the STS is not necessarily present in HCs and has a false-positive rate of approximately 19% [26]. Furthermore, a large-sample 7 T study provided evidence for differential diagnosis of parkinsonism. The study indicates that, similar to PD, the majority of patients with MSA-P and PSP show absence of STS, while STS is preserved in 79.5% of those with MSA-C, which suggests that STS can help distinguish MSA subtypes. The study also revealed disease-specific spatial patterns of iron deposition: PD and PSP showed iron accumulation predominantly in the dorsal and posterior regions of the SN, MSA-P in the intermediate and posterior regions, and MSA-C in the ventral region [73]. Additionally, in CBD, another form of parkinsonism, the nigrosome structure appears normal [48].

In terms of disease progression assessment, 7-T MRI techniques—including R2* mapping, QSM, and NM imaging—have revealed correlations in PD patients between N1 abnormalities such as reduced NM signal and increased iron deposition and the severity of their motor symptoms [74]. This supports the idea that 7-T MRI can be employed to assess disease progression at the individual level [31]. Moreover, decreased 7-T T2WI signal in the N1 region correlates positively with disease severity, further validating its value in reflecting pathological progression [44].

From a technical standpoint, imaging parameter selection is significantly influenced by field strength. Higher field strengths permit shorter echo times and repetition times, providing better SNR and lower specific absorption rate compared to conventional spin-echo sequences, which makes techniques like SWI more efficient and safer at 7 T [29]. Moreover, exploration of even higher field strengths continues. A postmortem study using 9.4-T MRI did not identify significant differences in nigrosome volume or signal intensity in PD patients [75]. Another study using > 11.7-T MRI successfully visualized N1 in control samples but not in PD samples [35]. However, due to the limited sample sizes, the diagnostic value of such ultra-high-field findings requires further validation.

ML

Traditional evaluation of STS relies on visual assessment by radiologists, a process that is inherently subjective, time-consuming, and highly dependent on examiner experience. In recent years, with the advancement of AI technologies, ML-based methods for the automatic, rapid, and accurate segmentation and identification of STS have been extensively developed.

To address the lengthy processing times associated with traditional QSM reconstruction algorithms, a study has employed a deep learning network to replace conventional iterative algorithms, which has developed an efficient and rapid susceptibility imaging processing pipeline for visualizing N1 on conventional clinical 3-T MRI [76]. In the image analysis domain, models like NINet have demonstrated the ability to efficiently and accurately identify STS from multi-sequence 3-T MRI data, achieving solid diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.87) [77]. Moreover, Ariz et al proposed a fully automated atlas-based segmentation method for the N1 region. This diagnostic model achieved a high differential diagnostic rate (AUC = 0.955) and proved valuable even in advanced PD patients where the STS is completely absent [78]. Beyond simple identification and segmentation, ML can also extract quantitative imaging features that are imperceptible to human eyes. One study utilized an ML model to quantitatively analyze the relative content of NM and iron within the N1 region, effectively distinguishing idiopathic PD from HCs. This approach overcomes the subjectivity inherent in purely visual assessments, serving as a valuable complementary tool to STS visual evaluation [78, 79].

While these ML applications demonstrate significant promise, several critical steps are essential to establish their clinical credibility and generalizability before widespread adoption. First, these models require rigorous external validation on large, multi-center cohorts. Currently, most studies are based on data from a single center or a single MRI vendor or protocol, which raises concerns about model robustness and generalizability, highlighting the need for further validation across diverse scanners, acquisition protocols, and field strengths from different institutions, as well as larger sample sizes, to enhance their reliability and broader applicability. Second, to demonstrate incremental value, the focal point of research should shift from simply comparing model performance to that of radiologists, towards investigating how ML models can augment diagnostic accuracy and efficiency. Quantifying this tangible clinical utility, especially for less experienced readers, is essential for establishing their impact.

In summary, ML has permeated all stages of STS research, from image reconstruction to disease diagnosis, substantially reducing manual effort and improving efficiency. However, transitioning these promising tools from the research domain to clinical practice hinges on future work that rigorously addresses their generalizability, robustness, and added value in multi-center settings.

Integrated STS with other imaging biomarkers

Although STS alone is a highly specific marker for diagnosing PD, its diagnostic capability as a single indicator remains limited in clinical practice, particularly in early-stage or atypical cases [80]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that combining STS disappearance with other biomarkers, such as the content and volume of NM, deep gray matter iron content, and the volume and iron content of SNc, significantly enhances diagnostic accuracy for PD compared to using STS alone [20, 81–83]. Diagnostic precision in both prodromal and early-stage PD also markedly improves, with AUC values increasing from 0.891 to 0.983 [20]. Furthermore, literature reports indicate that combining STS assessment with NM MRI in differential diagnosis significantly enhances the accuracy of differentiating early PD from ET (AUC = 0.935, specificity 92%) [84]. Similarly, combined evaluation of STS and putaminal hypointensity (AUC = 0.93) aids in identifying MSA, as MSA patients often exhibit both STS disappearance and prominent putaminal hypointensity—a pattern not typically observed in PD [85].

In summary, integrating STS, a valuable morphological biomarker, with other imaging biomarkers such as the content of NM and iron represents the current frontier in PD imaging diagnostics. This multi-biomarker integration not only improves diagnostic efficacy but also lays the foundation for developing automated and standardized diagnostic workflows, which hold great promise to become an indispensable auxiliary tool in future clinical practice.

Conclusions and future perspectives

Researchers are making significant efforts to identify non-invasive, convenient, and highly diagnostic imaging biomarkers for neurodegenerative diseases such as PD. In this review, we clearly and comprehensively elucidate the anatomical and pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the appearance and disappearance of STS. Furthermore, the majority of the presented literature demonstrated that STS, as an adjunctive tool, exhibits high sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing PD from HCs, and shows potential in differentiating various forms of parkinsonism. However, some studies have questioned its diagnostic value. To evaluate its diagnostic utility more definitively, future research should incorporate larger sample sizes and conduct long-term follow-up studies. Moreover, because STS is a qualitative imaging marker based on signal intensity, its visibility can be influenced by variations in scanning protocols, including magnetic field strength and sequence type. Consequently, developing optimized and standardized scanning protocols is essential to achieve clearer visualization of the STS. Meanwhile, quantitative MRI techniques such as QSM provide a complementary strategy by directly quantifying magnetic susceptibility changes. Notably, advancements in data post-processing, such as Apart-QSM and χ-seperation, enable the separation of paramagnetic and diamagnetic substances, improving the accuracy of iron deposition assessment [86–88]. Integrating qualitative and quantitative biomarkers within a multimodal imaging framework may enhance diagnostic accuracy and offer deeper insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying PD. Furthermore, large-scale sample cohorts, standardized imaging protocols, and the adoption of quantitative techniques will also help establish a foundation for building big data environments. With technological advancements, the use of super-resolution reconstruction to enhance 3-T images to a quality comparable to that of 7-T images is a promising future research direction for the identification of STS, as it could reduce hardware requirements and save costs.
