Objective

To apply an integrated causal-inference and welfare-optimization framework to Iranian Parkinson’s disease (PD) inpatient insurance claims, estimating how resource-allocation decisions relate to recovery, discharge risk, cost and financial burden; testing whether these relationships vary across patient subgroups; and assessing whether budget-neutral reallocation could improve efficiency and equity simultaneously.

Setting

Six provinces of Iran, using de-identified administrative inpatient claims obtained from the Iran Health Insurance Organization for admissions with a primary or secondary diagnosis of PD during the 2023–2025 observation period.

Design

Retrospective claims-based cohort study. Transaction-level records (n = 21,121) were aggregated into 1,813 hospitalization episodes and analyzed through a six-stage pipeline: gradient-boosted predictive models; double machine learning and augmented inverse-probability weighting for average treatment effects, restricted to an admission-time-only, pre-treatment severity adjustment; causal forests for subgroup heterogeneity; concentration-index decomposition for socioeconomic inequality; constrained integer-linear-programming optimization for reallocation scenarios; and bootstrap, E-value and design-effect sensitivity analyses.

Results

Predictive models showed acceptable-to-strong discrimination (recovery, AUC-ROC 0.709; unsuccessful-discharge proxy, AUC-ROC 0.870) and explained 81.3% of variance in total cost. Prolonged length of stay, treated as an exploratory, within-episode exposure rather than a causal one because it is realized during the hospitalization itself, was associated with a 6.4-percentage-point lower discharge proxy and 200% higher cost (E-value limit 1.93–4.04). Higher insurance-coverage generosity, the study’s pre-treatment, policy-assignable exposure, was associated with an approximately 40% reduction in cost (E-value limit 2.19) and no significant change in the discharge proxy; effects were consistent across age, severity, hospital sector, rurality and socioeconomic rank, with an attenuated estimate in a small private-hospital subgroup. Recovery was concentrated among higher-socioeconomic episodes, while cost, out-of-pocket burden and length of stay were concentrated among lower-socioeconomic episodes; age and hospital ownership, not disease severity (which contributed only −0.8% to +6.2% of the observed gradients), were the leading correlates. A coverage-based, budget-neutral reallocation scenario reached 35.8% of the cohort within the observed budget. Ninety-eight percent of significant associations remained robust to plausible within-patient clustering, the principal limitation arising from the absence of a patient identifier in these claims data.

Conclusion

Insurance-coverage generosity showed the strongest evidence of a causal, cost-reducing effect, while socioeconomic inequity in PD hospitalization outcomes was driven primarily by administrative rather than clinical-severity factors. The reallocation scenarios illustrate model-based, budget-neutral efficiency-equity gains and should be read as decision-support for phased piloting, not as empirically demonstrated policy effects. Patient-level claims linkage and validated clinical severity measures are needed to extend this framework toward genuine longitudinal outcomes.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder worldwide and one of the fastest-growing neurological conditions in terms of absolute burden [1,2]. Global Burden of Disease estimates through 2021 document a sustained rise in age-standardized prevalence and incidence, with disability-adjusted life years intensifying disproportionately in low- and middle-income countries as mortality accelerates and population ageing compounds the epidemiological transition [1–3]. This trajectory has been characterized as an emerging “Parkinson pandemic,” driven jointly by demographic ageing and possible environmental exposures [4], a framing echoed by recent commentary describing PD as among the fastest-growing neurological conditions confronting health systems worldwide [5]. Advanced PD generates markedly higher direct and indirect costs than early-stage disease across high-income settings [6]; Medicare data confirm that motor symptoms and treatment-related adverse events independently predict greater resource use and cost [7], late-stage parkinsonism imposes the heaviest burden across European systems through formal care, hospitalization, and informal caregiving [8], and Canadian estimates place the aggregate annual burden in the billions of dollars [9].

Iran exemplifies these pressures in an accelerating form. The Iranian Parkinson’s Disease Registry reports a standardized prevalence of 223 per 100,000 across six provinces, with 70% of patients classified as late-onset PD [10], and national trend data show that Iran’s age-standardized prevalence and incidence rose sharply between 1990 and 2021, with the highest burden concentrated in East Azarbayejan province and closely tied to social development indicators [11]. These pressures are already financially acute: a substantial share of Iranian households experience catastrophic health expenditure, with a meaningful proportion impoverished by health-related costs [12]. Iran’s health insurance system remains fragmented, with equity concerns and coverage gaps for diagnostic imaging, outpatient neurology, rehabilitation, and advanced interventions such as deep brain stimulation [13]. Family caregivers absorb much of the resulting burden absent adequate long-term care [14], and although the 2014 Health Transformation Plan expanded inpatient access, chronic neurodegenerative conditions remain inadequately prioritized within a fragmented, poorly coordinated system [15].

Despite this well-documented burden, a critical knowledge gap persists regarding how insurance-level resource allocation decisions causally shape PD outcomes. Health technology assessment and cost-effectiveness analyses have confirmed the value of specific PD interventions such as deep brain stimulation [16], and personalized, nurse-led care frameworks have been proposed to better match services to need [17]; yet both rely largely on data and assumptions from high-income settings with limited transferability to LMIC contexts. Emerging evidence indicates that PD clinical care varies substantially across centers, often reflecting institutional patterns rather than individualized need, with services such as physical and mental-health therapy frequently underused until after adverse outcomes occur [18] – underscoring the need for “precision public health” approaches to optimizing the distribution of specialist care, diagnostics, and essential drugs [19]. Causal machine learning offers a promising route toward such precision: DML has been used to isolate the causal effect of anti-dementia drug policy on economic burden in related neurodegenerative populations [20], and causal-inference methods are increasingly applied within PD itself to assess treatment effects on survival and to build causally informed feature-selection pipelines for severity monitoring [21,22]. Critically, real-world insurance claims data available in Iran have not been leveraged to answer causal or equity questions system-wide, leaving policymakers without the evidence needed to guide resource reallocation.

This study addresses that gap by applying an integrated causal-inference and welfare-optimization framework to Iranian PD insurance claims data, structured across five linked stages: gradient-boosted outcome prediction; Double Machine Learning to estimate average treatment effects of length-of-stay, coverage generosity, and service intensity [23]; Causal Forests, within the generalized random forest framework, to characterize treatment-effect heterogeneity across subgroups [24,25]; concentration-index decomposition to quantify socioeconomic inequity; and constrained integer-linear-programming optimization to identify budget-neutral reallocations, with uncertainty propagated throughout via bootstrap inference and Rosenbaum-bounds sensitivity analysis. We ask three questions: how do resource-allocation decisions causally affect recovery, readmission, cost, and financial burden; do these effects vary across patient subgroups; and can budget-neutral reallocation simultaneously improve efficiency and equity?

As the literature review below details, each of these methods – DML, Causal Forests, concentration-index decomposition, and constrained optimization – is individually well established, and each has previously been applied, separately, to neurodegenerative or chronic-disease populations elsewhere. The contribution of this study is therefore not novelty at the level of individual techniques, but their integration into a single, policy-oriented analytical pipeline, applied to Iranian administrative PD insurance data. Methodologically, the study offers a replicable causal-equity-optimization framework for LMIC health systems facing comparable pressures. Empirically, it generates causal and equity evidence for a national PD population in a setting where prior analyses have been almost exclusively descriptive or predictive. Practically, it translates these estimates into budget-neutral reallocation scenarios directly relevant to insurer and public-policy decision-making in Iran and comparable middle-income contexts.

Literature review

A growing body of work has begun applying causal machine learning to chronic and neurodegenerative disease policy questions. DML addresses confounding by indication – the tendency for more severely affected patients to receive more intensive treatment – by using flexible machine-learning models to jointly estimate treatment assignment and outcome equations, isolating an orthogonalized causal effect [23]. DML was first applied in adjacent dementia research to quantify the causal effect of anti-dementia drug use on economic burden [20], and its use within PD has since expanded to causal survival analysis of dopaminergic therapy and to causal graph-based feature selection for remote severity monitoring, prioritizing features with a demonstrated causal link to progression over those merely associated with it [21,22]. Complementing average-effect estimation, Causal Forests within the generalized random forest framework recover conditional average treatment effects and characterize subgroup heterogeneity without requiring pre-specified interaction terms [24,25].

Beyond effect estimation, a parallel literature addresses how estimated effects should be translated into allocation decisions. Bi-level contextual-bandit frameworks optimize subgroup-level budget allocation under delayed feedback, explicitly modelling the lag between resource investment and observable outcomes in chronic disease management [26]. Fair reinforcement-learning frameworks embed counterfactual and group-level equity metrics directly into the reward function, so scarce resources such as diagnostic equipment or hospital beds are distributed without reproducing historical bias [27], while frameworks combining treatment-effect learning with online allocation reconcile treating patients now with continuing to learn which interventions work best [28]. In PD specifically, a cost-aware multimodal monitoring architecture assigns patients to budget-tiered assessment pathways – reserving expensive comprehensive evaluations for cases where diagnostic return justifies the expense – illustrating how constrained optimization can be embedded into clinical monitoring workflows [29]. Markov decision processes have similarly derived optimal monitoring policies under budget and adherence uncertainty [30], and two-stage conformal-prediction frameworks now provide calibrated uncertainty intervals around medication-need forecasts to guide which patients warrant closer monitoring [31].

Insurance structure is a recurrent determinant of PD care quality internationally. In South Africa, patients in the private sector report substantially higher quality of life and treatment access than public-sector users, who rely predominantly on basic medication under considerable resource constraints [32]; in Brazil’s public Sistema Único de Saúde, incomplete capture of medication use in administrative claims constrains system-level analysis despite frequent PD-related outpatient and inpatient utilization [33]. Insurance type and related administrative variables have also been shown to meaningfully predict discharge destination in nationwide cohort studies, supporting their use in multidisciplinary discharge planning and post-acute resource allocation [34]. Policy-level insurance interventions can shift outcomes at scale: the introduction of long-term care insurance in rural China has been shown, using difference-in-differences designs, to reduce medical-expenditure burden and improve subjective well-being among vulnerable older adults [35].

Equity constraints persist even where formal coverage is comparatively broad. Spatial-accessibility analyses using enhanced floating-catchment-area methods in Ontario, Canada find that geography, socioeconomic status, and demographic characteristics continue to shape utilization of PD services despite near-universal insurance [9]. In the United States, social determinants of health – including income and connectivity – are independently associated with PD prevalence and access, although observational designs limit causal interpretation of these associations [36]. Access to advanced, device-aided PD therapies remains constrained by health-system capacity and specialized surgical infrastructure, and social-return-on-investment analyses suggest that conventional cost-effectiveness frameworks systematically understate the value of expanding such access by omitting family and caregiver spillovers [37]. These international patterns closely mirror Iranian evidence on catastrophic and impoverishing health expenditure among households facing chronic illness [38], and on the concentration-index and horizontal-inequity decomposition methods – the Erreygers-corrected concentration index and Wagstaff-type decomposition – used to formally attribute socioeconomic gradients in health and payments to specific structural drivers [39–41].

Taken together, this literature establishes three points relevant to the present study. First, causal machine-learning methods – DML, Causal Forests, and their fairness- and budget-aware extensions – are individually well developed and have already been applied, separately, to neurodegenerative and chronic-disease policy questions outside Iran [20–31]. Second, formal equity-measurement techniques capable of decomposing socioeconomic gradients in health and payments are mature and have been applied extensively to catastrophic-expenditure and horizontal-inequity questions internationally and within Iran’s broader health system [38–41]. Third, and most consequentially, no identified study has combined causal effect estimation, heterogeneity analysis, formal equity decomposition, and constrained welfare optimization within a single analytical pipeline applied to real-world PD insurance claims, and none has done so for Iran, where administrative claims data – despite their availability – remain underutilized for causal or equity-focused policy analysis [10,11,32,33]. Existing PD-focused machine-learning applications in the Iranian and broader LMIC context remain largely descriptive or predictive rather than causal, and formal equity analyses of PD specifically, as distinct from chronic disease generally, remain scarce. It is this combination of methodological integration and contextual application to an underexamined, high-burden LMIC setting that the present study is designed to address.

Methods

Source of data

This study used de-identified administrative health insurance claims data obtained from the Iranian Health Insurance Organization (IHIO). The dataset comprised inpatient admissions for patients with a primary or secondary diagnosis of Parkinson’s disease across six provinces of Iran. The underlying claims covered admissions from March 6, 2023 through March 18, 2025, with the final included claim in the analytic dataset dated March 20, 2025 (the later of that episode’s admission or discharge date). Dates in the original administrative records were recorded according to the Jalali (Persian) calendar and were consistently converted to the Gregorian calendar for data processing, analysis, and reporting. The complete de-identified dataset used for the present analysis was received from IHIO on February 24, 2026 — after the close of the observation period, consistent with the claims-run-out time typically needed for administrative data to finalize. The observation period was defined according to the dates of the underlying inpatient claims and was therefore independent of the date on which the dataset was received.

The raw dataset comprised 21,121 claim transaction records, each representing a single billable service line submitted to the insurer within a hospitalization episode. Variables captured at the transaction level included patient demographics (age, sex), geographic identifiers (province), institutional characteristics (hospital ownership type), clinical information (admitting and discharging physician specialty, service group, discharge status), admission and discharge dates, and a comprehensive set of financial fields: total service cost, total amount requested from the insurer, total amount paid by the insurer, and the patient’s out-of-pocket share. Although individual patient identifiers were unavailable due to data anonymization protocols, each record contained sufficient administrative detail to reconstruct discrete hospitalization episodes, as described in the following section.

Data preprocessing and sample refinement

The raw transaction records underwent a multi-stage preprocessing pipeline to produce a clean, analytically suitable episode-level dataset. In the first stage, all column names were standardized to resolve inconsistencies arising from trailing whitespace, capitalization differences, and minor label variations across data extracts. Date fields for admission and discharge were then parsed and validated, and length of stay (LOS) was computed directly from the difference between these dates to serve as the ground-truth measure; where this derivation yielded implausible or missing values, the recorded LOS field was used as a fallback. Temporal attributes including admission year, month, day of week, and a weekend admission flag were derived from the parsed dates to support subsequent stratification.

Missing values were addressed through targeted imputation: categorical fields such as insurance fund, gender, service group, and discharge status were imputed using the within-sample mode, financial fields with missing values were assigned zero on the basis that their absence reflects no payment activity rather than data loss, and age was imputed at the sample median. All numeric fields were then coerced to appropriate data types, and a gender harmonization procedure resolved heterogeneous encodings—spanning English, Persian, and abbreviated forms—into a binary Male/Female variable. Records missing critical operational fields (LOS, total cost, or admission/discharge dates) were removed from the dataset.

Outlier treatment was applied using an IQR-based fencing procedure with a conservative 3 × multiplier, chosen to preserve clinically legitimate extreme values common in neurological inpatient care while censoring implausible data artefacts. Variables treated included LOS, age, total cost, total requested, total paid, and insured share; values falling outside computed fences were winsorized to the fence boundaries rather than deleted. Same-day discharges (LOS = 0) were flagged for sensitivity analyses. A set of logical consistency checks was then applied: records where discharge preceded admission were removed, insured share values exceeding total cost were capped at total cost, total paid values exceeding total requested by more than a 1% tolerance were capped at the requested amount, and residual negative financial values were set to zero. Exact duplicate rows were subsequently identified and removed at the transaction level.

The final and most consequential transformation was the aggregation of transaction-level records into hospitalization episodes. Because a single inpatient admission typically generates multiple claim lines—reflecting distinct service categories billed separately to the insurer—analyzing transactions as independent observations would violate the assumption of unit independence and artificially inflate the effective sample size. An episode was defined by the unique combination of province, sex, age, insurance fund, admission date, and discharge date. Within each episode, financial fields were summed to represent the total episode-level expenditure, LOS was taken as the maximum across constituent transactions, and administrative and clinical attributes were carried forward from the first transaction of each episode. Four analytically central derived variables were constructed at the episode level: coverage ratio (total paid divided by total requested, bounded between 0 and 1), patient out-of-pocket burden (the insured share), patient out-of-pocket ratio (insured share as a proportion of total cost), and cost per inpatient day (total cost divided by LOS). This aggregation reduced the dataset from 21,121 transaction records to 1,813 unique hospitalization episodes, which constitute the unit of analysis for all subsequent modeling.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study used secondary, non-identified administrative healthcare claims data obtained from the Iran Health Insurance Organization (IHIO). No direct contact, intervention, or interaction with human participants was involved. All data were fully anonymized prior to access and analysis, and the research team had no means of identifying individual patients.

Although the study did not involve human subjects in the conventional experimental sense, ethical approval was obtained due to the sensitive nature of healthcare administrative data. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the School of Public Health and Allied Medicine at Tehran University of Medical Sciences under the ethical approval code IR.TUMS.NIHR.REC.1403.015.

All analyses were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional guidelines and regulations, and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki were strictly followed throughout the study.

Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized administrative data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Tehran University of Medical Sciences National Institute for Health Research Ethics Committee.

This extensive, episode-oriented model has provided a solid foundation for accurately tracking expenses and has guided a framework for fairly distributing resources in the context of inpatient PD care in Iran.

Feature engineering.

Following episode-level aggregation, a structured feature engineering pipeline was applied to the clean dataset to construct the analytical variables required across all five modeling steps. This pipeline addressed four distinct needs: the derivation of a clinically grounded severity proxy to control for case-mix in causal models; the operationalization of three binary treatment variables representing the resource allocation decisions of primary policy interest; the construction of outcome variables suited to both predictive and causal estimation; and the generation of socioeconomic, demographic, and interaction covariates to support equity measurement and welfare optimization. To address multicollinearity, variables exhibiting perfect or near-perfect collinearity were excluded prior to modeling: patient_oop was dropped due to perfect collinearity with insured_share, while total_requested and total_paid were excluded on the basis of extreme variance inflation factors (VIF > 300), retaining total_cost, coverage_ratio, and patient_oop_ratio as non-redundant financial representations. Statistical and analytical methods

The analytical pipeline proceeded through six sequential, interdependent stages: (i) outcome prediction with gradient boosting machines (GBM), (ii) causal effect estimation via Double Machine Learning (DML), (iii) heterogeneous treatment effect (HTE) estimation via Causal Forest, (iv) socioeconomic inequity measurement using the Concentration Index (CI) and horizontal inequity (HI) decomposition, (v) constrained welfare optimisation, and (vi) multi-layer uncertainty quantification. Each stage was designed to address a distinct research objective while building directly on the outputs of the prior stage, ensuring internal coherence across the causal, equity, and normative dimensions of the study. The sequence follows the causal inference pipeline formalised by Chernozhukov et al. and extended to equity-sensitive resource allocation by van Doorslaer and Wagstaff [23,40]. All analyses were conducted in Python 3.11 using scikit-learn 1.4, XGBoost 2.0, econml 0.15, and PuLP 2.8. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 (two-sided) throughout. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied across outcomes because each outcome addresses a theoretically and clinically distinct research question (recovery, readmission, cost, OOP burden, length of stay), and within each causal model a single pre-specified primary estimand is tested; this follows the guidance of Rothman [61], who argues that applying blanket multiplicity corrections to conceptually distinct hypotheses inflates Type II error without controlling a coherent family-wise error rate. Sensitivity of all inferences is evaluated in Stage VI. Full model specifications, hyperparameters, and diagnostic procedures for every stage are reported in S1–S7 Appendix in S1 Appendix.

Stage I: Outcome prediction with gradient boosting machines.

Five patient-level outcomes were modelled: clinical recovery at discharge (Y1 length of stay (Y5). Gradient Boosting Machines implemented via XGBoost [43] were selected because they impose no parametric functional form, accommodate the outcome-specific distributional families (binomial, Tweedie, Poisson) natively without transformation, are inherently robust to the multicollinearity documented in the variance-inflation analysis, and generate the out-of-fold nuisance predictions required for the Stage II cross-fitting procedure. Hyperparameters were tuned by Bayesian search (Tree-structured Parzen Estimator, 50 trials per outcome) [62,63] within stratified five-fold cross-validation. Binary outcomes were evaluated by AUC-ROC, AUC-PR, Brier score, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow statistic, with post hoc probability calibration (isotonic for Y2, Platt scaling for Y1) [64,65] and SHAP-based feature attribution [66]. Full hyperparameter grids, calibration diagnostics, and objective-function justification are reported in S1 Appendix.

Stage II: Causal effect estimation via double machine learning.

Stage II estimated population-average treatment effects (ATEs) of three resource-allocation decisions — prolonged length of stay (T1), high coverage generosity (T2), and high service intensity (T3) — on the risk of unsuccessful discharge (Y2) and log cost (Y3), using Double Machine Learning in the Partially Linear Regression form [23,67]:

Yi=θ Ti+g(Xi)+εi,     Ti=m(Xi)+νi (1)

where θ is the causal parameter of interest and g(·), m(·) are non-parametric nuisance functions estimated by the Stage I models under five-fold cross-fitting. The ATE was recovered by the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell projection of cross-fitted residuals,

θ^=(Σi ν^i2)−1 Σi ν^i ε^i (2)

with inference via the influence-function sandwich estimator [68] (full expression in S2 Appendix in S1 Appendix in S1 Appendix, Eq S1). For the binary treatment T2, propensity scores were estimated by L2-penalised logistic regression, trimmed to [0.05, 0.95] to enforce the overlap assumption [69], and effects were estimated via Augmented Inverse Probability Weighting for double robustness [67]. DML was preferred over propensity-score matching, difference-in-differences, or instrumental-variable designs because it accommodates high-dimensional confounding without requiring a correctly specified propensity model, and because no valid instrument or repeated pre-post structure was available in this cross-sectional administrative dataset. Causal identification rests on conditional ignorability, overlap, and the stable unit treatment value assumption; overfitting guards, placebo permutation tests, and the T1/T3 threshold-robustness sweep are detailed in S2 Appendix in S1 Appendix, together with the full identification-assumptions discussion.

Stage III: Heterogeneous treatment effects via causal forest.

Stage III estimated patient-level Conditional Average Treatment Effects (CATEs),

τ(x)=E[Y(1)−Y(0)| X=x] (3)

using Causal Forest within the Generalised Random Forest framework [24,25], selected for its ability to discover treatment-effect heterogeneity across arbitrary covariate combinations without pre-specified interaction terms, and for its honest-splitting property, which yields asymptotically valid confidence intervals. The forest comprised 1,000 trees with minimum leaf size 5 [24]. Heterogeneity was formally tested using the Best Linear Predictor regression [23] (S3 Appendix in S1 Appendix, Eq S2); where out-of-bag R2 fell below a pre-specified signal-strength threshold, patient-level CATEs were replaced by pre-specified subgroup ATEs from eleven clinically defined strata, following Künzel et al. [70]. Ninety-percent bootstrap confidence intervals (300 replications) were computed following GRF convention [25]. Full forest specification, pseudo-outcome construction, and the out-of-bag decision rule are reported in S3 Appendix in S1 Appendix.

Stage IV: Equity measurement.

Stage IV quantified socioeconomic inequality using the Concentration Index (CI),

CI=(2/μ)·Cov(Yi, ri) (4)

where μ is the mean outcome and rᵢ is the fractional socioeconomic rank, computed across three SES proxies (insurance fund type, a composite SES rank, and rural/urban residence), with the Erreygers-normalised index applied to bounded binary outcomes [39] (S4 Appendix in S1 Appendix, Eq S3). The CI was preferred over the Gini coefficient because it captures both the magnitude and the socioeconomic direction of inequality [40]. Horizontal inequity (HI) — the inequality unattributable to legitimate health need — was estimated by indirect standardisation [71]:

HI=CI(Yi)−CI(Y^ineed) (5)

where Ŷᵢneed is the need-standardised expected outcome predicted from age, sex, and severity score. The Wagstaff–van Doorslaer decomposition [40] further attributed the CI to determinant-level contributions via a Linear Probability Model, chosen to preserve the exact additivity of the decomposition identity (S4 Appendix in S1 Appendix, Eq S4). All estimates carried bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap confidence intervals (1,000 replications). Full decomposition diagnostics and reliability flags are reported in S4 Appendix in S1 Appendix.

Stage V: Constrained welfare optimisation.

Stage V identified budget-neutral treatment reallocations maximising a utilitarian welfare function,

max W=Σi [α·τi(xi)·ti−λ·OOP_deltai(ti)] (6)

subject to bed-day, insurer-cost, and hard equity constraints (S5 Appendix in S1 Appendix, Eqs S5–S7) requiring that high-severity patients be treated at least as frequently as low-severity patients, consistent with the ethical framework of Williams and with the Iranian health system’s stated equity objectives [72]. Binary Integer Linear Programming, solved by CBC branch-and-bound [73], was selected over continuous relaxation or greedy heuristics because treatment decisions are inherently binary and because ILP guarantees a globally optimal solution given the global coupling the equity constraint introduces across severity strata; a greedy heuristic served as a warm-start and robustness comparison. Three policy scenarios were evaluated — observed practice, welfare-optimal allocation, and equity-maximising allocation. Welfare weights (α, λ) were set to 1 in the base case, with a 4 × 4 sensitivity grid spanning plausible societal preferences reported in S5 Appendix in S1 Appendix.

Stage VI: Uncertainty quantification.

Stage VI propagated four distinct sources of uncertainty. Causal effect estimates carried analytical 95% confidence intervals from the influence-function sandwich variance; equity indices carried BCa bootstrap confidence intervals (1,000 replications) [74]; sensitivity to unobserved confounding was assessed via Rosenbaum bounds [75], reporting the critical Γ at which a finding would be nullified by hidden bias; and welfare conclusions were propagated through 500 CATE-perturbation replications to yield 95% credible intervals for total welfare, treatment rates, and the severity-targeting ratio. Full layer-by-layer specifications, including the direction-aware Rosenbaum p-value procedure and Monte Carlo precision checks, are reported in S6 Appendix in S1 Appendix.

Identification assumptions and limitations

Causal identification in Stages II–III rests on conditional ignorability, overlap, and the stable unit treatment value assumption; these conditions, their empirical support in the administrative covariate set, and their formal robustness assessment via Rosenbaum bounds are discussed in full in S7 Appendix in S1 Appendix, together with three additional limitations concerning the normative (non-causal) status of the Stage V optimisation, the episode-level unit of analysis, and the six-province scope of the dataset.

Results

Stage 1: Predictive outcome models

Out-of-fold (OOF) discrimination and calibration were evaluated for the two binary Stage 1 outcomes, recovery (Y1) and the premature/unsuccessful discharge proxy (Y2), each trained on the full analytic sample (n = 1,813). The recovery model achieved an OOF area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) of 0.709 and an OOF average precision (AUC-PR) of 0.869 against a 76.1% positive-class prevalence, with a non-significant Hosmer–Lemeshow (HL) statistic after Platt-scaling calibration (χ2 = 6.39, p = 0.604). The discharge-risk proxy model, predicting a substantially rarer outcome (11.9% prevalence), achieved higher discrimination (AUC-ROC = 0.870) but a lower average precision (AUC-PR = 0.568), consistent with its lower base rate; isotonic, cross-validated calibration reduced its Brier score from 0.155 to 0.074, although the HL test remained formally significant (χ2 = 35.14, p < 0.001). Both binary models showed acceptable-to-strong discrimination on held-out folds, with AUC-ROC values comfortably above the 0.70 threshold conventionally regarded as informative for clinical risk models. The lower average precision obtained for Y2 reflects its markedly lower prevalence (11.9%) rather than weak model performance: average precision is mechanically bounded by the positive-class base rate, and a value of 0.568 against an 11.9% baseline represents a roughly five-fold enrichment over a non-informative classifier. The residual statistical significance of the Hosmer–Lemeshow test for Y2 after isotonic calibration is a well-recognised large-sample artefact: with n  =  1,813 and pronounced class imbalance, the HL statistic is sensitive to small deviations across probability deciles that carry negligible practical consequence, as illustrated by the substantial reduction in Brier score (0.155 to 0.074) achieved by the same calibration step. Because Y2 is constructed from same-day discharge, non-recovery and adverse-discharge indicators rather than from a linked subsequent hospital admission, it is reported throughout as an administrative discharge-risk proxy rather than as readmission, and the results below should be read as predictive validation of that discharge-risk construct rather than as evidence regarding rehospitalisation. It should also be emphasised that Stage 1 is a purely predictive exercise: the discrimination reported here establishes that the outcome constructs and engineered features carry genuine predictive signal, but does not by itself support a causal interpretation, which is addressed separately — under an explicit pre-treatment identification strategy — in Stage 2.

Stage 2: Causal effect estimation via double machine learning (DML)

Building on the descriptive and covariate-balance results presented above, Stage 2 estimated average treatment effects (ATEs) for three treatment definitions — prolonged length-of-stay (T1), high insurance coverage (T2), and high transaction intensity (T3) — using a cross-fitted, orthogonalized double machine learning (DML) estimator. T2 and T3 are administratively or contractually determined exposures (insurance coverage tier and service-intensity category) that are not mechanically related to length of stay, cost, or discharge status at all, so the reconstructed severity score removes, rather than merely attenuates, the earlier post-treatment concern for those two specifications. A genuinely clinical confounder set (e.g., diagnosis/comorbidity burden, prior utilisation, and disease-stage information captured at or before admission) was not available in the linked claims data and is flagged as a priority for future data linkage; in its absence, causal language for T2 and T3 in this manuscript should be read as effects estimated under an unconfoundedness assumption conditional on the observed covariates, not as definitively identified causal effects.

Two results should be interpreted causally with the qualification stated above, and one should not. The T1 estimates indicate a strong, precisely estimated association between prolonged length-of-stay and both a higher unsuccessful-discharge rate and substantially higher cost; the corresponding E-values (2.53 for the discharge proxy, 3.25 for cost) indicate that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both prolonged stay and the outcome by a risk ratio of roughly 2.5- to 3.2-fold, beyond what the 18 measured covariates already explain, to fully account for these associations. This is a moderately demanding bar, but it is not proof of an absent confounder, and it does not resolve the structural concern that length-of-stay, cost and discharge status are all determined during or after the hospitalisation and are plausibly driven by unmeasured clinical severity (disease stage, comorbidity burden) that is only partially captured by the severity index discussed under Table 6. Accordingly, the T1 results are reported strictly as associational throughout this manuscript and are not used to support causal claims about the effect of reducing length-of-stay. The T2 and T3 estimates, by contrast, concern administratively or contractually assigned exposures and are interpreted, conditional on the observed covariates, as causal effects: high coverage is associated with materially lower cost with no detectable change in unsuccessful discharge, while high transaction intensity shows no statistically significant effect on either outcome in its primary specification. Because the underlying claims data lack a unique patient identifier, repeated admissions from the same patient cannot be linked and may be present as apparently independent observations; the reported standard errors and confidence intervals do not incorporate within-patient clustering, and the true precision of all estimates in this table should be regarded as an upper bound pending patient-level linkage or a cluster-robust sensitivity analysis.

Prior to effect estimation, a directed acyclic graph (DAG) encoding the assumed causal structure of the study was prespecified and validated computationally rather than asserted narratively. This prespecified-DAG approach directly constrains the adjustment sets to variables that are fixed at or before the point of treatment assignment; length of stay, cost, discharge status and the components used to build the severity index are excluded from the confounder role and instead modelled as downstream, post-treatment quantities. Because prolonged length of stay (T1) is itself the treatment whose duration mechanically co-determines cost, discharge status, and any index built from them, the graph and the estimates that follow do not treat the T1 comparison as free of endogeneity; instead, this structural constraint is what allows the T1 estimates to be labelled explicitly as associational rather than causal throughout this section (Table 11), while the two exposures that are plausibly determined at or before admission (insurance-coverage tier and claims-intensity) are evaluated under the stated conditional-exchangeability assumption. The DAG check therefore documents what was adjusted for and on what basis, without overstating what the design can identify.

Because the composite severity index (severity_score) is retained in the confounder set, its empirical association with the three post-treatment utilisation quantities from which its components are conceptually related — length of stay, cost per day, and total cost — was screened independently on the full analytic sample (n = 1,813; 52 candidate columns) using a prespecified threshold of |r| = 0.30. All three correlations were small (|r| ≤ 0.078) and the gate returned an overall status of pass, with zero flagged columns (Table 10).

The E-values attached to the two estimates carried forward as causal or exploratory (T1 and T2) indicate that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both the exposure and the outcome by a risk ratio of roughly 1.3 to 4.3 (point estimate) — and no less than 1.0 to 4.0 at the confidence bound — to fully explain away the corresponding estimate; this is reported as a quantitative bound on, not a resolution of, residual confounding from the clinical variables (disease stage, comorbidity burden, medication regimen) that were not observed in these claims data. Stage 4: Socioeconomic-related inequality in discharge, cost, and financial-protection outcomes

This step is a descriptive analysis of socioeconomic-related distributional gradients — concentration indices, horizontal-inequity indices, and a linear-decomposition of each concentration index into the contributions of its correlates — computed on the same 1,813-episode analytic sample used in Step 3. Stage 6: Multi-layer uncertainty quantification

Point estimates from the causal and welfare models are subject to sampling variability, unmeasured confounding, and specification choices; each of these sources of uncertainty was therefore quantified separately, using 1,000 bootstrap replications for the causal, subgroup, and equity estimates and 500 replications for the welfare-optimisation estimates (Table 21). Throughout this section, the length-of-stay-based exposure (T1) is reported as an exploratory association rather than a causal effect, because it is derived from the same hospitalisation episode as the outcomes and no validated pre-treatment clinical severity measure (e.g., Hoehn–Yahr stage, UPDRS score) was available to support an ignorability assumption; the insurance-coverage exposure (T2) is treated as the primary causal estimand; and the continuous claims-volume exposure (T3) is reported as a secondary, descriptive comparison. The constructed outcome Y2 is labelled the Unsuccessful-Discharge Proxy throughout, to distinguish it from an observed 30-day hospital readmission, which could not be constructed from these cross-sectional claims data.

Sensitivity to within-patient dependence

No patient identifier was available in the source claims data, so the 1,813 analysed episodes could not be definitively linked to unique patients; some episodes may represent repeat admissions from the same individual, which would violate the independence assumption underlying the reported standard errors, 95% confidence intervals, and the downstream welfare optimisation. Because true patient-level clustering could not be recovered from the available fields, its potential impact was instead bounded using a design-effect framework: for an assumed average within-patient cluster size, m, and intraclass correlation, ICC, the clustered variance is approximated as DEFF ≈ 1 + (m − 1) × ICC times the naive variance (Kish, 1965). m was swept over {1.0, 1.1, 1.25, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0} and ICC over {0.00, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.30, 0.50} for every one of the 66 average treatment effects reported in the primary uncertainty-quantification analysis (6 main models plus 60 subgroup models), and the breaking-point ICC — the weakest within-patient correlation at which a given result would lose statistical significance at α = 0.05 — was recorded for each. As a loose contextual anchor only, episodes grouped on coarse demographic fields (age, sex, insurance fund, rural residence) contained an average of 3.45 episodes per group, with 88.6% of episodes falling in a group of size greater than one; because most episodes sharing only these coarse fields are almost certainly different patients rather than repeat visits by the same patient, this figure is reported strictly as an upper bound on plausible values of m, not as an estimate of the true repeat-admission rate.

Discussion

This study set out to answer three questions: how insurance-level resource-allocation decisions causally shape recovery, discharge, cost and financial-burden outcomes among hospitalized Parkinson’s disease (PD) patients in Iran; whether these effects vary across clinically and administratively defined subgroups; and whether budget-neutral reallocation could simultaneously improve efficiency and equity. Findings for each question are considered in turn, in relation to prior claims-based PD literature.

For the first question, the three candidate resource-allocation levers occupied markedly different positions on the causal-inference spectrum, a distinction that governs how each result is discussed below. Prolonged length of stay was labelled the exploratory exposure throughout: it was associated with a 6.4-percentage-point reduction in the unsuccessful-discharge proxy (average treatment effect [ATE] −0.064, 95% CI −0.086 to −0.041) and a 200% increase in total treatment cost (ATE + 1.099 on the log scale, 95% CI 1.045 to 1.154), but because length of stay is itself the treatment and is mechanically entangled with the very outcomes used to construct the ancillary severity covariate, no validated pre-treatment clinical severity measure, such as Hoehn-Yahr stage or a UPDRS score, was available to support an ignorability assumption. These estimates are therefore reported, and should be read, as descriptive associations characterizing what happened to patients who experienced longer admissions, not as the causal effect of authorizing a longer stay; the corresponding E-values (point 2.50, limit 1.93, for discharge; point 4.26, limit 4.04, for cost) indicate that a comparatively modest unmeasured confounder could account for the weaker association, reinforcing the exploratory label rather than upgrading it. High insurance-coverage generosity was the primary exposure: it was associated with an approximately 40% reduction in total cost (ATE −0.507, 95% CI −0.572 to −0.438; E-value limit 2.19), while its association with the discharge-outcome proxy was small, statistically non-significant, and consistent with no effect (ATE + 0.022, 95% CI −0.005 to 0.049). Because coverage tier is a lever insurers can set in advance of admission, and its adjustment set was validated as pre-treatment, this is the only exposure-outcome pair for which both the point estimate and interval are compatible with a genuine causal effect; even so, adjustment for this observed, pre-treatment covariate set is not equivalent to identification of the true causal effect, and residual confounding from unobserved clinical severity remains a plausible, bounded, alternative explanation. Treatment intensity, the most weakly grounded exposure, showed a similarly large association with cost (+1,155%) that is explicitly non-causal, reflecting the mechanical link between billing-transaction volume and billed cost rather than a clinical or behavioural mechanism, and is retained only for descriptive completeness.

These findings sit alongside, and extend, previous claims-based PD literature. Liu et al.’s analysis of almost 20,000 Chinese inpatient episodes identified insurance type, alongside age and sex, as a significant correlate of length of stay and hospitalization cost, consistent with the leading role that administrative case-mix, prolonged stay and service group played among the predictors retained by the cost model here [76]. The coverage-cost finding agrees closely with Zhang et al.’s Guangzhou claims analysis, in which patients covered by the more generous employee-based insurance scheme incurred substantially lower out-of-pocket shares than those on the resident-based scheme, 24.3% versus 47.9%; the concentration of out-of-pocket burden among lower-socioeconomic-status episodes reported here (concentration index C = −0.056, 95% CI −0.077 to −0.035) is the equity-focused counterpart of that mechanism [77]. Agreement across two health systems with very different financing architectures strengthens the inference that coverage generosity, rather than any single country’s institutional idiosyncrasy, is the operative lever.

Where the present results diverge from prior work, the divergence is instructive rather than contradictory. Johnson et al.’s US commercial-claims analysis found that costs escalate sharply with PD progression, from roughly US$4,072 in newly diagnosed patients to US$37,410 among institutionalized patients [78], a severity gradient the reliability-tiered decomposition here does not reproduce at the level of socioeconomic inequality: where the decomposition was classified as reliable (residual share of C below 80%), the admission-time severity score contributed only −0.8% to +6.2% of the observed concentration index for recovery, cost and length of stay, far behind age (up to +42.4% for recovery) and hospital ownership. These are not competing claims about the same quantity: Johnson et al. describe how severity drives the level of cost, whereas the decomposition here describes what drives the socioeconomic distribution of cost. The contrast clarifies that in this Iranian sample, unequal financial protection is better explained by who a patient is administratively, in terms of age and hospital sector, than by how clinically severe their episode is coded as being.

The unsuccessful-discharge proxy requires particular caution when set against the wider literature. Crowther et al. reported 172 Medicare readmissions per 1,000 PD hospitalizations, while a UK teaching-hospital cohort of 712 people with Parkinson’s disease (PwP), accounting for 1,156 admissions, reported that 35% of admissions were followed by readmission within 30 days of discharge [79,80]; because the outcome used here is constructed from same-day discharge, non-recovery and adverse-discharge indicators within a single episode and is not linked to any subsequent admission, it cannot be equated with either readmission rate, and no such comparison is drawn anywhere in this manuscript. What can be compared is discriminative performance: the discharge-risk-proxy model’s out-of-fold AUC-ROC of 0.870 is broadly comparable to, though not directly equivalent to, the 0.774–0.832 range reported for a nationwide discharge-destination and mortality model built on 281,664 admissions [81], supporting the proxy’s predictive validity while leaving its relationship to true rehospitalization an open empirical question, one that the UK cohort’s findings on dementia, fracture and pneumonia as readmission risk factors underline the importance of resolving [80].

For the second research question, subgroup treatment effects for the exploratory and primary exposures were consistent in sign and approximate magnitude across age band (−8.6 to −4.3 percentage points for length of stay on the discharge proxy; −37.4% to −42.1% for coverage on cost), severity tier, hospital sector, rurality and socioeconomic rank, with the exception of an attenuated, imprecisely estimated effect in the small private-hospital stratum (n = 100). This homogeneity is informative when read against the Ontario rurality study, which found that rural PD patients used emergency care more, and family-physician and neurologist care less, than urban patients, interpreting this pattern as evidence of inequitable upstream access [82]. The absence of strong rural-urban treatment-effect heterogeneity here is not inconsistent with the Ontario findings; it instead suggests that rural disadvantage, to the extent it exists in this sample, is more likely concentrated in whether and how patients reach inpatient care than in how they respond to length-of-stay or coverage decisions once admitted, a distinction with direct implications for where equity-oriented policy should intervene.

Finally, for the third research question, the multinational finding that hospitalization odds rise sharply with PD severity in the United States and Europe, though far more modestly in Japan [83], supports in principle the severity-sensitive logic underlying the constrained welfare-optimization framework, while cautioning against transplanting those specific odds ratios into an Iranian, insurance-administered context. Under the primary, coverage-based scenario, the optimized allocation treated an estimated 35.8% of the cohort (95% CI 34.7–36.9%; welfare index W = 290.69) with an age-75-and-over targeting ratio close to 1.0 (0.968–1.075), indicating neither systematic over- nor under-targeting of older patients relative to their share of eligible episodes; the exploratory, length-of-stay-based scenario treated a smaller 27.3% of the cohort (W = 487.34) but is not used to support any allocation recommendation. These optimization results are presented as model-based counterfactual reallocation scenarios rather than as empirically demonstrated policy effects, since provider, insurer and patient behaviour could plausibly change under an implemented reallocation in ways the estimated treatment effects cannot capture. Taken together, the three questions are answered with deliberately uneven confidence: the coverage-cost relationship is the study’s most defensible causal claim, subgroup heterogeneity is limited but real in one small stratum, and the reallocation scenarios remain a normative exercise built on that single causal claim rather than a tested intervention.

Theoretical and policy implications

Theoretically, this study contributes less at the level of any single technique than at the level of their integration. Double machine learning, causal forests, concentration-index decomposition and constrained integer-linear-programming optimization have each previously been validated separately in neurodegenerative or chronic-disease populations; assembling them into one policy-oriented pipeline, applied for the first time to Iranian administrative PD insurance data, offers a replicable template for causal-equity-optimization analysis in low- and middle-income health systems that lack the patient-level registries typical of high-income settings. A second theoretical contribution is methodological rather than substantive: the explicit three-way stratification of exposures into associational, causal-under-a-stated-assumption, and mechanically non-causal categories, together with the admission-time-only construction of the severity covariate and its complete-removal robustness check, offers other researchers working with single-timepoint administrative claims a concrete template for avoiding post-treatment adjustment bias when the treatment of interest is itself realized during the encounter being studied.

The policy implications that follow are deliberately narrower than the full set of findings, in proportion to the strength of the underlying evidence. Because coverage generosity (T2) is the only exposure supported by a causal estimate under a stated, testable identification assumption, and because it was associated with a large reduction in total cost that is consistent, in direction, with the socioeconomic concentration of out-of-pocket burden reported here and with the coverage-related financial-protection gradient reported by Zhang et al. in Guangzhou, harmonizing benefit-package generosity across Iran’s fragmented insurance funds is the recommendation best supported by this evidence, rather than a general call to expand health-system spending [77]. Because age and hospital ownership, not disease severity, were the dominant observed correlates of the socioeconomic gradient in cost, length of stay and recovery, policy attention directed at administrative and structural sources of inequity, such as tariff differences between private and public providers and age-related gaps in benefit design, is likely to be more productive than efforts framed solely around clinical severity. The budget-neutral reallocation scenarios, in which the coverage-based optimization exhausted the insurer-cost budget while using only a minority of the bed-day budget, illustrate that efficiency and equity objectives are not necessarily in tension under this framework, but, consistent with their explicitly normative construction, they should inform phased, monitored pilots rather than direct implementation, since the estimated treatment effects that underlie them could shift once providers, insurers and patients respond to a changed allocation rule.

Limitations and future research

Several limitations temper these conclusions and should guide how the findings are used. First, the analytic dataset carries no patient identifier, so multiple admissions from the same patient may have been treated as independent episodes; a dedicated design-effect sensitivity analysis showed that the great majority of significant associations remained robust across a literature-informed range of within-patient correlation, but this sweep bounds rather than resolves the risk, and patient-level linkage, if it becomes available, would allow this limitation to be addressed directly with cluster-robust inference. Second, the unsuccessful-discharge proxy (Y2) is constructed from same-day discharge, non-recovery and adverse-discharge indicators within a single episode and is not a linked, longitudinally observed thirty-day readmission; all effect estimates and equity indices involving this outcome are reported, and should be read, as evidence about discharge risk rather than about rehospitalization, and obtaining or linking subsequent-admission data is a priority for future work. Third, although the causal models were restricted to a validated set of pre-treatment covariates and the E-values attached to the coverage and length-of-stay estimates indicate that a fairly strong unmeasured confounder would be required to fully explain them away, the underlying claims data lack validated Parkinson’s disease stage, UPDRS or Hoehn-Yahr scores, comorbidity burden, medication regimens, caregiver support and finer socioeconomic detail; adjustment for observed confounding under a pre-specified, validated directed acyclic graph is not equivalent to identification of the true causal effect, and residual confounding from these unobserved clinical variables remains plausible. Fourth, the welfare-optimization scenarios are explicitly normative rather than causal: they translate estimated conditional treatment effects into counterfactual allocations under a fixed budget and welfare specification, but do not, and cannot, establish what would occur after implementation, since provider, insurer and patient behaviour could change in response to a new allocation rule. Fifth, the study is confined to a roughly two-year observation window within a single, though nationally structured, insurance system across six provinces; longer-term Taiwanese evidence suggests that a short observation window may understate the cumulative economic burden of Parkinson’s disease [84], and the private-hospital subgroup in particular was small and imprecisely estimated, warranting caution against over-interpreting its attenuated effect sizes.

These limitations point directly to a future research agenda. The most consequential next step is establishing patient-level linkage within Iranian claims data, which would simultaneously resolve the within-patient dependence problem and permit construction of a genuine thirty-day, or longer, readmission outcome to replace the discharge-risk proxy used here. A second priority is incorporating validated clinical severity measures, such as Hoehn-Yahr stage or UPDRS scores, alongside comorbidity and medication data, ideally through linkage with outpatient and pharmacy claims, to strengthen the pre-treatment confounder set and narrow the residual-confounding bounds indicated by the E-value analysis. Third, because the optimization results are scenario-based rather than validated, phased or pilot implementation of coverage-harmonization policies, with prospective monitoring of recovery, cost and discharge outcomes, is needed before the reallocations proposed here are treated as demonstrated policy effects. Finally, extending the observation window and linking inpatient episodes with long-term and outpatient care, as has been done in Taiwan and Luxembourg would allow the substantial inpatient-cost burden identified here to be situated within the fuller lifetime economic burden of Parkinson’s disease in Iran.

Conclusion

This study provides the first causal-inference and equity evidence for hospitalized Parkinson’s disease care within Iran’s fragmented insurance system, distinguishing carefully among an associational length-of-stay pattern, a causal coverage-cost effect estimated under a stated pre-treatment identification strategy, and a mechanical, non-causal treatment-intensity association. Its central substantive finding, that higher insurance-coverage generosity causally reduces total cost and that socioeconomic inequity in cost, out-of-pocket burden and length of stay is driven primarily by administrative factors such as age and hospital ownership rather than by clinical severity, offers a more actionable target for reform than severity alone would suggest. The novelty of the work lies less in any individual causal or equity method than in combining them, together with constrained optimization, into a single replicable pipeline for LMIC insurance systems facing comparable pressures. Because the reallocation scenarios derived from this pipeline are explicitly normative and rest on a discharge-risk proxy rather than observed readmission, their value at this stage is as a decision-support tool for phased, monitored piloting of benefit-package harmonization, not as a validated policy prescription. Realizing the framework’s full potential will depend on the specific extensions identified above, patient-level linkage, validated clinical severity data, and genuine longitudinal readmission outcomes, which together would allow future work to move from bounded, assumption-dependent causal estimates toward more definitive evidence on how resource allocation shapes recovery, equity and cost for Parkinson’s disease patients in Iran and comparable middle-income health systems.
