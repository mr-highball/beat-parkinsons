PRISMA 2020 systematic review of 10 independent human RCTs (11 reports) evaluating orally administered olive-derived interventions on exercise performance, fatigue, recovery, and muscle function. Only two tiny acute crossover pilots tested actual EVOO (25 mL: Esquius 2019 n=7; Esquius 2021 n=3), neither showing improved fatigue or exercise duration. Perceived fatigue reductions occurred only with isolated olive-fruit maslinic acid (Shirai 2023, n=12) or hydroxytyrosol olive-fruit-water phytocomplex (Roberts 2022/2023, n=29), whereas oleuropein olive-leaf extract (Somerville 2019, n=32) had unclear fatigue and possibly harmful soreness. Authors explicitly warn olive-derived preparations are chemically distinct and cannot be generalized.

[2. Materials and Methods] This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement [23]. The literature-search process was additionally reported according to the PRISMA-S extension for reporting searches in systematic reviews [24]. Because quantitative pooling was not considered methodologically appropriate owing to clinical and methodological heterogeneity, the synthesis was reported according to the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) guideline [25]. This systematic review was not prospectively registered, and no formal review protocol was published. Eligibility criteria were defined a priori according to the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, and Study design (PICOS) framework. Eligible studies included healthy human participants, including competitive athletes, recreationally active individuals, and other healthy participants undergoing structured exercise, physical training, or standardized assessments of physical or skeletal-muscle function. Studies conducted in participants recruited because of diagnosed metabolic, cardiovascular, inflammatory, neuromuscular, or other clinical conditions, or as part of a rehabilitation program, were excluded. Healthy older adults were eligible when the study context was exercise, physical function, or training adaptation rather than disease treatment. Eligible interventions comprised orally administered olive-derived nutritional products or bioactive compounds from Olea europaea L., including extra virgin olive oil, phenolic-rich olive-oil preparations, olive-fruit or olive-leaf extracts, hydroxytyrosol- or oleuropein-rich preparations, maslinic acid, and other clearly characterized olive-derived phenolic or triterpenoid compounds. [3. Results] The database and trial-registry searches identified 1147 records, comprising 1126 records from bibliographic databases and 21 records from trial registries. After removal of 367 duplicate records and 4 records removed for other reasons, 776 records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 714 records were excluded, and 62 reports were sought for retrieval. Two reports could not be retrieved, leaving 60 reports for full-text eligibility assessment, of which 52 were excluded. Overall, 11 reports corresponding to 10 independent randomized controlled trials met the predefined eligibility criteria and were included in the systematic review. The difference reflects two publications Roberts et al. [13,26] originating from the same trial (NCT04959006), which were treated as a single independent study. Study populations included competitive and recreational athletes, physically active adults, and healthy older individuals. Discussion] This systematic review synthesized evidence from 10 independent randomized controlled trials of olive-derived nutritional interventions across exercise performance, fatigue, recovery, skeletal-muscle outcomes, and exercise-related physiological responses. The principal finding is that these interventions have not consistently improved conventional measures of exercise performance. Favorable responses were observed for selected physiological, perceptual, muscular, and biochemical outcomes, but their direction and magnitude varied across formulations, doses, populations, and exercise settings. These findings require an intervention- and outcome-specific interpretation rather than suggesting a common ergogenic effect of olive-derived products. In particular, changes in soreness, perceived exertion, or biochemical markers should be distinguished from outcomes that demonstrate improved sport-specific performance or sustained training capacity.
