ABSTRACT

Dopamine (DA) homeostasis is crucial for several relevant body functions, including cognition. Diabetes mellitus (DM) is characterized by both cognitive decline and dopaminergic dysfunction. Methylglyoxal (MGO), a reactive neurotoxic dicarbonyl, which accumulates in DM, induces dopaminergic dysfunction, contributing to DA depletion, and is associated with cognitive deficit. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying MGO impact on dopaminergic function are still unknown. This study aims to clarify how MGO damages the dopaminergic system, analyzing the contribution of miRNAs in its deleterious effect in SH‐SY5Y cells. We found that treatment with MGO significantly reduces the intracellular DA content, increasing the expression of proteins known to negatively affect DA amount, such as COMT (Catechol‐O‐methyltransferase), MAO (Monoamine oxidase), and α‐Syn (α‐synuclein), encoded by the SNCA gene. This was paralleled by a significant reduction in the expression of miR‐190a and miR‐214, known to be regulated by MGO in other cellular models. We found, by TARGETSCAN analysis, that these two miRNAs are predicted regulators of COMT and SNCA. Gain‐ and loss‐of‐function experiments highlighted that these miRNAs are able to modulate the expression of these proteins. We also showed that COMT is a direct target of both miR‐190a and miR‐214, while SNCA is a direct target of miR‐190a and an indirect target for miR‐214. Importantly, the transfection of miR‐190a and miR‐214 specific mimics reverted MGO effects on COMT and α‐Syn expression and restored DA intracellular content. Thus, these miRNAs could represent innovative pharmacological targets for the treatment of DM‐associated dopaminergic dysfunction and cognitive decline.

In SH‐SY5Y cells, Methylglyoxal (MGO) reduces the intracellular dopamine content, up‐regulating the expression of proteins that modulate dopamine amount (MAO‐B, COMT, and α‐Syn). The MGO‐induced increase of COMT and α‐Syn expression is mediated by the reduction of miR‐214 and miR‐190a levels. Accordingly, the overexpression of these two miRNAs mitigates MGO effect on dopamine content.

1 Introduction

Global life expectancy drastically soared in the last century and is expected to continue like this in the coming years. Due to the increasingly aged population, cognitive impairment, intended as a decline in thinking, memory, concentration, and other brain functions, became a major public health issue [1]. It is known for at least 30 years that altered homeostasis of neurotransmitters such as acetylcholine, noradrenaline, serotonin, GABA, and dopamine (DA) largely contribute to the onset of cognitive dysfunction [2, 3]. In particular, several alterations of the dopaminergic system, including a reduction of DA receptors and transporters density [4, 5, 6, 7] occur during aging‐associated cognitive dysfunction [8]. Indeed, in healthy subjects, an age‐related decline in striatal DA transporter (DAT) density and a loss of D2 receptors in both striatal and extrastriatal areas were observed in parallel with a worse performance on several tasks [9, 10] and simultaneous cognitive deficits [11, 12, 13]. An increased DA catabolism [14] and impairment of DA synthesis [15] were observed too. Thus, it is likely that dopaminergic dysfunction could represent the missing link between aging and cognitive deficit [16]. Moreover, several pathologies, such as schizophrenia [17, 18], attention deficit hyperactivity disorder [19, 20], Alzheimer's disease [21], autism [22], Huntington's chorea [23] and Parkinson disease (PD) concurrently feature dopaminergic dysfunction [24] and alterations of several cognitive domains. Accordingly, imaging studies highlighted a positive correlation between decreased DA amount and cognitive dysfunction in PD patients [25, 26, 27, 28], further supporting DA relevance for cognitive function. Finally, experimental manipulation of the dopaminergic system in rodents [29, 30, 31, 32] and monkeys [33, 34], pharmacological studies in humans with DA agonist and antagonist [35, 36] and the finding that dopaminergic polymorphisms impinge on specific cognitive domains definitely addressed this issue [37, 38]. Interestingly, recent studies have found that both dopaminergic dysfunction and cognitive decline also characterize Diabetes Mellitus (DM), a chronic metabolic disorder [39]. Indeed, anomalies of dopaminergic system often coexist with cognitive deficit in animal models of DM [40, 41]. More in detail, several authors found alterations of DA levels in specific brain regions of alloxan‐ or streptozotocin (STZ) rats [42]. The reduced DA amount in the hippocampus of STZ rats is paralleled by an altered expression of DRD1 and DRD2 and plays a role in the onset of cognitive deficit [43]. Moreover, in the striatum of STZ rats and of genetically diabetic db/db mice, was found a significant decrease in the expression of proteins that regulate DA's neurotransmission and stimulus‐dependent striatal DA release, such as DAT, VMAT2 (vesicular monoamine transporter 2), and Girk2 (G protein‐activated inward rectifier potassium channel 2) [44]. The amount of proteins involved in the synthesis and degradation of DA, such as Tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) [45, 46], Monoamine oxidase (MAO) [47], Catechol‐O‐methyltransferase (COMT) [48, 49, 50], and α‐synuclein (α‐Syn) [51], appears also altered in DM. Human studies further confirmed the harmful effect of DM on the dopaminergic system and cognitive function, highlighting that 20%–70% of patients with DM suffer from cognitive deficit and evidencing the association between DM and an increased risk of dementia [52] and of less serious cognitive alterations [53]. Finally, Pagano and coworkers showed that DM was accompanied by increased levels of proteins involved in neurodegeneration, such as tau and α‐Syn in cerebrospinal fluid and by lower striatal DAT [51]. It is also known that chronic hyperglycemia, characteristic of DM, negatively affects the dopaminergic system, leading to dopaminergic dysfunction and likely playing a key role in the onset of cognitive deficits. In particular, chronic hyperglycemia state leads to glucotoxicity, a deleterious condition characterized by an abnormally increased amount of MGO, a highly reactive dicarbonyl, generated endogenously from glycolytic pathways [54]. Interestingly, MGO damages dopaminergic neurons' homeostasis, decreasing their survival, and is involved in cognitive dysfunction; indeed, in murine models, MGO treatment induces cognitive decline and reduces DA levels [55, 56], while in humans, increased MGO levels in serum are paralleled by cognitive decline [57]. Thus, once established the relevance of DA transmission in cognitive function, the hypothesis that alterations of the dopaminergic homeostasis observed in DM could be induced by glucotoxicity and responsible for the pathogenesis of cognitive impairment associated to DM is appealing. However, the harmful effects of MGO on dopaminergic homeostasis probably involve several molecular mechanisms that still remain obscure. The aim of the present work is to clarify whether and how MGO could affect dopaminergic system using as cellular model SH‐SY5Y cells. These cells, when differentiated with retinoic acid and TPA (12‐O‐tetradecanoylphorbol‐13‐acetate), express TH, DAT, and DA receptors and feature the ability to synthesize DA, thus acquiring a phenotype strongly resembling central dopaminergic neurons [58].

2 Material and Methods

2.1 Reagents

Media, sera and antibiotics for cell culture were from Thermo Fisher (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; 10270‐106). Protein electrophoresis and Western blot reagents were from Bio‐Rad (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA; 5000001) and ECL reagents LiteAblot PLUS kit was from Euroclone (Milan, Italy, #EMP011005). MGO (40% in water) was from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA, #M0252). The primary antibodies used were: anti‐Carboxymethyl Lysine antibody (Abcam, Trumpington, Cambridge, UK #27684), anti‐MGO (Abcam, Trumpington, Cambridge, UK #243074), DDC (DDC D6N8N, Cell Signaling, Leiden, Netherlands, #13561); TH (monoclonal anti‐Tyrosine hydroxylase clone TH‐2, Sigma Aldrich #T1299); COMT (COMT D4N6M, Cell Signaling #14368S); α‐Synuclein (α‐Synuclein Antibody, Cell Signaling #2642S); MAO‐B (MAO‐B D‐6, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, Texas, USA, #sc515354); β‐actin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc47778); vinculin (7F9, Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc73614); secondary antibodies were: anti‐mouse (Goat anti Mouse IgG (H/L): HRP, Bio‐Rad #STAR117P); anti‐rabbit (Goat anti Rabbit IgG (H/L):HRP, Bio‐Rad #STAR124P). SuperScript III was from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, California, USA, #12574026) and QIAzol Lysis Reagent were from QIagen (Hilden, Germany, #79306). SYBR Green Supermix was from Bio‐Rad (Hercules, California, USA, #1725124). All other chemicals were from Sigma (St Louis, MO, USA).

2.2 Cell Culture

SH‐SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells (ATCC CRL‐2266) come from the SK‐N‐SH cell line, derived from the bone marrow biopsy of a 4‐year‐old girl with neuroblastoma [58]. Cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% (v/v) CO2 in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM), with the addition of 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM L‐glutamine, 0.1 mM non‐essential amino acids, 100 U/mL penicillin, 50 μg/mL streptomycin, 1.5 μg/mL amphotericin B (Sigma‐Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA). Cells were differentiated with 10 μM retinoic acid (RA) and 80 nM TPA (12‐O‐tetradecanoylphorbol‐13‐acetate) for 6 days and treated with 400 μM MGO for 48 h. RA, TPA, and MGO were from Sigma Aldrich (Saint Louis, Missouri, USA), product number: RA #R2625; TPA #P8139; MGO #M0252.

2.3 Sulforhodamine B Assay

SH‐SY5Y cells were seeded into wells of a 96‐plate at a mean density of 10 000 cell/well and differentiated as described above. Then, cells were treated for 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h with 200 μM, 400 μM, and 600 μM MGO, and cold TCA (50% in H2O) was added. Plates were stirred for 1 h at 4°C, washed three times with distilled water, and 50 μL of Sulforhodamine B 0.4% in 1% Acetic Acid was added to each well. Upon 30 min incubation at room temperature and three washes with 1% acetic acid, plates were left to dry overnight. Finally, 100 μL of 10 mM Tris–HCl at pH 7.5 was added in each well, and the plate was stirred for 30 min. Reading was performed with the Glomax reader at an absorbance of 495 nm.

2.4 Cell Transfection

To modulate miR‐190a and miR‐214 levels, SH‐SY5Y were seeded in 60‐mm plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well, differentiated and transiently transfected with specific mimics and inhibitors. Concentration used were as follows: 5 nM of mimic specific for miR‐190a (miRIDIAN miR‐190a Mimic from Dharmacon, Lafayette, CO, USA) and 50 nM of inhibitor specific for miR‐190a (miRIDIAN miR‐190a Hairpin Inhibitor from Dharmacon, Lafayette, CO, USA); 50 nM of mimic specific for miR‐214 (miRIDIAN miR‐214 Mimic from Dharmacon, Lafayette, CO, USA) and 5 nM of inhibitor specific for miR‐214 (miRIDIAN miR‐214 Hairpin Inhibitor from Dharmacon, Lafayette, CO, USA). The miRIDIAN microRNA Mimic Negative Control #1 and miRIDIAN microRNA Hairpin Inhibitor Negative Control #1 (Dharmacon, Lafayette, CO, USA) were transfected in parallel and used as mimic and inhibitor negative control, respectively. Transient transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions.

2.5 Western Blot Assay

RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4; 150 mM NaCl; 1 mM EDTA; 1 mM EGTA; 1% NP‐40; 0.5% deoxycholic acid C24H40O4; 0.1% SDS; 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonylfluoride and 10 μg/mL aprotinin) was used to solubilize SH‐SY5Y cells. Cell lysates were subjected to thermal shock (30 min at 4°C) followed by 2 h at −80°C and then clarified by centrifugation at 13 200 rpm for 20 min. Protein concentration in the supernatant was assayed using the Bradford color assay. 50 μg of cell lysates were loaded onto SDS‐PAGE and then transferred to 0.45 μm Immobilon‐P PVDF membranes (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). Membranes were incubated with primary and secondary antibodies, and immunoreactive bands were detected by chemiluminescence. ImageJ software was used to perform densitometric analysis.

2.6 RNA Isolation and Quantification

QIAzol Lysis Reagent (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used to isolate total RNA from SH‐SY5Y cells following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration was measured using a MN‐913A MaestroNano Pro spectrophotometer (Maestrogen Inc., Hsinchu City, Taiwan).

2.7 Reverse Transcription

Total RNA (1 μg) was reverse transcribed in cDNA using SuperScript III First‐Strand Synthesis System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) in a Biometra T3000 Thermocycler, according to the manufacturer's instructions.

2.8 Real Time qPCR

cDNA (10 ng for reaction) was analyzed using the iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. All reactions were performed in triplicate in a CFX Connect Real‐Time PCR System (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). HRPT1, 18S rRNA, and β‐actin were used as internal controls. 2.9 Da Elisa

SH‐SY5Y cells were harvested in resuspension buffer (1 mM EDTA, Sodium Metabisulfite 5 mM, PBS 1×) and sonicated. Intracellular DA was measured using the competitive ELISA (enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay) kit supplied by LDN diagnostics (LDN Labor Diagnostika Nord GmbH & Co, Nordhorn, Germany, BA E‐5300R) following the manufacturer's instructions. The results were analyzed by a GloMax reader at an absorbance of 450 nm and the DA amount (ng/mL) was calculated by interpolation based on a standard curve obtained using known concentrations of DA supplied by the kit. Values were normalized to the corresponding total protein content and shown as a percentage of control.

2.10 Bioinformatics for miRNA Target Prediction

miRNA binding site predictions for annotated human 3′‐UTRs were obtained from TargetScanHuman (http://www.targetscan.org). The predictions were based on site type and contextual features.

2.11 RNA Immunoprecipitation (RIP)

RIP assays were conducted using the miRNA Target IP Kit (Active Motif, Carlsbad, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. In brief, the pan‐AGO antibody or mouse IgG control was conjugated to the protein G beads after pre‐incubation with a non‐specific protein like bovine serum albumin to remove background. The cell extracts were incubated with the antibody‐immobilized protein G beads at 4°C for 8 h. The eluted samples were subjected to proteinase K treatment to facilitate the release of proteins from RNA, followed by RNA isolation. The isolated RNA was processed for RT‐qPCR analysis, as described earlier.

2.12 Statistical Procedures

All data, except where differently indicated, are expressed as a percentage relative to their respective internal controls, set to 100% in each individual experiment. Each experiment was performed at least three times and data are shown as the mean ± SD where applicable. Differences were evaluated using one‐way ANOVA for 3‐group comparisons and t‐tests for 2‐group comparisons. Statistical significance was determined (*p ≤ 0.05; **p ≤ 0.01; ***p ≤ 0.001). The probability of p < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

3 Results

3.1 MGO Effects on SH‐SY5Y Cells

MGO is able to induce cytotoxicity and then apoptosis in several cell types, including hippocampal neurons [59, 60]. Accordingly, it has been shown that the treatment with high MGO concentrations negatively affects cell viability of SH‐SY5Y cells [61, 62]. Thus, MGO toxicity in SH‐SY5Y cells was firstly estimated, performing time‐course and dose–response curves. To this aim, cells were incubated for different times (24 h, 48 h, 72 h) with different concentrations (200–400–600 μM) of MGO and cell viability was assessed by sulforhodamine B assay. The higher MGO concentration (600 μM) is toxic already upon 24 h and then induces a significant decrease of 30% in cell viability upon 48 h and 72 h. Upon 72 h, 200 μM and 400 μM MGO concentrations significantly reduce cell viability by 20% also. In contrast, these concentrations do not affect cell viability upon either 24 h or 48 h (Figure 1a). Thus, all the experiments were performed using 400 μM MGO concentration for 48 h, which appears as non‐toxic in differentiated SH‐SY5Y cells. MGO reacts with intracellular proteins leading to the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), responsible for cell and tissue damage. It is well known that MGO‐ and AGEs‐induced damage is a major player in the pathophysiology of long‐term complications of diabetes [63]. Thus, the amount of Nε‐(Carboxymethyl) lysine (CML) and arg‐pyrimidine (Arg‐Pyr), two AGEs which represent typical markers of MGO binding to intracellular proteins [64, 65], was evaluated in SH‐SY5Y cells upon incubation with MGO. Interestingly, the levels of CML (Figure 1b) and Arg‐Pyr (Figure 1c) adducts increase after the treatment with 400 μM MGO for 48 h, evidencing MGO‐induced protein modification in SH‐SY5Y cells.

3.2 MGO Effect on Intracellular DA Content

Subsequently, the MGO capability to affect dopaminergic function was evaluated. To this aim, cells were incubated with MGO 400 μM for 48 h and the intracellular DA content was assessed by ELISA assay. Interestingly, treatment with MGO significantly decreases the intracellular DA content by 57% compared to untreated control cells, evidencing that MGO is able to induce dopaminergic dysfunction (Figure 1d). However, MGO treatment does not modify the DA secretory ability of SH‐SY5Y cells, as shown in Figure S1.

3.3 MGO Action on the Expression of Dopaminergic Genes

The reduction of DA content observed in MGO treated SH‐SY5Y cells could be the result of MGO induced changes in the expression of genes involved in DA metabolism. To verify this hypothesis, both mRNA and protein levels of enzymes responsible for DA synthesis, such as TH and L‐DOPA decarboxylase (DDC), were firstly evaluated in SH‐SY5Y cells treated for 48 h with 400 μM MGO. As shown in Figure 2, no significant changes were observed in either mRNA (Figure 2a) or protein (Figure 2b) levels of TH compared with untreated controls. Similarly, incubation with MGO did not significantly modify gene (Figure 2c) and protein (Figure 2d) expression of DDC when compared with untreated cells. Therefore, the expression of the enzymes involved in DA synthesis was unmodified by MGO in SH‐SY5Y cells. However, MGO could affect TH and DDC function, inducing the formation of CML and Arg‐Pyr adducts. To explore this hypothesis, SH‐SY5Y cells were treated for 48 h with 400 μM MGO. Cell lysates were then immunoprecipitated with anti‐TH or anti‐DDC antibodies and analyzed by Western blot with anti‐CML and anti‐Arg‐Pyr antibodies. As shown in Figure S2, MGO treatment did not modify CML and Arg‐Pyr adducts formation on TH (Figure S2a,b) and DDC (Figure S2c,d). Then, MGO action on DA catabolism was investigated, evaluating the expression levels of genes involved in DA degradation, such as MAO‐B and COMT. In contrast, the incubation with 400 μM MGO for 48 h enhanced the expression of MAO‐B in SH‐SY5Y cells. In particular, MAO‐B mRNA and protein levels were increased by 34% (Figure 3a) and up to 97% (Figure 3b), respectively, compared to control cells. Similarly, mRNA and protein levels of COMT, the enzyme catalyzing the metabolic breakdown of catecholamines, were also augmented. Indeed, upon MGO treatment, a significant 38% increase was observed in COMT mRNA levels (Figure 3c), accompanied by an even more marked increment in COMT protein levels of about 70% (Figure 3d), compared to the untreated control. In addition, expression levels of SNCA, encoding the soluble protein α‐synuclein (α‐Syn), were also evaluated in SH‐SY5Y cells treated with 400 μM MGO for 48 h. This protein forms insoluble aggregates involved in various neurodegenerative diseases, including PD [66]. In this context, α‐Syn is particularly interesting since it is increased in the cerebrospinal fluid of DM patients [51] and has been shown to negatively modulate DA amount [67, 68, 69]. Importantly, in SH‐SY5Y cells the incubation with 400 μM MGO for 48 h significantly enhanced SNCA mRNA and α‐Syn protein levels by about 38% (Figure 3e) and about 80% (Figure 3f), respectively.

3.4 Modulation of miRNAs Expression by MGO

In order to clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying the MGO induced increase in the expression of the proteins involved in DA degradation in SH‐SY5Y cells, attention was focused on specific miRNAs [70, 71] known to be regulated by MGO. For instance, miR‐214 and miR‐190a are known to be downregulated in mouse aortic endothelial cells exposed to MGO [72, 73]. Importantly, using TargetScan [74] for the prediction of miR‐190a (Figure 4a) and miR‐214 (Figure 4b) targets, both COMT and SNCA were here identified as targets, considering that repetitive binding sites for the same miRNA can result in a higher regulatory efficiency. Thus, the levels of these two miRNAs were evaluated in SH‐SY5Y cells treated for 48 h with 400 μM MGO. As shown in Figure 4, significant reductions of 27% and 33% were observed in miR‐214 (Figure 4c) and miR‐190a (Figure 4d) expression levels, respectively, in SH‐SY5Y cells treated for 48 h with 400 μM MGO, compared to untreated controls.

3.5 Effect of miR‐214 on α‐Syn and COMT Expression

In order to block miR‐214 activity and to study loss‐of‐function effects, a miRIDIAN Hairpin Inhibitor specific for miR‐214 was transfected in SH‐SY5Y. In parallel, a non‐targeting inhibitor was also transfected as a control inhibitor (Ctrl inhibitor). The reduction of miR‐214 levels significantly enhanced α‐Syn protein amount of about 70% compared to the control inhibitor (Figure 5a). Similarly, miR‐214 inhibitor increased COMT protein expression of about 60% in comparison with the control inhibitor (Figure 5b). In order to increase miR‐214 levels and to investigate gain‐of‐function effects, SH‐SY5Y cells were transfected with a miRIDIAN miRNA mimic specific for miR‐214, a double‐stranded RNA oligonucleotide able to mimic the function of the endogenous miRNA. In parallel, a non‐targeting control mimic oligonucleotide (Ctrl mimic) was transfected as control. The overexpression of miR‐214 significantly decreased α‐Syn protein amount of 45% compared to the cells transfected with the control mimic (Figure 5c). Similarly, miR‐214 mimic reduced COMT protein expression of about 40% compared to the cells transfected with the control mimic, as shown in Figure 5d. Thus, an inverse correlation exists between miR‐214 levels and both α‐Syn and COMT protein expression, confirming the known regulation of α‐Syn by miR‐214 [75] in SH‐SY5Y cells and supporting the bioinformatic target prediction for α‐Syn and COMT.

3.6 Effect of miR‐190a on α‐Syn and COMT Expression

A miRIDIAN Hairpin Inhibitor specific for miR‐190a or a non‐targeting control inhibitor was also transfected in SH‐SY5Y cells. The consequent inhibition of miR‐190a enhanced α‐Syn protein amount of about 90% compared to the control inhibitor (Ctrl inhibitor) (Figure 6a) and COMT protein levels of about 80% (Figure 6b). Then, a miRIDIAN miRNA Mimic specific for miR‐190a or a non‐targeting control mimic (Ctrl mimic) was also transfected in SH‐SY5Y cells. The overexpression of miR‐190a significantly reduced α‐Syn and COMT protein amount of about 55% (Figure 6c) and 50% (Figure 6d), respectively, compared to the cells transfected with the control mimic. Thus, these results suggest that miR‐190a levels are inversely correlated to both α‐Syn and COMT protein expression, suggesting α‐Syn and COMT as miR‐190a targets.

3.7 Direct In Vivo Binding of miR‐214 and miR‐190a With SNCA and COMT mRNAs

To assess whether miR‐214 directly binds to SNCA and COMT mRNAs, RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) experiments were performed using an antibody against AGO2, a major component of the RNA‐induced Silencing Complex (RISC), and then analyzing the RNA associated with RISC. SH‐SY5Y cells were transfected with the miRIDIAN miRNA mimic specific for miR‐214 or with the control mimic (Ctrl mimic). A significant increase was observed in the enrichment of the COMT transcript pulled down by AGO2, but not in the enrichment of the SNCA transcript, when miR‐214 was overexpressed (Figure 7a). ATG5 [76] and RUNX3 [77], which are known targets of miR‐214, were used as positive controls. HPRT1, a common housekeeping gene not targeted by miR‐214, was instead used as a negative control (Figure 7a). To validate SNCA and COMT as miR‐190a targets, RIP experiments were performed after transfection of SH‐SY5Y cells with the miRIDIAN miRNA Mimic specific for miR‐190a or the control mimic (Ctrl mimic). The overexpression of miR‐190a increased the enrichment of both the COMT and SNCA transcripts in RISC, in addition to the previously demonstrated miR‐190a target KLF6 [78], used as positive control. HPRT1, that is not targeted by miR‐190, was used again as a negative control (Figure 7b). These findings provide first evidence that COMT is a direct target of miR‐214, while SNCA appears not to be directly regulated by miR‐214. Furthermore, both COMT and α‐Syn are direct targets of miR‐190.

3.8 Effect of miR‐214 and miR‐190a on Intracellular DA Content

Finally, given the modulatory action of both miR‐214 and miR‐190a on α‐Syn and COMT protein expression, the functional effect of these miRNAs on intracellular DA amount was investigated upon transfection of specific miRIDIAN Hairpin Inhibitors or miRIDIAN miRNA mimics in SH‐SY5Y cells. Intriguingly, the inhibition of miR‐214 significantly decreased the intracellular DA content by 40% compared to the inhibitor control (Ctrl inhibitor). Similarly, a reduction in the intracellular DA content of about 32% was observed upon transfection of miR‐190a inhibitor, as shown in Figure 8a. In contrast, the overexpression of either miR‐214 or of miR‐190a significantly increased the intracellular DA content by 25% and by 53%, respectively, compared to the mimic control (Ctrl mimic) (Figure 8b).

3.9 Ability of miR‐214 and miR‐190a to Revert MGO Effects on DA Homeostasis

In order to demonstrate that miR‐214 and miR‐190a are effectively involved in the deleterious action of MGO on the dopaminergic function, the ability of miR‐214 and miR‐190a to revert MGO effects in SH‐SY5Y cells was investigated. To this aim, SH‐SY5Y cells were transiently transfected with the miRIDIAN miRNA mimic specific for miR‐214 or for miR‐190a and treated with 400 μM MGO for 48 h. Then, α‐Syn and COMT proteins expression was evaluated by Western blot. As shown in Figure 9, MGO increases α‐Syn (a) and COMT (b) levels by 60% and by 104% respectively. Interestingly, the transfection with the miRIDIAN miRNA mimic specific for miR‐214 decreases α‐Syn and COMT protein expression upon MGO treatment by 70% and by 75% respectively, compared to cells transfected with the control mimic (Ctrl mimic). Similar results were obtained when SH‐SY5Y cells were transfected with the mimic specific for miR‐190a and then incubated with MGO. Indeed, as shown in Figure 9, the transfection of miR‐190a mimic reduces α‐Syn (c) and COMT (d) protein expression in MGO treated SH‐SY5Y cells by 48% and by 80% respectively, compared to cells transfected with the control mimic (Ctrl mimic). Then, the ability of miR‐214 and miR‐190a mimics to mitigate MGO action on intracellular DA content was investigated. To this aim, SH‐SY5Y cells were transfected with these specific mimics or with a control mimic (Ctrl mimic) and intracellular DA amount was evaluated by ELISA, upon 48 h incubation with 400 μM MGO. As expected, in SH‐SY5Y cells transfected with control mimic, MGO treatment decreased DA levels by 54% compared with untreated cells. Interestingly, in MGO treated cells, the transfection of miR1‐90a and miR‐214 mimics reverted MGO effect, increasing DA amount by 39% and 35% respectively, compared to cells transfected with control mimic (Figure 10). Thus, the transfection of miR‐214 and miR‐190a mimics strongly attenuates MGO effects on α‐Syn and COMT protein expression and on DA content, providing the first evidence that the downregulation of miR‐214 and miR‐190a is crucial for MGO deleterious action on DA homeostasis in SH‐SY5Y cells. Intriguingly, the transfection of the miR‐190a and miR‐214 mimics does not significantly modify the profile of CML and Arg‐Pyr adducts in SH‐SY5Y cells (Figure S3), strengthening the idea that MGO induced DA imbalance in SH‐SY5Y cells is specifically mediated by miR‐190a and miR‐214 regulation of COMT and SNCA.

4 Discussion

Dopaminergic system is crucial in modulation of several neurological functions, including cognition [39]. Interestingly, DM is characterized by both dopaminergic dysfunction [40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46], and cognitive deficit [52, 53] and it is likely that alterations in DA function significantly contribute to the DM associated cognitive impairment. A consequence of hyperglycemia, typical of DM, is the abnormally abundant amount of MGO, a highly reactive dicarbonyl compound representing a glycolysis side‐product [54]. Previous studies evidenced MGO toxicity in neuronal cells, highlighting its ability to induce oxidative stress and apoptosis [60, 61, 62]. In murine models, MGO induces cognitive deficit and a decrease of DA levels [55, 56] and in human serum increased MGO levels are associated with cognitive decline [57]. Thus, the aim of the present study was to investigate MGO impact on dopaminergic function regardless of its capability to initiate apoptosis in a cellular model of dopaminergic neurons. Interestingly, we found that MGO has a deleterious effect on dopaminergic system. Indeed, MGO treatment induces a significant reduction of intracellular DA amount in SH‐SY5Y cells, without affecting DA secretion capability. To clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying MGO action, the expression of genes involved in regulation of DA levels has been evaluated, in presence of MGO. DA synthesis was not affected by MGO since the expression of the DA synthesizing enzymes TH and DDC was not modified. In addition, MGO did not induce CML and Arg‐Pyr adducts formation on these proteins, excluding their malfunctioning. In contrast, we observed an increased amount of both mRNA and protein levels of negative regulators of DA levels, such as MAO‐B, COMT and α‐Syn. Then, we hypothesized that MGO could regulate the expression of these genes through a miRNAs mediated mechanism. This idea came from our previous results obtained in endothelial cells, where we discovered that MGO affects the levels of two specific miRNAs, miR‐190a and miR‐214 [72, 73]. These miRNAs represented attractive candidates for this study, since SNCA is regulated by miR‐214 [75] and miR‐190a exerts neuroprotective effects according to literature [79, 80]. More importantly, SNCA and COMT resulted as predicted targets of these miRNAs, according to the analysis performed with TargetScan, a comprehensive atlas of miRNA–target interactions based on an integrated approach incorporating several public bioinformatics tools [74]. In contrast, no positive match was found for MAO‐B as a potential target of either miR‐214 or miR‐190a. Furthermore, we did not consider the other isoenzyme of MAO, MAO‐A, as a top‐ranking gene in the present study. Indeed, DA is mainly degraded by MAO‐A in the rodent brain, while MAO‐B plays a major role in this process in humans and other primates, representing about 80% of total MAO activity in human brain [81, 82]. Thus, we focused our attention on clarifying miR‐214 and miR‐190a role in MGO regulation of α‐Syn and COMT expression. First, we evaluated the levels of these two miRNAs in MGO treated SH‐SY5Y cells and highlighted MGO ability to reduce their expression. These findings prompted us to perform loss‐of‐function and gain‐of‐function experiments in order to investigate in detail miR‐214 and miR‐190a contribute to MGO deleterious effect on dopaminergic function. To study loss‐of‐function effects, miR‐214 and miR‐190a activity was suppressed by transfection of specific miRIDIAN Inhibitors, which are single‐strands RNA oligonucleotides designed to bind and to sequester the complimentary mature miRNA strand. In contrast, to study gain‐of‐function effects, SH‐SY5Y cells were transfected with specific miRIDIAN miRNAs mimics, which are double‐stranded RNA oligonucleotides designed to mimic the function of the endogenous, mature miRNAs. It is noteworthy that the inhibition of the endogenous miR‐214 and miR‐190a obtained upon transfection or upon MGO treatment is sufficient to induce a significant increase in both α‐Syn and COMT protein levels, confirming the ability of these miRNAs to modulate the expression of these proteins. Then, we investigated if miR‐214 and miR‐190a directly bind to SNCA and COMT mRNAs, performing RIP assays. Importantly, we provide here the first evidence that COMT is a direct target of both miR‐214 and miR‐190a and that SNCA is a direct target of miR‐190a. Concerning miR‐214 and SNCA, miR‐214 ability to downregulate SNCA has been already highlighted [75]. However, our results strongly suggest that this modulation is mediated by a not yet identified third factor. Further studies are required to fully address this issue. Similarly, the involvement of other miRNAs regulated by MGO and contributing to its deleterious effect on dopaminergic function in SH‐SY5Y cells cannot be excluded to date and this hypothesis should be further investigated. Anyway, the key role of miR‐214 and miR‐190a in this context is currently undeniable, as evidenced by the impact of their modulation on MGO effect. Indeed, the transfection of miR‐214 and miR‐190a mimics almost blunted the overexpression of COMT and α‐Syn induced by MGO treatment. Importantly, similar evidence was obtained when DA amount was measured in SH‐SY5Y cells transfected with miR‐214 and miR‐190a mimics and incubated with MGO. Also in this case, MGO effect was almost abolished by mimics transfection which restored DA amount to levels comparable to the ones found in SH‐SY5Y cells transfected with control mimics in absence of MGO. In conclusion, the present study clarifies, at least in part, the molecular mechanisms underlying MGO deleterious effect on dopaminergic homeostasis. To date, studies on MGO in neuronal cells focalized above all on its proapoptotic action [59, 60, 61, 62]. Thus, a further novelty of this study is represented by the choice to use a non‐toxic MGO concentration, focusing on its ability to damage dopaminergic system regardless of apoptosis. In murine models, DA depletion and decreased memory performance in response to MGO treatment were observed [83]. Szczepanik and coworkers highlighted RAGE (Receptor for AGEs) involvement in MGO deleterious effect on memory [84], showing that a high‐affinity RAGE antagonist reverts MGO induced deficits on the short‐term aversive memory and working memory [85]. However, the downstream molecular mechanisms underlying MGO effect on DA amount have never been elucidated. In particular, little attention has been given to MGO ability to modulate the expression of genes involved in the modulation of DA levels. Thus, the identification of miR‐214 and miR‐190a as key mediators of MGO effect on dopaminergic homeostasis is a strength point of this study, too. Indeed, miRNAs dysregulation was recently found in animal models of neurodegenerative diseases [86, 87, 88] and in human pathology too [89]. The modulation of specific miRNAs by MGO may represent the “missing link” between glucotoxicity and DM‐associated dopaminergic system deregulation, and it will pave the way to new therapeutic approaches based on miRNA targeting by antisense oligonucleotides for the treatment of DM‐associated dopaminergic dysfunction and cognitive decline. Importantly, these findings could also have a diagnostic perspective. Cognitive impairment in DM remains often undiagnosed, leading to impaired self‐management and increasing the incidence of DM complications [90]. Thus, a better knowledge of the relationship between DM, dopaminergic dysfunction and cognitive impairment may help to identify novel biomarkers for the early detection of diabetic patients at high risk of developing cognitive decline. This is a crucial point for the identification and the stratification of patients to develop appropriate preventive strategies and more effective treatments. The impaired expression profile of these miRNAs observed in SH‐SY5Y cells could be detected also in peripheral blood lymphocytes of diabetic subjects and correlate with cognition scores. In this case, miR‐214 and miR‐190a would represent easily accessible markers of dopaminergic dysfunction and cognitive impairment in individuals with DM.
