This study shows that Parkinson’s disease–associated kinase LRRK2 is activated by inhibition of PIKfyve, which metabolizes phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate, and reveals a novel mechanism of LRRK2 activation that requires interaction with phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate, as well as CASM and Rab.

LRRK2 is a Parkinson’s disease–associated kinase that phosphorylates Rab GTPases. Hyperactivation of LRRK2, which is thought to cause Parkinson’s disease, occurs on stressed endolysosomal membranes via conjugation of ATG8 to single membranes (CASM) and Rab, whereas the involvement of specific membrane lipids remained unclear. Here, we found that the inhibition of PIKfyve that metabolizes phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI3P) induces hyperactivation of LRRK2 and that additional inhibition of the class III phosphoinositide 3-kinase that generates PI3P suppressed LRRK2 hyperactivation. Cellular PI3P levels were indeed elevated when LRRK2 was activated. CASM-induced activation of LRRK2, but not CASM itself, was suppressed by phosphoinositide 3-kinase inhibitors, whereas inhibition of CASM or Rab12/Rab29 suppressed LRRK2 activation induced by PIKfyve inhibitors. LRRK2 and phospho-Rab10 were colocalized with PI3P on LAMP1-positive enlarged vacuoles, and LRRK2 directly bound PI3P in vitro via its N-terminal positively charged amino acids. Together, we propose an updated mechanism of LRRK2 hyperactivation that simultaneously requires CASM, Rab, and PI3P-rich membranes.

Introduction

Leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2) is a large serine/threonine kinase that phosphorylates a subset of Rab GTPases in cells (Alessi & Pfeffer, 2024). LRRK2 consists of 2527 amino acids and harbors multiple functional domains, including the kinase domain, the Ras of complex proteins (Roc)–C terminus of Roc domain, the LRR domain, the N-terminal armadillo (ARM) and ankyrin repeats, and the C-terminal WD40 domain (Iannotta & Greggio, 2021; Komori & Kuwahara, 2023). Missense mutations in LRRK2 have been identified as a major cause for autosomal-dominant Parkinson’s disease (PD) (Paisán-Ruíz et al, 2004; Zimprich et al, 2004). The well-established familial mutations reported to date are all located either in the kinase domain (G2019S, I2020T) or in the Roc–C terminus of Roc domain (N1437H, R1441C/G/H, Y1699C), and structural analyses using cryo-electron microscopy have revealed the close proximity of these two domains (Myasnikov et al, 2021; Zhang & Kortholt, 2023). All these pathogenic mutations have been shown to significantly enhance the Rab phosphorylation activity of LRRK2 (Steger et al, 2016), leading to the hypothesis that abnormal hyperactivation of LRRK2 causes PD.

LRRK2 is widely expressed in the body, with particularly high expression in the brain, lung, kidney, and immune cells such as macrophages and neutrophils (Giasson et al, 2006; Gardet et al, 2010; Fan et al, 2018). In the brain, LRRK2 is expressed ubiquitously in neurons, astrocytes, and microglia. Among these cell types, macrophage lineage cells express high levels of LRRK2 upon exposure to inflammatory stimuli, especially interferon-γ (Gardet et al, 2010; Thévenet et al, 2011). Genetic variants in LRRK2 have also been associated with Crohn’s disease and leprosy (Barrett et al, 2008; Zhang et al, 2009). These reports point to the potential importance of LRRK2 in immune-related cells, particularly in macrophage lineage.

Research on LRRK2 has been extensively conducted in the PD research field, and in clinical settings where LRRK2 inhibitors have been explored intensively (Jennings et al, 2023). In basic biological research, the focus has been on the mechanism of LRRK2 hyperactivation associated with PD and the downstream mechanisms caused by the enhanced Rab phosphorylation. It has been reported that excessive Rab phosphorylation leads to lysosomal dysfunctions indicated by altered morphology or positioning, as well as ciliary defects caused by abnormal centrosome function (Kuwahara & Iwatsubo, 2020; Fasiczka et al, 2023). We and others have shown that LRRK2 is activated on lysosome membranes when lysosomes are stressed by chloroquine (CQ), monensin, nigericin, or L-leucyl-L-leucine methyl ester (LLOMe) (Eguchi et al, 2018; Bonet-Ponce et al, 2020; Herbst et al, 2020; Kalogeropulou et al, 2020). This activation can be mediated by several Rab GTPases, such as Rab32 family members (Rab29, Rab32, Rab38), and Rab12 (Purlyte et al, 2018; Dhekne et al, 2023; Unapanta et al, 2023; Wang et al, 2023a; Gustavsson et al, 2024; Hop et al, 2024). LRRK2 is also activated during conjugation of ATG8 to single membranes (CASM), which is induced by pH elevation and ionic/osmotic imbalances within lysosomes (Florey et al, 2015; Jacquin et al, 2017) and is primarily mediated by the V-ATPase-ATG16L1 axis (Durgan & Florey, 2022; Wang et al, 2022). CASM mediates activation by recruiting LRRK2 and ATG8 family proteins onto the endolysosomal membranes (Eguchi et al, 2024; Kuwahara & Iwatsubo, 2024), and this may involve binding of GABARAP to LRRK2 (Bentley-DeSousa et al, 2025b). In both mechanisms, Rab and GABARAP have been shown to bind to specific sites within the N-terminal ARM repeats of LRRK2, thereby anchoring LRRK2 to the endolysosomal membranes (Bentley-DeSousa et al, 2025a; Sakurai & Kuwahara, 2025).

LRRK2 has also been shown to bind directly to negatively charged lipids in membranes (Wang et al, 2023b). Because LRRK2 becomes activated on the membranes of stressed lysosomes, specific lipids may also contribute to LRRK2 activation, although such a mechanism has remained unexplored. Endolysosomes mainly contain two phosphoinositide (PIP) lipids: phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI3P) and phosphatidylinositol 3,5-bisphosphate (PI(3,5)P2), with the ratio of PI(3,5)P2 increasing as endosomes mature (Wallroth & Haucke, 2018; Posor et al, 2022). We found that treating cells with PIKfyve inhibitors that block PI3P metabolism to PI(3,5)P2 potently activates LRRK2, leading us to study the relationship between PIP changes and LRRK2 activation. The results offer a novel activation model where PI3P is involved in LRRK2 activation in addition to CASM and Rab.

Results

LRRK2 is activated in response to pharmacological stimuli that cause an increase in PI3P

To explore the endolysosomal lipid component that influences LRRK2 kinase activity, we focused on PIKfyve, a lipid kinase that regulates endolysosomal function by generating PI(3,5)P2 from PI3P (Rivero-Ríos & Weisman, 2022). Inhibition of PIKfyve has been shown to induce the formation of swollen endolysosomal vacuoles in cells (Krishna et al, 2016; Uwada et al, 2025), which resemble changes induced by treatment with CQ or monensin, known pharmacological activators of LRRK2. We used mouse macrophage RAW264.7 cells pretreated with interferon-γ, as this treatment greatly increases the expression of endogenous LRRK2 in these cells (Gardet et al, 2010), enabling sensitive detection of LRRK2 activity changes (Eguchi et al, 2018; Kuwahara et al, 2020). Incubation of these cells with apilimod or YM201636, two widely used inhibitors of PIKfyve (Fig 1A), for 2 h caused the formation of swollen endolysosomal vacuoles positive for LAMP1 (Fig 1B). We found that these treatments also caused a marked increase in the kinase activity of LRRK2, as measured by the phosphorylation of its substrate Rab GTPases including Rab10 (Fig 1C and D). This phosphorylation is dependent solely on LRRK2, as treatment with an LRRK2 inhibitor MLi-2 abolished Rab10 phosphorylation (Fig S1A). As an orthogonal approach, we treated cells with two different small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) specific to PIKfyve and confirmed the increase in Rab10 phosphorylation, as well as PIKfyve knockdown after 48 h of treatment (Fig S1B and C). To determine whether this LRRK2 hyperactivation resulted from a decrease in PI(3,5)P2 or an increase in PI3P because of PIKfyve inhibition, we treated cells with inhibitors of class III phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) that generates PI3P from phosphatidylinositol (PI) (Fig 1A). The results showed that the elevation of LRRK2 activity upon PIKfyve inhibition was suppressed by treatment with a selective class III PI3K inhibitor VPS34-IN1 (Fig 1C and D), a second class III PI3K inhibitor Compound 19 (Fig 1E and F), or another PI3K inhibitor 3-methyladenine (Fig S1D and E). These results suggest that an increase in PI3P, rather than a decrease in PI(3,5)P2, is involved in LRRK2 hyperactivation. As a note, treatment with class III PI3K inhibitors alone, especially VPS34-IN1, in the absence of PIKfyve inhibitors did not reduce the low basal level of Rab10 phosphorylation but rather increased it (P = 0.0025 for VPS34-IN1) (Fig 1C–F), implying that low LRRK2 activity under steady-state conditions is also influenced in a complex manner by factors other than PI3K.

Next, we examined whether similar effects could be observed in cells other than RAW264.7 cells. Human lung epithelial A549 cells are known to express high levels of endogenous LRRK2 (Kamikawaji et al, 2013; Lobbestael et al, 2013). Treatment of A549 cells with PIKfyve inhibitors activated LRRK2, and this activation was suppressed by PI3K inhibition (Fig 1G and H). Human embryonic kidney 293 (HEK293) cells express lower levels of endogenous LRRK2, whereas these cells transfected with 3×FLAG-tagged human LRRK2 exhibited elevation of LRRK2 activity upon PIKfyve inhibition, which was again suppressed by PI3K inhibition (Fig 1I and J). More importantly, HEK293 cells expressing PD-associated mutant LRRK2 (R1441C, Y1699C) exhibited higher basal LRRK2 activity, as well as further elevated activity upon PIKfyve inhibition, as compared to cells expressing WT LRRK2 (Fig 1I and J). These results suggest that LRRK2 activation by PIKfyve inhibition occurs regardless of cell type and may represent a phenotype potentially relevant to PD.

We further examined whether the up-regulation of PI3K activity to enhance PI3P generation is sufficient to induce LRRK2 activation. Tat-D11 is an improved form of the class III PI3K-activating peptide Tat-Beclin 1, which facilitates the incorporation of Beclin 1 into the PI3K complex by interfering with its binding to Golgi-localized GAPR-1 (Chang et al, 2019; Zhao et al, 2021; He et al, 2022; Fig 1K). We found that the Tat-D11 treatment in RAW264.7 cells induced the up-regulation of Rab10 phosphorylation and LC3 lipidation (Figs 1L and M, and S1F–H). This up-regulation of Rab10 phosphorylation was canceled by PI3K inhibition (Fig 1L and M), confirming the involvement of PI3K activity in LRRK2 activation. Also, treatment with a LRRK2 inhibitor MLi-2 suppressed the up-regulation of Rab10 phosphorylation but not LC3 lipidation (Fig S1F–H), suggesting that LC3 lipidation occurs independently of LRRK2 activation under enhancement of PI3P generation.

Hyperactivation of LRRK2 by diverse endolysosomal stimuli is accompanied by an increased level of PI3P

LRRK2 is well known to be activated on the membranes of lysosomes stressed by lysosomotropic agents, ionophores, or lysosome-permeabilizing agents (Eguchi et al, 2018; Bonet-Ponce et al, 2020; Herbst et al, 2020; Kuwahara et al, 2020). We then examined the possibility that LRRK2 activation in response to lysosomal stress may involve an increase in PI3P. CQ is a representative lysosomotropic agent that accumulates within lysosomes and causes their osmotic swelling (Marceau et al, 2012; Florey et al, 2015). Treatment of RAW264.7 cells with CQ activated LRRK2, and this activation was suppressed by treatment with the PI3K inhibitors VPS34-IN1 or Compound 19 (Fig 2A and C). CQ treatment also induced LC3 lipidation via CASM, but this was not suppressed by PI3K inhibition (Fig 2A, D, and E), consistent with previous findings that PI3K activity is not required for CASM (Florey et al, 2015; Fletcher et al, 2018).

We next tested the effects of monensin and nigericin also known to activate LRRK2 (Kalogeropulou et al, 2020; Kuwahara et al, 2020). Both reagents act on the lysosomal membrane as monovalent cation/proton ionophores, inducing ionic and osmotic imbalances (Mollenhauer et al, 1990; Jacquin et al, 2017; Rangasamy et al, 2018). Treatment with these reagents indeed activated LRRK2, and this activation was again suppressed by the PI3K inhibitors (Fig 2F and G). LC3 lipidation induced by monensin or nigericin was not suppressed by PI3K inhibition, as observed under CQ treatment (Fig 2F and G). These findings suggest that lysosomal stress–induced activation of LRRK2 requires PI3P generation, whereas the concurrent LC3 lipidation via CASM occurs independently of PI3P generation.

Although the results above supported a link between increased PI3P levels and LRRK2 activity in cells, inhibition of PIKfyve has been shown to primarily cause a reduction in cellular PI(3,5)P2 levels (Jefferies et al, 2008; Cai et al, 2013). However, several studies have also shown a significant increase in PI3P levels at the same time (Cai et al, 2013; Shimanaka et al, 2022), and given that PIP levels are regulated by a complex balance between kinase and phosphatase activities (Posor et al, 2022; Rivero-Ríos & Weisman, 2022), their changes in levels are expected to vary depending on cellular conditions and environment. Thus, we sought to perform a mass spectrometry–based quantification in our cells to confirm changes in PIP isomer levels in response to PIKfyve inhibition, PIKfyve plus PI3K inhibition, or lysosome stress. Because simple mass spectrometry cannot measure the levels of PI3P and PI(3,5)P2 while distinguishing them from regioisomers having the same mass (i.e., PI monophosphates [PI4P, PI5P] and diphosphates [PI(3,4)P2, PI(4,5)P2]), we employed a recently developed method that integrates the separation of regioisomers using specialized chiral chromatography (Morioka et al, 2022). As a result, PI3P levels increased upon apilimod treatment, decreased upon both apilimod and VPS34-IN1 treatment, and increased upon CQ treatment (Fig 2H and Table S1). The levels of PI(3,5)P2 did not change significantly in our cells under these conditions, and the levels of other PIPs remained unchanged as well (Fig 2H and Table S1). CQ treatment in addition caused a decrease in three phosphatidylinositol diphosphates (PI(3,4)P2, PI(3,5)P2, and PI(4.5)P2) (Fig 2H and Table S1). These data strengthen the notion that hyperactivation of LRRK2 involves an increase in intracellular PI3P levels.

CASM is involved in LRRK2 activation upon PIKfyve inhibition

Next, we investigated the relationship between LRRK2 activation induced by PIKfyve inhibition and CASM, which also mediates LRRK2 activation in response to lysosomal stress (Eguchi et al, 2024; Kuwahara & Iwatsubo, 2024; Bentley-DeSousa et al, 2025b). The key factors in CASM are WD40 domain of ATG16L1, which is not required for macroautophagy but is required for binding ATG16L1 and the ATG5–ATG12 LC3 conjugation machinery to the lysosomal V-ATPase in response to raised lysosomal pH (Fig 3A). Unlike macroautophagy, CASM does not require class III PI3K activity (Florey et al, 2015; Fletcher et al, 2018), and therefore, it remained unclear whether CASM is involved in LRRK2 activation upon PI3P elevation.

We first examined the effect of knockdown of Atg16l1 or Atg5, both of which participate in CASM execution, on PIKfyve inhibition–induced activation of LRRK2. Knockdown of Atg16l1 or Atg5 in RAW264.7 cells significantly suppressed LRRK2 activation, not only lipidation of LC3B/GABARAP, upon apilimod treatment or the positive control of CQ treatment (Figs 3B and C and S2). We also tested the effect of bafilomycin A1, which inhibits the lysosomal V-ATPase required for CASM, and confirmed its suppression of LRRK2 activation after PIKfyve inhibition (Fig 3D and E). To further validate the involvement of CASM and not autophagy in LRRK2 activation, we used bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs) from mice that express mutant Atg16l1 lacking the WD40 domain because of a stop codon inserted after E230 residue (E230/ΔWD mice) (Rai et al, 2019); these cells retain macroautophagy activity but lack CASM. We found that apilimod-induced activation of LRRK2 was suppressed in E230/ΔWD BMDMs compared with WT cells (Fig 3F and G). Thus, these data indicate that CASM machinery is required for the activation of LRRK2 induced by PIKfyve inhibition.

We in addition found that treating cells with not only CQ but also apilimod up-regulated lipidation of LC3B and GABARAP, although that of GABARAP was faint (Figs 3B and F and S2). This lipidation was largely suppressed in E230/ΔWD BMDMs (Fig 3F), indicating that PIKfyve inhibition up-regulated CASM, not just the blockage of autophagy flux after lysosome dysfunction. Given that PI3K inhibition suppresses CASM-induced activation of LRRK2 but not CASM itself (Fig 2A–G) and that the independency of PI3K from CASM has been confirmed (Florey et al, 2015; Fletcher et al, 2018), these data suggest that PIKfyve inhibition induces LRRK2 hyperactivation through both PI3P increase and CASM induction.

Although PI3P is not involved in typical CASM, a specific type of CASM called LC3-associated phagocytosis (LAP) is elicited through NADPH oxidase 2 (Nox2), which is activated by PI3P (Ueyama et al, 2011; Durgan & Florey, 2022). Nox2 produces reactive oxygen species after uptake of external pathogens, which then induces LAP (Huang et al, 2009; Fig 3A). Thus, it was still possible that increased PI3P could activate LRRK2 by up-regulating Nox2 and then LAP. To test this possibility, we treated RAW264.7 cells with a Nox2 inhibitor diphenyleneiodonium and examined its effect on LRRK2 activation upon PIKfyve inhibition. We found that Nox2 inhibition did not suppress apilimod-induced activation of LRRK2 (Fig 3H and I), whereas it suppressed LRRK2 activation and LC3B lipidation upon treatment with zymosan, a yeast cell wall preparation that induces LAP (Fig 3H, J, and K). Thus, although CASM is involved in LRRK2 activation upon PIKfyve inhibition, this does not appear to be mediated by the PI3P-Nox2 pathway upstream of LAP.

Rab12 and Rab29 are also required for the activation of LRRK2 upon PIKfyve inhibition

It has been shown that not only CASM but also specific Rab GTPases, that is, Rab12 and Rab32 family members (Rab29, Rab32, Rab38), are involved in LRRK2 activation. The functionally dominant Rab32 family member varies depending on the cell type, condition, etc., with Rab29 being one of essential regulators in our RAW264.7 cells (Eguchi et al, 2018; Kuwahara et al, 2020). We then examined the involvement of Rab12 and Rab29 in LRRK2 activation upon PIKfyve inhibition. As a result, knockdown of Rab12 or Rab29 in RAW264.7 cells significantly suppressed LRRK2 activation upon treatment with apilimod or YM203616 (Fig 4A–D). Thus, combining the results obtained thus far, it has become clear that these Rabs, CASM, and PI3P are all essential factors for the activation of LRRK2.

Endogenous LRRK2 and phospho-Rab10 colocalize with PI3P in cells exposed to PI3P-increasing stimuli

Next, we investigated whether LRRK2 is localized on PI3P-positive membranes in cells, particularly under PIKfyve inhibition. The subcellular localization of PI3P can be assessed by detecting the fluorescence of a PI3P probe p40PX-EGFP, where EGFP is fused to the Phox homology domain of p40phox that specifically binds to PI3P (Kanai et al, 2001). We first employed HEK293 cells that are fully competent for overexpression and observed the localization of overexpressed p40PX-EGFP and 3×FLAG-LRRK2. However, we could not see their colocalization likely because of strong expression of both proteins throughout the cytoplasm, whereas endogenous LRRK2 in HEK293 cells was hardly detected by immunocytochemistry. Then, we examined the localization of endogenous LRRK2 in RAW264.7 cells. Because this cell line is hardly transfectable, we sought to express p40PX-EGFP by lentiviral infection, albeit at low efficiency. In this case as well, cells strongly expressing p40PX-EGFP were unsuitable for analysis, but in cells with low expression levels (where most of the cytoplasm had little fluorescence), endogenous LRRK2 was well colocalized with p40PX-EGFP on LAMP1-positive vacuoles formed by treatment with apilimod, YM201636, or CQ (Fig 5A). This endogenous LRRK2 was not detected in Lrrk2 knockout RAW264.7 cells, including its diffuse localization in the cytoplasm, ensuring the specificity of immunostaining (Fig S3). We also examined the localization of endogenous Rab10 phosphorylated at Thr73, and it was similarly colocalized with p40PX-EGFP (Fig 5B). Because not all signals were well matched with p40PX-EGFP, the possibility remains that other PI3P-independent mechanisms for LRRK2 recruitment exist. Nonetheless, quantitative analysis with Pearson’s correlation coefficient revealed that the colocalization of p40PX-EGFP with LRRK2 or phospho-Rab10 was increased upon treatment with apilimod, YM201636, or CQ compared with untreated controls (Fig 5C and D). These colocalized proteins were found on a subset of LAMP1-positive endolysosomal vacuoles distributed throughout the cytoplasm, and the LAMP1 positivity in p40PX-EGFP–positive area was increased upon treatment with PIKfyve inhibitors or CQ (Fig 5E). These observations suggest that LRRK2 becomes concentrated and activated on PI3P-rich endolysosomal membranes after PIKfyve inhibition or lysosomal stress.

LRRK2 directly binds to PI3P via its N-terminal positively charged cluster

Because LRRK2 and PI3P were found to colocalize on stressed endolysosomes, we investigated the possibility that LRRK2 may directly bind PI3P. Recombinant human full-length LRRK2 protein with FLAG tag was reacted with nitrocellulose membranes spotted with 15 different phospholipids (each 100 pmol) including PI3P and other PIPs. We found that LRRK2 reacted strongly with three phosphatidylinositol monophosphates (PI3P, PI4P, and PI5P) and phosphatidic acid, with the highest reactivity on PI3P (Fig 6A). This binding profile suggested that electrostatic interactions between LRRK2 and anionic lipids carrying a negative charge equivalent to one phosphate group may be involved in binding. Also, recombinant LRRK2 lacking its N-terminal 969 amino acids did not exhibit binding to the lipids on the membrane strip (Fig 6A), suggesting the importance of the N-terminal domains for lipid binding. We in addition examined the binding of recombinant full-length LRRK2 with biotin-conjugated liposomes containing either PI, PI3P, or PI(3,5)P2 by a pull-down assay. LRRK2 was specifically pulled down with liposomes containing PI3P, and, to a slightly lesser extent, with those containing PI(3,5)P2 (Fig 6B and C).

The N-terminal region of LRRK2 contains ARM and ankyrin repeats, and particularly, the most N-terminal region in ARM repeats (12-705 amino acids) has been shown to interact with multiple Rab and GABARAP proteins anchored on the membrane. Structural analysis of full-length LRRK2 by cryo-electron microscopy has also predicted that the most N-terminal region faces the membrane (Myasnikov et al, 2021). However, this region does not appear to contain known PI3P/PIP-binding domains such as the FYVE domain or the Phox homology domain. We then searched for positively charged regions within the N-terminal domain that might be involved in binding to anionic lipids. Although most regions were found to be negatively charged, we confirmed the presence of clusters bearing positive charge (Fig S4A). Also, by employing computational methods to identify positively charged amino acids with potential to interact with PI3P, we identified K87, K137, and the K178/K182/H185 cluster, whose side chains are all predicted to be oriented toward the membrane (Figs 6D and S4B). These five amino acids are conserved across a wide range of animal species, implying important functions (Fig S4B). We expressed mutant LRRK2 with these five amino acids substituted by alanine in HEK293 cells and first screened for those whose activation was suppressed upon apilimod treatment. Activation at 80–90% of control still occurred in LRRK2 mutants with a single Ala substitution at each amino acid or the triple mutant where K178/K182/H185 were substituted with Ala (TriA), whereas the quintuple mutant with five amino acids substituted with Ala (PentaA) reduced activation by 50% (Fig 6E and F). This mutant also exhibited reduced expression levels, which was thought to result from the mutation weakening LRRK2’s membrane binding and thereby causing protein destabilization. We in addition confirmed through AlphaFold 3 predictions that the N-terminal structure is unlikely to change significantly with the PentaA mutation (Fig S4C). Then, we generated recombinant WT and PentaA mutant LRRK2 protein with 3×FLAG tag purified from overexpressed HEK293 cells and evaluated their binding to PIPs immobilized on nitrocellulose membranes. PentaA LRRK2 was found to exhibit an overall reduction in binding affinity for PIPs, including PI3P, compared with WT LRRK2 (Fig 6G). Thus, the five positively charged amino acids modified in PentaA appear to be involved in the binding to these PIPs.

Lastly, we examined the possibility that LRRK2 behaves as a PI3P-dependent kinase in vitro, like known PIP-dependent kinases such as 3-phosphoinositide–dependent protein kinase 1 (Alessi et al, 1997). An in vitro kinase assay using recombinant full-length LRRK2 revealed that LRRK2 autophosphorylation activity was not changed upon addition of liposomes containing PI, PI3P, or PI(3,5)P2 (Fig S5A). Also, in a cell-free assay where Lrrk2 knockout RAW264.7 cell–derived membranes were incubated with recombinant LRRK2, no changes in LRRK2 kinase activity were observed upon PI3P addition (Fig S5B). These results indicate that the simple addition of PI3P in vitro is not sufficient to activate LRRK2 as observed in cells. Given that PI3P, CASM components, and multiple Rab GTPases are properly positioned at different sites in the N-terminal region of LRRK2, a model of LRRK2 hyperactivation emerges where these essential activators bind LRRK2 simultaneously in cells to stabilize it on the endolysosomal membranes (Fig 6H).

Discussion

In this study, we revealed that LRRK2 becomes activated in cells under conditions that increase PI3P, triggered by modulation of the activities of PIKfyve and the class III PI3K. Activation of LRRK2 has previously been shown to be elicited via CASM machinery and multiple Rab GTPases, and now we added PI3P, another mediator that acts in parallel to CASM, yet both function as essential factors. Unlike macroautophagy, CASM does not require PI3P generation; however, each component separately participates in LRRK2 activation, forming a rather complicated LRRK2 activation mechanism. In our previous study reporting CASM-dependent LRRK2 activation (Eguchi et al, 2024), we tried to show its independence from PI3K activity but unexpectedly found it to be necessary; our present finding has now solved this mystery.

It is well known that LRRK2 is translocated to the membranes of lysosomes subjected to various forms of stress, but Rab and CASM components (e.g., GABARAP) alone could not fully explain its localization specificity to stressed endolysosomes. The endolysosomal membrane is rich in PI3P and PI(3,5)P2, with the ratio of PI(3,5)P2 increasing during maturation, whereas stress is thought to cause dematuration to increase PI3P levels. Therefore, simultaneous binding of LRRK2 to such membranes, as well as Rab and CASM components, may ensure the specificity of its localization. Although PI3P increase associated with endolysosomal dematuration likely has other primary purposes, it also contributes to LRRK2 activation possibly as another rational mechanism. Once LRRK2 is properly tethered on the membrane with the aid of PI3P, CASM components, and Rab, it becomes fully stabilized and activated, boosting the lysosomal stress response via Rab phosphorylation.

Our data suggested that PI3P-rich membranes, CASM components, and Rab12/Rab29 all act as essential factors for LRRK2 activation. Although their detailed relationship remains debatable, these factors are thought to act both in parallel and in tandem. First, they can simultaneously bind to different sites in the N-terminal region of LRRK2, enabling their parallel action to fully stabilize LRRK2 on membranes and activate it. Thus, depletion of any single factor significantly reduced LRRK2 activation, whereas it did not completely cancel LRRK2 activation in the presence of other factors. On the other hand, our data showed that PIKfyve inhibition caused CASM induction (Fig 3F) and CASM in turn increased PI3P levels (Fig 2H), supporting the existence of their tandem relationship. Thus, such complex interplay among these essential factors is thought to contribute to LRRK2 hyperactivation.

The relationship between LRRK2 and PIPs has not been well understood, whereas it has been reported that LRRK2 directly binds to acidic lipid membranes and alters their curvature (Wang et al, 2023b), supporting the findings of this study. On the other hand, different from this study, there is a report showing that inhibition of class III PI3K selectively activates PD-associated mutant LRRK2 overexpressed in HEK293 cells (Rinaldi et al, 2023). Although the effect of PI3K inhibition on mutant LRRK2 was not tested in the present study, endogenous LRRK2 activity in RAW264.7 cells in the absence of PIKfyve inhibitors showed a tendency to increase upon PI3K inhibition. This observation suggests the potential existence of additional mechanisms not directly involving PI3P when LRRK2 activity is low. Such mechanisms include the involvement of phospholipids other than PI3P, as LRRK2 exerts its activity on the intracellular membranes as long as it can bind to other phospholipids.

Activation of LRRK2 by PIKfyve inhibition has also been reported recently (Malankhanova et al, 2025 Preprint; Clegg et al, 2026). These studies reported that an increase in lysosomal osmotic pressure and volume expansion activates LRRK2, and one study suggested that this increase is mediated by the activation of a chloride/proton antiporter ClC-7, which results from an expected decrease in PI(3,5)P2 upon PIKfyve inhibition (Clegg et al, 2026). Our present study does not rule out the possibility of a local decrease in PI(3,5)P2, whereas our bulk quantification of PIPs showed that the increase in PI3P was the only significant change and correlated with LRRK2 activation (Fig 2H). Nonetheless, an increase in osmotic pressure is highly plausible in our PIKfyve inhibition experiments as well, and this may accompany an increase in PI3P. Another difference between our data and the aforementioned study is the necessity of CASM in LRRK2 activation upon PIKfyve inhibition. Our results, especially the finding that this LRRK2 activation is suppressed in ATG16L1 ΔWD40 cells that specifically lack CASM, strongly support the role of CASM as a contributing factor. This is further supported by previous findings that linked lysosomal osmotic stress to CASM (Florey et al, 2015). A commentary on the above study also stated LRRK2 activation under PIKfyve inhibition as CASM-free (Pfeffer, 2026), but this needs to be carefully verified through independent studies before it becomes established as general idea.

Although the PI3P-mediated activation model is promising, another point requiring caution is whether direct binding of LRRK2 to PI3P in vitro can sufficiently explain its activation in cells. First, it remains unclear whether the binding pattern in vitro is identical to that occurring within cells. Second, even if binding occurs as expected in cells, LRRK2 activation may occur not only via this binding but also through other PI3P-dependent pathways. Nonetheless, LRRK2 PentaA mutation predicted to abolish its binding to PI3P significantly suppressed LRRK2 activation, supporting the notion that binding and activity are linked. Furthermore, we tested the possibility that PI3P might indirectly activate LRRK2 via NOX2-mediated CASM activation, but this was ruled out. Therefore, the model of LRRK2 activation through direct binding to PI3P appears to be most plausible.

We also conducted computational analysis to predict candidate N-terminal amino acids in LRRK2 that could bind to PI3P, and we indeed found evidence of their involvement in binding. Although in silico prediction in the absence of known PI3P-interacting motifs leaves open the possibility of additional binding sites, the predicted amino acids differ from known binding sites for Rab12, Rab29, phospho-Rab, or GABARAP and are consistent with the predicted orientation toward the membrane. We should also note that although the binding is thought to be mediated by electrostatic interactions, the positively charged binding site is located in a structurally slightly indented region, and the surrounding area has a higher negative charge. This may explain why it readily binds to monophosphates such as PI3P, which carries a weaker negative charge compared with diphosphates and triphosphates. This line of reasoning in turn suggests that LRRK2 may bind to monophosphates PI4P and PI5P as well, and indeed, this was observed in vitro. Thus, the possibility remains that LRRK2 in addition localizes to membranes rich in such lipids. For example, PI4P is known to increase in lysosomal membranes damaged by LLOMe and other lysosomal membrane–permeabilizing agents (Radulovic et al, 2022; Tan & Finkel, 2022), and LRRK2 may bind to such PIP to localize there because LRRK2 is known to be activated also by LLOMe treatment (Herbst et al, 2020; Kalogeropulou et al, 2020). This possibility is worth further consideration in the future.

The relationship between PD pathomechanisms and PI3P-associated LRRK2 activation is also of interest. Given that elevated LRRK2 kinase activity has been reported in sporadic PD tissues and cells (Di Maio et al, 2018; Petropoulou-Vathi et al, 2022), it is possible that PI3P levels are increased at the lesion site. Alternatively, the endolysosomal localization of Rabs or CASM components may be increased there. Because lysosomal dysfunction in human cells is known to progress with aging, as evidenced by the accumulation of lipofuscin granules, SA-β-gal, etc. (Goyal, 1982; Kurz et al, 2000), such dysfunction may lead to increased PI3P and/or CASM, potentially raising the risk of LRRK2-associated PD. Therefore, it is desirable to investigate these possibilities using human patient–derived samples in the future. In terms of drug discovery, compounds that block the interaction between LRRK2 and PI3P could potentially yield drugs that suppress abnormal activation of LRRK2 without inhibiting its basal activity. Such an approach may also serve as a possible direction for future research in disease studies.

Plasmids and siRNAs

Plasmids encoding 3×FLAG-human LRRK2 WT, R1441C, and Y1699C were used previously (Fujimoto et al, 2018; Kuwahara et al, 2020). To construct plasmids encoding 3×FLAG-human LRRK2 with an alanine substitution mutation at the N-terminal region, p3×FLAG-human LRRK2 WT was first cut by Bsp1407 I (Takara Bio) to remove the LRRK2 N-terminal region, and then, the two PCR amplicons overlapping the mutation site, as well as the cleaved plasmid, were assembled using NEBuilder (New England Biolabs). To construct a lentiviral plasmid for the expression of p40PX-EGFP in RAW264.7 cells, the p40PX-EGFP sequence (Addgene) was PCR-amplified and inserted into the XhoI-NotI site of the pLVSIN-CMV Neo vector (Takara Bio) using Ligation high Ver.2 (Toyobo). All constructs were sequence-verified. siRNAs for the knockdown of gene expression were purchased from Horizon Discovery (Dharmacon siGENOME, Pikfyve: Cat#D-040127-05 and D-040127-06, Atg5: Cat#D-064838-01, Atg16l1: Cat#D-051699-01, Rab12: Cat#M-040865-01, Rab29: Cat#M-053101-00).

Cell culture and transfection

RAW264.7 mouse macrophage cells (ECACC, RRID: CVCL_0493) and Lrrk2 knockout RAW264.7 cells (ATCC, RRID: CVCL_UL72) were routinely cultured on culture dishes for suspended cells (Sumitomo Bakelite Co.) in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) FBS. These cells were pretreated with interferon-γ (15 ng/ml, Cell Signaling) for 24–48 h before assay, which was necessary to fully elicit the activity of endogenous LRRK2. HEK293 cells (ATCC, RRID: CVCL_0045) and human lung carcinoma A549 cells (ECACC, RRID: CVCL_0023) were cultured on normal dishes in DMEM supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) FBS. BMDMs were generated from femurs and tibias of WT and E230 mice as described previously (Eguchi et al, 2024). Briefly, macrophages were generated from adherent cells in RPMI 1640 containing 10% FBS and M-CSF (315-02; PeproTech) (30 ng/ml) for 6 d. Macrophage populations were quantified by FACS using antibodies against CD16/CD32, F4/80, and CD11b (101320, 123107; BioLegend). The experiment of BMDM isolation from mice was performed in accordance with UK Home Office guidelines and under the UK Animals (Scientific procedures) Act 1986. All cell lines were cultured at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cells were treated with chemical reagents for 2–3 h by medium exchange. Transfection of LRRK2 plasmids in HEK293 cells was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s protocols, and cells were analyzed after 24–48 h. Transfection of siRNAs was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and cells were analyzed after 48–72 h. For immunocytochemistry, cells were seeded on a coverslip without any special coating.

Lentiviral expression of p40PX-EGFP

For the production of recombinant lentiviral particles, pLVSIN-CMV Neo vector encoding p40PX-EGFP and Lentiviral High Titer Packaging Mix (Takara Bio) were transfected into Lenti-X 293T cells (Takara Bio) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The medium was changed after 6–12 h of transfection, and then, medium was collected and refreshed daily for 2 d. The lentiviral particles in medium were concentrated using Lenti-X Concentrator (Takara Bio), resuspended in DMEM, and then used immediately to infect RAW264.7 cells together with polybrene (Millipore). The medium was changed after 24 h, and the infected cells were analyzed the next day.

SDS–PAGE and immunoblot analysis

Immunoblot analysis basically follows our previous method (Sakurai & Kuwahara, 2021; Abe et al, 2024). Cells were washed with PBS on ice and lysed in a lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 1% (vol/vol) Triton X-100, cOmplete EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche), and PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche). Lysates were centrifuged at 17,800g for 5 min at 4°C, and supernatants were mixed with NuPAGE LDS sample buffer (4×) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For SDS–PAGE, samples were loaded onto polyacrylamide gels (SuperSep Ace, Fujifilm Wako) and electrophoresed. After electrophoresis, samples were transferred to PVDF membranes using iBlot3 (Invitrogen). Transferred membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk (BD), and incubated with primary antibodies and then with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch). Membranes were incubated with ImmunoStar Zeta (Fujifilm Wako), and the chemiluminescence of the protein bands was detected using LAS-4000 (Fujifilm). For densitometric analysis, the integrated densities of protein bands were calculated using ImageJ software (NIH). To further align the overall chemiluminescence intensity among several different membranes, the band intensities were normalized so that the sum of the intensities of all bands for each membrane was equal.

Measurement of cellular PIP levels by mass spectrometry

RAW264.7 cells cultured on two 10-cm-diameter culture dishes (for suspended cells, Sumitomo Bakelite Co.) were treated with each drug for 3 h, and then, cells were harvested using a cell scraper. After counting the cells, they were centrifuged at 200g for 3 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was temporally stored at −80°C. The subsequent analysis was conducted at Lipidome Lab Co., Ltd, as described previously (Morioka et al, 2022). Briefly, samples were dissolved in methanol, and total lipids were extracted by the modified Bligh and Dyer method. The total lipid fraction was further purified and recovered using an anion exchange column to isolate the acidic phospholipid fraction including PIPs. The purified fraction was dried under a stream of nitrogen, then redissolved in acetonitrile, and transferred to vials. LC-MS/MS analysis was performed using the Xevo TQ-XS mass spectrometer with an ACQUITY UPLC H-Class (Waters). The lipids were separated using a CHIRALPAK IC-3 column (2.1 × 250 mm, 3 μm, DAICEL) at 23°C under the conditions as described previously (Morioka et al, 2022). PIP species were measured using multiple reaction monitoring in positive ion mode, and peak picking was conducted using analytical software MassLynx 4.2 (Waters). Peak areas of individual species were normalized with those of the internal/surrogate standards, and final quantification data were obtained by correcting data for the total phosphatidylserine amount detected and presented in pmol/PS 1000 pmol units.

Immunocytochemistry

Cells cultured on coverslips were fixed with 4% (wt/vol) PFA for 20 min, followed by immersion in 100% EtOH at −20°C, which was necessary for the staining with an anti-LRRK2 antibody c41-2 (MJFF2). Samples were washed with PBS and then permeabilized and blocked with 3% (wt/vol) BSA in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100. Samples were incubated with primary antibody solutions diluted at the ratio as described above, followed by incubation with corresponding secondary antibodies conjugated with fluorescent dyes (goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 546 and goat anti-rat IgG Alexa Fluor 647, Thermo Fisher Scientific) diluted at 1:1,000 in the blocking buffer. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 1:5,000 dilution. The samples were imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (FLUOVIEW FV3000, Evident) equipped with a 100× oil immersion objective lens. Image contrast and brightness were adjusted using Photoshop 2025 software (Adobe). Colocalization of p40PX-EGFP fluorescence and endogenous LRRK2/pRab10 signal was measured using Pearson’s correlation coefficient, whereas colocalization of p40PX-EGFP and LAMP1 was measured using Manders’ coefficient, both using the ImageJ (NIH) JACoP plugin with Costes’ automatic threshold method.

Purification of the LRRK2 protein

Human LRRK2 WT and PentaA proteins tagged with 3×FLAG at the N terminus were purified from monoclonal HEK293 cells stably expressing either 3×FLAG-LRRK2 WT or PentaA as follows. First, these cells cultured on ten 15-cm-diameter dishes were washed once with D-PBS, collected into 10 ml (per dish) of D-PBS using cell scraper (SPL), and centrifuged at 200g for 3 min. Cell pellets were then lysed in 12.5 ml of ice-cold lysis buffer (TBS [50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl], 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.5% [vol/vol] Triton X-100, cOmplete EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail [Roche], PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitor cocktail [Roche], 1 mM PMSF [Wako]) and rotated for 1 h at 4°C. The lysates were centrifuged at 17,800g for 5 min at 4°C, and after collecting a small aliquot as the input fraction, supernatants were mixed with 100 μl of TBS-equilibrated anti-FLAG M2 Affinity Gel (Merck-Millipore) and rotated overnight at 4°C. Samples were then centrifuged at 5,800g for 1 min at 4°C, and the gel pellets were resuspended in 1.6 ml of ice-cold wash buffer (TBS [50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl], 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.1% [vol/vol] Triton X-100, 1 mM PMSF [Wako]). After repeating this wash step four times, the gel pellets were mixed with 200 μl of 3×FLAG peptide (200 μg/ml in TBS, Sigma-Aldrich) by pipetting and incubated for 20 min at room temperature with rotation. Samples were centrifuged at 5,800g for 1 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was transferred to new tubes. The gel pellets were again mixed with 160 μl of 3×FLAG peptide, and the procedure of incubation followed by centrifugation was repeated to obtain the supernatant from two runs as the elute fraction. To remove 3×FLAG peptide carried over, this fraction was further added to Nanosep 30K Omega centrifugal filters (Pall Life Sciences) and centrifuged at 2000g for 20 min at 4°C. Samples remaining in the upper chamber were mixed with 450 μl of TBS and centrifuged again, and after repeating this step twice, the samples in the upper chamber were recovered as the purified concentrated fraction.

Protein–lipid overlay assay

The direct binding of LRRK2 to various phospholipids was assessed using PIP Strips (P-6001; Echelon Biosciences). PIP membranes were first blocked with blocking buffer containing 3% BSA in TBS-T (0.1% [vol/vol] Tween-20 in TBS) for 1 h and then probed with 1 μg of recombinant FLAG-tagged full-length LRRK2 WT (Invitrogen, and the protein purified in-house for comparison with PentaA), LRRK2 PentaA (purified in-house), or GST-tagged ΔN-LRRK2 (970-2527 amino acids) (Invitrogen) in blocking buffer for 1 h. Membranes were then washed with TBS-T three times and incubated with anti-FLAG antibody (Sigma-Aldrich) or anti-GST antibody (Cytiva) for 1 h. After washing with TBS-T three times, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch) for 1 h and then washed again with TBS-T three times. Membranes were incubated with ImmunoStar Zeta (Fujifilm Wako), and the chemiluminescence of the signal was detected using LAS-4000 (Fujifilm).

Liposome pull-down assay

Recombinant FLAG-tagged full-length LRRK2 WT (0.25 μg, Invitrogen) was mixed with or without 7.5 μl of biotin-conjugated polymerized liposomes containing PI, PI3P, or PI(3,5)P2 (PolyPIPosome, Echelon Biosciences) in 300 μl of the binding buffer (0.05% [vol/vol] NP-40 in TBS). After 15 min of incubation with rotation at room temperature, the samples were mixed with 15 μl of streptavidin-coupled magnetic beads (Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin T1, Thermo Fisher Scientific), prewashed twice with TBS, and then incubated for 30 min with rotation at room temperature. The samples were washed four times with the binding buffer using a magnetic rack, whereas the supernatant after the first wash was collected as the flow-through fraction. After removing buffer, the samples with beads were resuspended in 25 μl of NuPAGE LDS sample buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated for 5 min at 90°C. The samples were then placed on a magnetic rack for 1–2 min, and the supernatants were collected as the bound fraction. The presence of FLAG-LRRK2 in the flow-through and the bound fractions were analyzed by SDS–PAGE and immunoblotting using an anti-FLAG M2 antibody.

In silico prediction of the LRRK2-PI3P interaction site

The DiffDock-L (Corso et al, 2024 Preprint), deep-learning–based docking method, was used to predict the interaction sites of LRRK2 with PI3P using the N-terminal portion of 325 residues from the crystal structure of LRRK2 (C chain of 8FO8) and Ins(1,3)P2, a soluble mimic of PI3P, for the prediction. Note that the crystal structure of LRRK2 lacks a few lysine residues on its α-helix. The prediction was performed using DiffDock-Web on Hugging Face Spaces with the default parameters. Candidate sites were narrowed down by taking into account the conservation of residues among homologous proteins in vertebrates. The multiple sequence alignment of homologous proteins was calculated with FAMSA (Deorowicz et al, 2016). The surface charges of LRRK2 were calculated and visualized using UCSF Chimera.

In vitro/cell-free kinase assay

For in vitro kinase assay evaluating LRRK2 autophosphorylation, recombinant FLAG-LRRK2 (200 ng, Invitrogen) was mixed with 4 μg of liposomes containing 5% PI, PI3P, or PI(3,5)P2 (PolyPIPosome, Echelon Biosciences) in 20 μl of an assay buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM CaCl2, 2 mM DTT, 1 mM ATP) and incubated for 20 min at 30°C while shaking at 300 rpm using Thermo Shaker BSR-MS100 (Biomedical Science). Samples were then subjected to immunoblot analysis using anti-phospho-Thr and anti-FLAG antibodies. For cell-free kinase assays, membrane fractions from Lrrk2 knockout RAW264.7 cells were first prepared by homogenizing cell pellets in a homogenization buffer (20 mM Hepes-KOH [pH 7.5], 250 mM sucrose, cOmplete EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail [Roche]) followed by centrifugation at 800g for 10 min (collecting sup) and then ultracentrifugation at 100,000g for 15 min. The pellet was resuspended in a kinase assay buffer (50 mM Hepes-KOH [pH 7.5], 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM CaCl2, 2 mM DTT, 1 mM ATP, cOmplete EDTA-free, PhosSTOP [Roche]), and then, recombinant FLAG-LRRK2 G2019S (5 nM, Invitrogen) and PI(3)P diC16 (Echelon Biosciences) at several concentrations were added to 30 μl of the mixture. Samples were incubated for 60 min at 30°C while shaking at 400 rpm and subjected to the immunoblot analysis using anti-phospho-Rab and other antibodies.

Statistics

The statistical significance of difference in mean values was calculated by one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test using GraphPad Prism 9. Standard error of the mean was shown in all graphs. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. No exclusion criteria were applied to exclude samples from analysis.
