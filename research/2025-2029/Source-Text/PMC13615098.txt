Parkinson’s disease (PD), the most prevalent neurodegenerative movement disorder, is characterised by widespread alpha-synuclein (α-Syn) pathology in the brain and the progressive degeneration of nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons. Experimental and clinicopathological data support a templated, prion-like component to α-Syn spread; however, the cellular mechanisms governing fibril uptake and intercellular transmission remain incompletely defined. Fc gamma receptor IIb (FcγRIIb or CD32b), the sole cross-species inhibitory Fcγ receptor, has been implicated in α-Syn internalisation in vitro, though its contribution to α-Syn propagation in vivo is not well established. Here, we examine the role of FcγRIIb in α-Syn seeding, propagation, and neurotoxicity in vivo using the preformed fibril (PFF) α-Syn intracranial injection model in mice. Genetic ablation of murine FcγRII reduced phosphorylated α-Syn (pα-Syn) burden and anatomical distribution across multiple brain regions at 90 days post-PFF injection, consistent with impaired development of Lewy-like pathology. Attenuated pα-Syn pathology was accompanied by reduced microglial reactivity, preservation of nigrostriatal dopaminergic integrity, and protection from PFF-associated motor and exploratory deficits. In parallel, pharmacological blockade of human (h) FcγRIIb using monoclonal antibodies reduced acute-phase pα-Syn accumulation in hFcγRIIb-transgenic mice following PFF challenge. Collectively, these findings identify FcγRIIb as a critical regulator of α-Syn propagation in vivo and support receptor-targeted blockade as a mechanistically distinct and therapeutically tractable strategy for modifying disease progression in PD and related α-synucleinopathies.

Introduction

Central to Parkinson’s disease (PD) pathophysiology is alpha-synuclein (α-Syn), an intrinsically unstructured, presynaptically enriched protein that is prone to misfolding and assembly into β-sheet-rich conformers [9, 10]. Aggregated α-Syn drives the formation of signature Lewy bodies (LB) and Lewy neurites, which spread along interconnected brain circuitry in a predictable spatiotemporal pattern [6]. Prevailing evidence supports a prion-like mechanism of α-Syn propagation, whereby fibrillar seeds of misfolded α-Syn disseminate trans-neuronally, templating the aggregation of naïve monomeric (Mono) α-Syn into toxic oligomers or fibrils [3, 39–41]. However, despite extensive characterisation of α-Syn aggregation and spread, the molecular mediators that govern the propagation of α-Syn are not well known. Multiple non-mutually exclusive mechanisms have been proposed, including tunnelling nanotube-mediated transfer, passive trans-membrane diffusion, and receptor-dependent endocytic uptake [7, 43].

Fc gamma receptor IIb (FcγRIIb or CD32b), referred to as FcγRII or CD32 in mice, has recently emerged as a promising receptor candidate implicated in α-Syn propagation, based on its capacity to bind aggregated α-Syn species [13, 14]. FcγRs comprise six isoforms in humans and four in mice [8, 20, 29], collectively orchestrating peripheral immune effector responses through a balance of activating and inhibitory signalling. Their principal role is to recognise and bind the Fc domain of IgG antibodies, coupling antigen recognition with downstream cellular responses such as phagocytosis and cytokine release, contributing to the preservation of peripheral immune homeostasis [29]. FcγRIIb is the sole inhibitory FcγR conserved between species and uniquely restrains immune cell activation following immune complex engagement, in part, via its immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif (ITIM) [16, 33]. Importantly, FcγRIIb expression is documented on both neuronal and microglial membranes within the central nervous system (CNS), particularly under conditions of proteopathic stress [2, 22, 23, 28].

Choi et al. provide direct evidence that fibrillar species of α-Syn bind FcγRIIb in vitro, activating downstream SHP-1/2-c-Src signalling [13–15]. Neuronal FcγRIIb expression facilitates the internalisation and neurotoxicity of amyloidogenic proteins, including α-Syn and β-amyloid aggregates [13, 23]. In parallel, engagement of microglial FcγRIIb by α-Syn fibrils in vitro suppresses phagocytic activity and by extension, clearance of extracellular debris like α-Syn, suggesting a dual systems role for FcγRIIb [14]. Interestingly, perturbation of the FcγRIIb-ITIM-SHP-1/2-c-Src axis via gene knockdown or pharmacological inhibition diminishes α-Syn uptake and its intercellular transmission [13, 15]. Together, this suggests that FcγRIIb signalling may be a critical mechanism in α-Syn propagation, although the functional relevance of FcγRIIb at the receptor level in vivo remains unresolved.

Here, we used the preformed fibril (PFF) α-Syn model to investigate the role of murine FcγRII (mFcγRII) towards α-Syn propagation in vivo using wild-type (WT) and mFcγRII-deficient (mFcγRII−/−) C57BL/6 mice [4]. The translational potential of targeting human FcγRIIb (hFcγRIIb) was also explored in transgenic mice expressing hFcγRIIb (hFcγRIIb Tg) [34, 37, 38] by using established clinical monoclonal antibodies (mAb) displaying either Fc-WT or N297Q mutant (Fc-Null) domains. Both mAb formats were designed to antagonise hFcγRIIb selectively, inhibiting ITIM activation and downstream signalling [34]. Herein, we report that both genetic knockout of mFcγRII and prophylactic hFcγRIIb blockade successfully mitigate α-Syn propagation in the PFF α-Syn mouse model.

Materials and methods

Mice

WT C57BL/6 (Charles River Laboratories), mFcγRII−/− (C57BL/6) [4], and hFcγRIIb± Tg (C57BL/6) mice [34] were bred and maintained at the Biomedical Research Facility (University of Southampton). hFcγRIIb Tg mice were intercrossed with mFcγRII−/− mice to eliminate endogenous mFcγRII expression. All experiments were performed in accordance with UK Home Office regulations (under PIL I84150147 and PPL PP6936815) and approved by the University of Southampton’s Animal Welfare and Ethical Review Body. Mice were maintained under standard housing conditions (21 ± 2 °C, 45–60% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) with environmental enrichment and ad libitum access to irradiated RM1 (E) diet and water. Mice were age-matched within experiments and groups assigned based on genotype and/or transgene expression [34].

Purification of recombinant α-Syn and preparation of PFF α-Syn

Recombinant human WT α-Syn was expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 RIPL pLacI cells transformed with a pET-α-Syn plasmid (kindly provided by Phil Williamson). Protein expression was induced with isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside and purified using Q-Sepharose anion exchange chromatography and gel filtration under denaturing conditions. Refolding was performed in an oxido-reduction buffer, followed by dialysis and re-purification on Q-Sepharose. Endotoxin removal was performed using 0.1% Triton X-114 [31]. Purified protein was buffer-exchanged into PBS, sterile-filtered, and stored at − 80 °C. Mono α-Syn (5 mg mL−1) was aggregated at 37 °C under continuous agitation (1000 rpm) for 7 days.

The resulting fibrils were fragmented into PFF particles (< 50 nm) via sonication in a cup-horn ultrasonic water bath (Fisherbrand™ Model 705). PFF formation was validated per preparation by transmission electron microscopy and Thioflavin-T Fluorimetry. To alleviate batch-effect inconsistency, the same batch of PFF α-Syn was used for intracranial injection across all mice used in this article. Human PFF α-Syn was used to model disease-relevant human fibrillar α-Syn species and because human PFF α-Syn has previously been shown to bind both mFcγRII and hFcγRIIb [13], enabling interrogation of conserved FcγRIIb-dependent mechanisms.

In vitro experiments

Primary neuron PFF α-Syn exposure

Cortical neurons were isolated from E15.5 WT or mFcγRII−/− C57BL/6 mouse embryos. Following dissection, tissue was enzymatically dissociated in 0.05% trypsin at 37 °C for 5 min, followed by gentle trituration to obtain a single-cell suspension. Cells were plated on poly-D-lysine-coated glass coverslips in 24-well plates and maintained in Neurobasal™ medium supplemented with 2% (v/v) B27™ and 0.5 mM GlutaMAX™ (Gibco) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. After 10 days, neurons were exposed to 1 µg PFF α-Syn per well and maintained for an additional 14 days. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate across four independent neuronal preparations.

Immunocytochemistry

Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB) at RT for 20 min and rinsed with PBS. Non-specific binding was blocked for 90 min using 10% normal goat serum (NGS) in blocking buffer (PBS containing 0.005% Triton X-100 and 0.05% thimerosal, pH 7.4), followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C in blocking buffer with 2% NGS and purified mouse anti-pSer129-α-Syn (BioLegend®). The following day, a biotinylated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (Vector Laboratories) was applied for 2 h at RT in blocking buffer with 2% NGS. Immunoreactivity was detected using streptavidin–horseradish peroxidase (ThermoFisher) and visualised with 0.05% 3, 3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) with 0.015% hydrogen peroxide. Coverslips were rinsed with PBS, detached, and mounted onto microscope slides with Mowiol® (Sigma).

In vivo experiments

PFF α-Syn model in WT and mFcγRII−/− mice

All surgical procedures were carried out under aseptic conditions. Adult male WT C57BL/6 and mFcγRII−/− mice (8 weeks old, n = 3–4 per group) were anaesthetised with isoflurane (inhaled) and positioned in a stereotaxic frame (KOPF Instruments). A Hamilton™ 10 µL syringe (GASTIGHT™ 1700 series) was used to unilaterally inject 5 µg PFF or Mono α-Syn (1 µL of 5 µg µL−1) at 0.4 µL min−1 at the following coordinates from Bregma: AP +0.4 mm, ML +2.0 mm, DV − 3.0 mm. The syringe was left in place for ≥ 5 min post-injection to facilitate diffusion before withdrawal. The incision was sutured and post-surgical care provided. Mice were euthanised at 30 or 90 dpi for immunohistochemical analysis.

Prophylactic hFcγRIIb mAb administration in the PFF α-Syn model

Adult hFcγRIIb Tg mice (12–16 weeks old, n = 6 per group) underwent unilateral stereotaxic surgery as described above. 5 μg (1 μL of 5 μg μL−1) PFF α-Syn was co-injected with 1 μg (1 μL of 1 μg μL−1) of an hIgG1 hFcγRIIb blocking mAb: Fc-WT (BI-1206) or Fc-Null (BI-1607), or an hIgG1 isocontrol mAb (cetuximab). In addition, antibodies were administered intraperitoneally (I.P.) at a dosage of 10 mg kg−1 to ensure sustained peripheral antibody load. I.P. injections were performed one day prior to surgery and again at 3 and 6 days post-surgery. One mouse from the Fc-Null group was culled early due to ill health following the surgical procedure. No other acute detrimental effect was observed following administration of the antibody in any of the other mice. Mice were euthanised 30 dpi for immunohistochemical analysis.

Behavioural testing

Mice (90 dpi cohort) underwent regular behavioural testing at monthly intervals prior to killing. Testing occurred 4 times in total: pre-surgery, 1, 2, and 3 months post-surgery. Mice were habituated to the procedure room and the investigator remained blind to experimental groups throughout.

Wire hang: The wire hang test of grip strength and motor coordination evaluates neuromuscular function in murine models [11]. A mouse was positioned on a 35 cm long, 2 mm diameter wire suspended 30 cm above a padded surface. The latency to fall off the wire was recorded and averaged across three trials with a maximum duration of 30 s per trial.

Pole test: The pole test assesses motor coordination and balance in mice [11]. A mouse was placed near the top of a 60 cm long, 1 cm diameter vertical pole. The time taken to turn and descend to the base was measured over three trials, with a maximum duration of 120 s per trial.

Rotarod test: The rotarod test is used to evaluate motor learning, coordination, and balance [18]. Prior to baseline assessments, mice were acclimated to the rotarod apparatus (Ugo Basile) for three 5 min trials at 10 revolutions per minute over two consecutive days. On the test day, mice were placed on the accelerating rotarod (ranging from 4 to 40 rpm over 300 s), and the latency to fall was recorded for a maximum of 5 min. Each mouse received three consecutive trials and the mean latency to fall was used for analysis.

Open field test: The open field test is a standardised assessment of locomotor activity and anxiety-related behaviour in rodents [11]. Mice were habituated to the open field arena for 10 min across two consecutive days prior to baseline assessments. On the test day, subjects were placed in the centre of a 76 cm × 76 cm × 50 cm square arena for 10 min, whilst locomotor activity was monitored using an automated infrared beam system (EthoVision). The initial 2 min of each session were excluded from analysis to account for initial excitability.

Tissue collection

Mice were terminally anaesthetised (Avertin®) and transcardially perfused with ice-cold heparinised saline (5 units mL−1), followed by 4% PFA in 0.1 M PB. Tissues were post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 4 h. For cryoprotection, brains were transferred to 30% sucrose in 0.1 M PB prior to sectioning. Coronal sections (40 μm) were obtained using a CM1860 UV cryostat (Leica) in 12-series and stored in antifreeze solution (30% ethylene glycol, 30% glycerol, 30% ddH2O, 10% 0.2 M PB) at − 20 °C until required. An additional cohort of naïve WT, mFcγRII−/−, and hFcγRIIb Tg mice (8–12 weeks old) were killed for RT-PCR analysis of brain and spleen tissue. These mice were terminally anaesthetised (Avertin®) and transcardially perfused with ice-cold heparinised saline. Brain tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C prior to homogenisation and RNA isolation.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed on 40-μm-thick free-floating coronal brain sections. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched (10% CH3OH, 3% H2O2 in ddH2O) for 10 min prior to blocking with 3% NGS in Tris-Buffered Saline (TBS) containing 0.2% Triton X-100 (TBS-T, pH 7.4) for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation at RT with primary antibodies (Table S1) in 1% NGS in TBS-T. Sections were incubated with biotin-conjugated secondary antibodies (Table S1) in TBS for 2 h, followed by an avidin–biotin complex amplification step (Vector Laboratories) for 90 min. Immunoreactivity was visualised using DAB peroxidase substrate. Chromogen development was halted in TBS and sections were mounted on gelatine-coated glass slides. Mayer’s haematoxylin (Sigma) was applied as a counterstain. Sections were sequentially dehydrated in a graded series of ethanol solutions (70%, 95%, and 100%), cleared in xylene, and cover-slipped with DPX (Sigma). The mFcγRII and hFcγRIIb stains (Fig. 1b, Fig. 5b) underwent an additional heat-induced antigen retrieval step in Tris–EDTA buffer (10 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, 0.05% Tween-20, pH 9.0) for 10 min at 90 °C prior to quenching.Fig. 1 Fcgr2b deletion reduces pα-Syn accumulation in primary neurons from mFcγRII−/− mice. a Agarose gel showing FcγR family RNA transcript expression in naïve mouse tissues. b Representative images of immunohistochemical mFcγRII staining in the SNpc from WT and mFcγRII−/− mice. Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescent IHC was performed on 40-μm-thick free-floating coronal brain or spleen sections. Blocking was with 3% NGS in TBS-T for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation at RT with primary antibodies (Table S1) in 1% NGS in TBS-T. Immunoreactivity was revealed using appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies (Table S1) conjugated to Alexa-fluor 488 or 568 (Invitrogen) in TBS for 2 h. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Merck) for 5 min. Stained sections were mounted on gelatine-coated glass slides and cover-slipped using Mowiol® (Sigma).

Reverse transcription quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from snap-frozen brain or spleen tissue using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesised from 400 ng of RNA using the TaqMan Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems™, Life Technologies). Quantitative PCR was performed using the StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems™, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with gene-specific primers (Table S1). All reactions were performed in technical duplicate. RT-PCR products were resolved by agarose gel electrophoresis and visualised using GelRed™ nucleic acid stain (Biotium) on a LI-COR Odyssey Fc imaging system to confirm amplicon size and specificity.

Image acquisition and analysis

Quantification of immunoreactivity was performed using unbiased design-based approaches. For primary neuronal cultures, five randomly selected fields of view were imaged per condition from each of three replicate coverslips (15 images in total). For tissue derived from in vivo experiments, three serial 40 µm coronal brain sections per animal encompassing regions of interest (ROIs) were selected for analysis. Within each section, images were acquired using a 40× objective from five randomly chosen fields of view per ROI (15 images per ROI per mouse). Quantification of staining was performed using automated thresholding of positive signal FIJI (ImageJ v1.53 h), measuring the percentage area of positive staining per field of view.

Distribution maps of pα-Syn+ immunoreactivity were generated ad hoc in FIJI to depict the average spatial distribution of Lewy-like pathology within each experimental group. Tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)+ neurons in the SNpc were manually counted from three serial 40 µm coronal section images captured at 20× magnification and corrected for total SNpc volume using the Cavalieri principle [30]. Optical density of TH immunoreactivity in the dorsal striatum was similarly measured in FIJI from three serial section images acquired at 10× magnification. All analyses were performed by an investigator blinded to experimental groups.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted and visualised with Prism™ v.10 (GraphPad), presented as bar charts with ± SEM or SD error bars and all data points. Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test where p>0.05 assumed normal distribution. Statistical analysis and post hoc comparisons were completed as indicated in figure legends. A significance threshold of p<0.05 was adopted for all statistical tests.

Results

Genetic deletion of Fcgr2b eliminates mFcγRII expression in the adult mouse brain

To establish a validated foundation for investigating the role of mFcγRII in α-Syn propagation within the CNS, we first sought to verify previous reports of mFcγRII expression in the adult WT mouse brain [22, 23, 28]. We evaluated mFcγRII expression in the brain of WT and homozygous mFcγRII−/− mice; the latter originally generated via targeted deletion of exons 4 and 5 of the Fcgr2b gene [4], resulting in a functional receptor knockout model. Examination of in situ hybridisation data from the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas (https://mouse.brain-map.org/) revealed diffuse yet discernible Fcgr2b mRNA signals throughout the WT C57BL/6 brain (Fig. S1). Consistent with these observations, RT-PCR analysis detected robust Fcgr2b transcript levels in WT brain and spleen homogenates (Fig. 1a), whilst Fcgr2b transcripts remained undetected in matched mFcγRII−/− mouse tissues (Fig. 1a).

Importantly, expression of other FcγR family members was unaffected by Fcgr2b deletion, confirming the selectivity of the knockout (Fig. 1a). At the protein level, immunodetection of mFcγRII in WT tissue sections demonstrated reproducible labelling across multiple brain regions, including the Substantia Nigra pars compacta (SNpc; Fig. 1b), which was confirmed in the spleen (Fig. 1c). No protein signal was observed in corresponding regions of mFcγRII−/− mice (Fig. 1b, c). Brain-resident Snca transcript levels and total α-Syn protein expression were also comparable between genotypes (Fig. S2). These data confirm that mFcγRII is expressed across the adult WT mouse brain, and its expression is abolished in mFcγRII−/− mice, establishing a robust genetic framework for functional studies of mFcγRII.

Phosphorylated (p) α-Syn is reduced in primary neurons from mFcγRII−/− mice

To complement previous in vitro studies using neuronal-like SH-SY5Y cells [13], and evaluate the role of mFcγRII in a physiologically relevant ex vivo system, we generated and applied human PFF α-Syn (Fig. S3) to primary cortical neurons derived from E15.5 WT and mFcγRII−/− embryos. Neurons were exposed to PFF at day 10, and the formation of pathological inclusions was assessed 14 days later (Fig. 1d) via immunostaining for α-Syn phosphorylated at serine 129 (pα-Syn), the predominant pathological modification of α-Syn [21]. Neuronal cultures from WT mice developed abundant pα-Syn-positive (pα-Syn+) immunoreactivity, localising to both perikarya and neuritic arbours (Fig. 1e). Under identical conditions, mFcγRII−/− neurons exhibited strongly reduced (88% less, n = 4) pα-Syn+ staining as compared to WT cultures (Fig. 1e, f). No pα-Syn+ signal was detected in control cultures treated with bovine serum albumin, confirming the specificity of the PFF-induced pα-Syn amplification (Fig. S4).

Loss of mFcγRII attenuates seeding and propagation of α-Syn in vivo

To determine the impact of mFcγRII deletion on the initiation and propagation of α-Syn pathology in vivo, we used the PFF α-Syn model of synucleinopathy in mice, which recapitulates cardinal features of α-Syn seeding and LB expansion [25, 42]. Male WT and mFcγRII−/− mice (2-month-old, n = 3–4 per group) received unilateral stereotaxic injections of human PFF α-Syn (Fig. S3) or non-fibrillar control Mono α-Syn into the dorsal striatum. Cohort size was determined prior to the study via pilot investigations of PFF injection in WT and mFcγRII−/− mice (Fig. S5). In WT mice, PFF injection induced robust pα-Syn+ LB-like pathology as early as 30 dpi, localised to the ipsilateral (ipsi; side of injection) SNpc. In contrast, mFcγRII−/− mice exhibited a near-complete absence of pα-Syn immunostaining at 30 dpi (Fig. 2b). Representative images of all regions of interest analysed at 30 dpi are presented in Figure S5a, revealing minimal pα-Syn+ immunoreactivity outside of the SNpc across all experimental groups (Fig. S6b). Notably, no pα-Syn+ signal was observed in WT or mFcγRII−/− mice injected with Mono α-Syn at 30 dpi (Fig. S6a) or 90 dpi (Fig. 2c, Fig. S7a), confirming the specificity of the PFF-seeding response [25]. By 90 dpi following PFF, pα-Syn+ inclusions and neurites were abundant and widely distributed in the WT mouse brain, localising predominantly to the ipsi hemisphere. Brain regions with high pα-Syn+ immunoreactivity included the ipsi prefrontal cortex (PFC), injection site (striatum), primary motor cortex (M1), SNpc (Fig. 2c), entorhinal cortex (EC), and amygdala (Amy) (Fig. S7a). Analogous contralateral (cont) regions also exhibited muted pα-Syn deposition at 90 dpi (Fig. 2c, Fig. S7a), consistent with reported examples of trans-hemispheric propagation in the unilateral PFF injection model [25, 35].

Remarkably, mFcγRII−/− mice injected with PFF exhibited sustained and profound suppression of pα-Syn+ immunostaining across all examined regions at 90 dpi. Sparse pα-Syn+ inclusions emerged in a subset of mice in select regions like the ipsi SNpc and Amy, although the overall pathological burden and its anatomical distribution were nominal as compared to WT mice (Fig. 2c, Fig. S7a). Quantitative image analysis revealed a significant reduction in total pα-Syn+ area (% coverage) across all examined brain regions in mFcγRII−/− mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 2d, Fig. S7b). Reductions ranged from greater than 70% in the ipsi SNpc to almost total abrogation (~ 99%) in the ipsi PFC and M1 (Fig. 2d), indicating impaired propagation of α-Syn along synaptically connected pathways. Enlarged images of ipsi SNpc-resident pα-Syn+ staining are shown in Fig. S8. At 90 dpi, we observed a ~72% reduction in Iba1+ microglial density and a 29% decrease in FcγRI expression within the ipsi striatum relative to WT counterparts (Fig. 3b, c). Nigrostriatal dopaminergic integrity is preserved in mFcγRII−/− mice

The PFF model is well established for recapitulating progressive neurodegeneration, with selective vulnerability of dopaminergic neurons in the SNpc leading to behavioural deficits beginning around 90 dpi [25]. We therefore examined whether mFcγRII deletion mitigated PFF-induced dopaminergic dysfunction, whereby TH was assessed as a marker of catecholaminergic neuron integrity [17]. At 90 dpi, WT mice injected with PFF exhibited a marked reduction in TH+ fibre density within the ipsi dorsal striatum (Fig. 4a, c), accompanied by a significant loss of TH+ neuronal somata in the ipsi SNpc (Fig. 4b, d) relative to both Mono-injected controls (Fig. 4a-d) and the 30 dpi cohort (Fig. S10). By contrast, mFcγRII−/− mice showed comparable TH+ fibre density and neuronal soma counts following either PFF or Mono injection at both 30 dpi (Fig. S10) and 90 dpi (Fig. 4a-d). As a functional correlate of nigrostriatal preservation, behavioural assays were conducted prior to killing at 90 dpi. WT mice injected with PFF showed changes consistent with motor and exploratory impairment, including reduced locomotor activity and exploratory drive, presenting as diminished ambulation and spontaneous rearing in the open field test (Fig. S11a). WT PFF mice also showed increased descent latency in the pole test (Fig. S11b). On the contrary, mFcγRII−/− mice maintained normal performance across all behavioural paradigms (Fig. S11a–d), performing equivalently to Mono α-Syn-injected mice. Given the small cohort size, these behavioural data are presented as suggestive only. Nonetheless, their directionality is consistent with the TH findings and supports the interpretation that preservation of nigrostriatal integrity in mFcγRII−/− mice may be accompanied by protection from motor-related dysfunction in the PFF model.

Expression of the hFcγRIIb transgene, along with the absence of mFcγRII, was confirmed in the brain via RT-PCR detection of FCGR2B transcripts in naïve hFcγRIIb Tg mouse brain homogenates (Fig. 5a). A mixed-sex cohort of adult hFcγRIIb Tg mice received intracranial co-administration of 5 µg PFF α-Syn together with 1 µg of an hFcγRIIb-specific mAb (n = 6 per group). Two hFcγRIIb blocking mAbs (hIgG1) were tested, incorporating either Fc-WT or Fc-Null (N297Q variant) domains [34, 37]. Inclusion of both variants enabled resolution between effects arising solely from hFcγRIIb blockade from those additionally influenced by Fc-mediated engagement of activating FcγRs on neighbouring immune cells. A hIgG1 isotype (iso) control mAb served as a comparator, and additional dosing (10 mg kg−1, I.P.) maintained circulating antibody levels (Fig. 5d).

At 30 dpi, immunohistochemical staining for pα-Syn revealed a near-complete abrogation of pα-Syn+ inclusions in the brain of hFcγRIIb blocking mAb-treated mice. Quantitative assessment demonstrated a ~90% decrease in pα-Syn+ burden within the ipsi striatum and SNpc of Fc-WT and Fc-Null mice relative to hIgG1 iso-treated controls (Fig. 5e-g). No ectopic pα-Syn immunoreactivity was detected elsewhere in the brain across all treatment groups, consistent with the restricted, early-stage distribution of pα-Syn mirrored in WT mice at 30 dpi (Fig. S6). These data suggest that prophylactic hFcγRIIb blockade effectively suppresses early-stage α-Syn pathology, consistent with suppression of uptake and pathologic templating at the injection site, inhibiting subsequent spread to anatomically connected regions like the SNpc.

Discussion

The cell-to-cell transmission of pathological α-Syn aggregates constitutes a fundamental mechanism driving the progression of PD and related synucleinopathies [40, 41]. Previous studies have convincingly shown that aggregated α-Syn binds directly to FcγRIIb in vitro, mediating uptake through lipid raft-dependent endocytic pathways [13, 15]. Moreover, receptor knockdown strategies undertaken during these investigations mitigated α-Syn uptake in vitro. However, the contribution of FcγRIIb to the in vivo dynamics governing propagation of α-Syn pathology has remained unexplored. Here, employing the PFF model [25], we demonstrate that mFcγRII, the murine orthologue of hFcγRIIb, sharing 52% identity within the extracellular ligand-binding domain (https://www.uniprot.org/align/), serves as a critical determinant of α-Syn seeding competence and the trans-neuronal propagation of pathology across the mouse brain.

Importantly, other cell-surface receptors have been implicated in α-Syn uptake. For example, LAG3 deletion reduces neuronal internalisation, delays pathology in vivo, and protects dopamine neurons [27], whilst APLP1 loss similarly diminishes propagation and neurotoxicity [26]. Other candidates, including the α3 subunit of Na+/K+ ATPase [36] and connexin 32 [32], also remain under investigation. This indicates that α-Syn fibrils may engage a diverse repertoire of receptors, akin to Aβ, highlighting the potential for multiple, possibly overlapping uptake pathways. Nonetheless, the overt reduction in pα-Syn burden observed in mFcγRII−/− mice suggests that FcγRIIb represents a mechanistically distinct and influential conduit for α-Syn propagation. Whilst differences in experimental design preclude direct quantitative comparison with LAG3 knockout studies [27], our findings indicate that mFcγRII contributes substantially to the efficiency of α-Syn propagation and resulting dopaminergic deficits in vivo.

The absence of mFcγRII was also associated with a markedly dampened microglial response to PFF. Although FcγRIIb is conventionally considered an inhibitory receptor that restrains immune activation [29, 33], its deletion did not exacerbate microgliosis in the model (Fig. 3). Compared to WT mice, mFcγRII−/− mice exhibited insubstantial Iba1 and FcγRI expression at both 30 dpi and 90 dpi. This likely reflects the diminished α-Syn burden observed in these mice, consonant with evidence that microglial responses scale with extracellular aggregate burden [19]. These observations may suggest that mFcγRII’s primary contribution to α-Syn pathology in vivo is neuron-intrinsic, with reduced fibril internalisation limiting secondary glial activation. However, further investigation is warranted to dissect the acute vs. chronic immune consequences of mFcγRII deletion in the context of α-Syn-induced neuroinflammation, along with the known role of microglial mFcγRII in anti-phagocytic mechanisms in vivo [14].

To evaluate the translational potential of this axis, we implemented a prophylactic mAb blockade strategy using two hFcγRIIb mAbs, which selectively antagonise hFcγRIIb with high specificity [34, 38]. Both the Fc-WT and Fc-Null variants successfully impaired acute α-Syn seeding competence in the striatum (site of injection), thereby disrupting downstream pα-Syn accumulation in the SNpc of hFcγRIIb Tg mice at 30 dpi (Fig. 5). The comparable efficacy of the Fc-WT and Fc-Null mAbs suggests that blockade is mediated predominantly via steric interference with the putative α-Syn fibril-binding interface on hFcγRIIb. Although the precise PFF-hFcγRIIb docking site has yet to be structurally resolved, it is thought to involve both extracellular Ig-like domains [13], and partial overlap with the mAb-binding groove could explain the observed efficacy of receptor blockade. By acting directly on hFcγRIIb, these antibodies may prevent α-Syn-hFcγRIIb binding and uptake, exploiting a mechanistically distinct pathway from conventional anti-α-Syn immunotherapies. This could offer a novel and perhaps complementary approach to suppress neuronal uptake and subsequent seeding of pathogenic α-Syn.

The mechanistic dissociation of Fc-WT and Fc-Null hFcγRIIb mAbs also highlights the translational versatility afforded by antibody engineering strategies to minimise off-target immune activation. For instance, the Fc-WT mAb is shown to elicit B cell depletion via engagement with activating FcγRs, whereas the Fc-Null variant, incorporating an N297Q substitution, retains potent hFcγRIIb blockade without triggering this adverse effect [34, 38]. Such Fc modifications are increasingly exploited in therapeutic antibody development, though parameters like antibody half-life, neonatal Fc receptor interactions, and residual low-affinity FcγR binding must be carefully considered in future translational efforts [8]. Importantly, the mAbs used herein are already advancing in oncology clinical trials [1], establishing a robust safety, tolerability, and pharmacodynamic footprint that strongly positions them for rapid repurposing towards neurodegenerative indications.

Several limitations of our study warrant consideration. First, our initial study using mFcγRII−/− mice was performed exclusively in male animals to minimise biological variability in this proof-of-concept investigation. Given the growing recognition of sex-specific immune responses in PD [5], incorporation of both sexes is an important consideration for future studies. Second, analyses were performed using small cohort sizes. Accordingly, additional studies using larger cohorts, particularly for behavioural analyses, will therefore be required to confirm and extend these observations, including comprehensive assessment of motor coordination as well as non-motor manifestations such as depression, anxiety, and cognitive impairment.

Third, although TH immunoreactivity provides an established readout of nigrostriatal dopaminergic integrity, the absence of an independent pan-neuronal counterstain such as NeuN or Nissl precludes definitive discrimination between frank neuronal loss, reduced TH expression, and dopaminergic neuronal dysfunction. Finally, whilst intracerebral mAb delivery affords proof-of-concept mechanistic dissection, it does not recapitulate the pharmacokinetics or biodistribution of systemic administration, critical for clinical translation. Moreover, our prophylactic paradigm does not address therapeutic intervention at stages of established pathology, which would be essential to reflect the clinical context of patients with PD.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that FcγRIIb is a pivotal mediator of α-Syn propagation in vivo, acting as a fibril uptake pathway that dictates the efficiency of early α-Syn seeding and downstream consequences. Targeted hFcγRIIb blockade using mAbs effectively disrupts acute pα-Syn deposition, representing a mechanistically precise, potentially disease-modifying strategy. When integrated with the existing clinical precedent for FcγRIIb-directed therapeutics in oncology [1, 24, 34], these data support the feasibility of repurposing or optimising receptor-specific interventions to arrest the progression of α-synucleinopathies.
