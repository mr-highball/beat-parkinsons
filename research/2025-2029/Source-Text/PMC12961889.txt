Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic inflammatory disease of the central nervous system (CNS) characterized by immune-mediated demyelination and axonal injury. Recent works have highlighted the critical role of myeloid cells in MS, contributing to both neurodegeneration and repair. FK506-binding protein 5 (FKBP5), a co-chaperone of the glucocorticoid receptor and regulator of stress responses, has been implicated in MS pathology, yet its mechanistic role remains poorly understood. Here, we show that FKBP5 expression is elevated in monocytes and dendritic cells from the cerebrospinal fluid of people with MS and enriched in microglial nodules. Additionally, we show that genetic ablation or pharmacological inhibition of Fkbp5 in microglia and macrophages enhances myelin uptake and degradation via phagolysosomal pathways and attenuates interferon-gamma (IFNg)-induced transcriptional programs. In the experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) model of MS, systemic treatment with the selective FKBP5 inhibitor SAFit2 reduced immune cell infiltration, dampened microglial activation, and suppressed IFNg signaling, resulting in improved clinical outcomes. Taken together, our data identify FKBP5 as a critical mediator of myeloid cell-driven neuroinflammation, and a promising therapeutic target in MS.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic immune-mediated disease of the central nervous system (CNS), typically beginning with relapsing–remitting episodes and progressing to irreversible neurological decline [1, 2]. While traditionally viewed as a T cell-driven disorder, mounting evidence highlights the central role of CNS-resident and infiltrating myeloid cells, such as microglia and monocyte-derived macrophages, in MS pathogenesis [3]. These cells dominate the immune landscape of MS lesions, shaping their spatial organization and reflecting disease activity [4]. In a preclinical model of MS, depletion of microglia and macrophages, genetically or by CSF1R blockade, ameliorates disease [3, 5, 6], whereas enhancing their phagocytic or metabolic capacity promotes remyelination [7, 8]. Despite their key contributions to neuroinflammation, currently no approved therapies target myeloid populations, underscoring the need to identify regulators of their function.

Recent advances in single-cell and single-nucleus transcriptomics have expanded our understanding of myeloid cell heterogeneity in MS, revealing disease-associated subsets and candidate therapeutic pathways. In two independent studies profiling peripheral blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from MS cohorts, FK506-binding protein 5 (FKBP5) emerged as one of the most consistently upregulated genes in myeloid populations [9, 10]. These findings suggest FKBP5 is a potential regulator of myeloid dysfunction in MS and prompt deeper mechanistic investigation in both experimental and human systems.

FKBP5 is a critical co-chaperone of the glucocorticoid receptor (GR) complex, where it modulates stress responsiveness by reducing ligand affinity, delaying GR nuclear translocation, and suppressing glucocorticoid-driven transcriptional activity [11–13]. Dysregulated GR signaling is a hallmark of stress-related conditions, including major depressive, bipolar, and post-traumatic stress disorders [14]. Genome-wide association studies have consistently linked FKBP5 variants to susceptibility across a spectrum of psychiatric conditions [15–18]. Notably, psychiatric disorders are highly prevalent in people with MS (pwMS) compared to healthy subjects, where they contribute to increased disease burden and worse long-term outcomes [11, 19]. Beyond its role in stress physiology, FKBP5 has emerged as a molecular regulator of innate immune cell activation in the inflamed CNS. Recent studies demonstrate that FKBP5 deletion in microglia dampens NF-κB–dependent inflammation in response to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) exposure [20], while its loss in the cuprizone model of CNS myelin injury confers protection against demyelination but impairs remyelination, potentially via disrupted PPARγ-mediated mitophagy [21]. These findings, together with the observed upregulation of FKBP5 in MS-associated myeloid populations, position FKBP5 as a key regulator in the CNS inflammation and repair, particularly within myeloid lineages.

Despite growing interest in FKBP5 as a stress-response gene and immunoregulatory factor, its precise role in MS pathophysiology remains undefined. Here, we investigate the function of FKBP5 in myeloid cells during neuroinflammation using the experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), a preclinical model of MS. Our work aims to bridge the mechanistic link between stress biology, innate immunity, and neuroinflammation in MS.

Methods

Mice and study approval

Animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Barrow Neurological Institute (BNI) (protocols 622, 625). All experiments were performed on C57BL/6J female mice that were obtained from Jaxson Laboratories and housed at the BNI vivarium. Mice were 6–8 weeks old at the initiation of these studies.

Experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis

EAE was induced in C57BL/6 J female mice via subcutaneous immunization with 50ug of myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein 35–55 (MOG35-55) (Genscript) in an emulsion mix with Incomplete Freund’s Adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich) containing 50 µg of heat-killed Mycobacterium tuberculosis (BD Difco). All animals were injected intravenously with a total of 300 ng pertussis toxin derived from Bordetella pertussis (List Biologics) at the time of, and 48 h following, immunization [22]. After immunization, mice were evaluated clinically in a blinded fashion using a disease severity scale that iss scored on a 5-point scale: 0 = no disease; 1 = tail weakness; 2 = paraparesis; 3 = paraplegia; 4 = paraplegia with forelimb weakness or paralysis; 5 = moribund or dead animal, as described [23]. Vehicle and SAFit2 (MedChemExpress) were injected i.p. for five days, starting at 7 days post-immunization (DPI). SAFit2 was solubilized at 20 mg/kg in vehicle containing 4% ethanol, 5% Tween80, and 5% PEG400 in 0.9% saline.

Isolation of CNS, lymph nodes and spleen inflammatory cells

Brain and spinal cord (SC) tissues from mice perfused with PBS were digested with DNAse I (Sigma) in HBSS (Cytiva) with a dounce homogenizer. The homogenized mixture was passed through a 70um strainer, washed, centrifuged and resuspended in Percoll gradient (Sigma). After the gradient CNS mononuclear cells were retrieved from the 37/70% Percoll layer. Spleen and lymph nodes were lysed using Red blood cell lysis buffer (Sigma) and resuspended in 1X PBS with 2% of FBS. Cells were counted and divided at a minimum of 8 × 105 cells for flow cytometry staining.

Flow cytometry

Cell suspensions were resuspended in PBS + 2% FBS buffer and labeled with a combination of the following conjugated antibodies for surface staining: CD45-AF700 (clone 30-F11), Anti-P2RY12-APC (clone S16007D), CD115-APC/Cy-7 (clone AFS98), CD38-APC/Fire 810 (clone 90), CD11b-BV 421 (clone M1/70), CD4-BV 570 (RM4-5), F4/80-BV605 (clone BM8), Ly-6G-BV 650 (clone 1A8), MHCII-BV711 (clone M5/114.15.2), PD-L1-BV785 (clone 10F.9G2), CD86-Pacific Blue (clone GL-1), CD206-PE (clone C068C2), CD11c-PE/Cy-5 (clone N418), Ly-6C-PE/Cy-7 (clone HK1.4), CD19-Spark Blue 550 (clone 6D5), CD8a-Spark Nir 685 (53–6.7), CD3-FITC (clone 145-2C11), CD38-APC Fire 810 (clone 90), and Live-Zombie Aqua. For intracellular staining we used CD44-BV650 (clone IM7), CD62L-FITC (clone W18021D), IL-17A-BV785 (clone TC-18M10.1), GM-CSF-APC (clone MPI-22F9), and IFNg-PE (clone XMG1.2) (all antibodies come from Biolegend). Dead cells were excluded by selecting the Zombie Aqua Viability Dye negative cells. FACS analysis was performed on a FACS Cytek Aurora machine (Cytek Biosciences), and data were analyzed with FlowJo v10.8.1 Software (BD Life Sciences).

Quantitative real-time PCR

RNA was extracted using the QIAzol lysis reagent (Qiagen) and chloroform (Sigma) for the denaturation step and isopropyl alcohol (Sigma) for the precipitation step. RNA was checked for concentration and purity using the NanoDrop ND-1000; upon adequate purity and concentration obtained, RNA was converted into cDNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems), and used at 20 ng in quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis. The delta-delta-Ct (ddCt) method was applied to determine differences in gene expression levels after normalization to the arithmetic mean of GAPDH as internal standards. The TaqMan probes used are the following for mouse studies: Gapdh (Mm99999915_g1); Fkbp5 (Mm00487406_m1); Cxcl10 (Mm00445235_m1); C4b (Mm00437893_g1). Real-time PCR was performed using the Applied Biosystems StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System.

Corticosterone ELISA

Serum corticosterone was determined using the enzyme immunoassay kit (DetectX ELISA, K014-H; Arbor Assays) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The standard curve ranged from 0.039–10 ng/mL. Mouse serum samples (5μL) were diluted 1:100 with supplied reagents prior to assay and ran in duplicate. Serum corticosterone levels were analyzed using a Four Parameter Logistic (4PL) curve fit using the Arbor Assays online tool, MyAssays.

Histological and immunofluorescence (IF) microscopy analyses

Paraffin spinal cord tissue slides were stained and quantify for H&E, Luxol fast blue (LFB) and immunofluorescence (IF) as previously done [24, 25]. Briefly, tissue underwent antigen retrieval in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer pH 6.0 and permeabilized with 1% horse serum in PBST for 10 min at RT, then blocked for non-specific binding with 5% horse serum in PBS-T for 1 h at RT. Tissue was then incubated with primary antibody accordingly, (goat anti-Iba1 1:250 (NB 100–1028), mouse anti-FKBP5 1:500 (NB110-96873), rat anti-CD3 1:100 (14–0032–82), rat anti-MHC II 1:200 (14–5321-82), rat anti—myelin basic protein (MBP) 1:200 (ab7349) overnight at 4ºC. Day 2, slides were washed with PBS and secondary antibody was added accordingly, (donkey anti-goat AF 555 1:500 (ab150130), rat anti-mouse AF488 1:500 (Biolegend), donkey anti-rat AF 488 1:500 (ab150149), donkey ant-rat AF 488 (ab150149)) and incubated at RT for 2 h. Slides were washed in 1% horse serum in PBST for 10 min. The slides were then mounted using DAPI mounting media (Thermofisher), visualized and imaged with the EVOS M7000 digital microscope.

Western Blot

Protein concentrations were determined by micro-BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Lysates (10–20 μg) were prepared in 5X Laemmli buffer. Samples were boiled for 10 min at 95 °C and run on a 4–12% Bis–Tris Gel at 100 V for 2.5 h in MOPS SDS Running Buffer (Invitrogen). Semi-dry transfer was performed by Power Blotter XL System (Invitrogen) for 45 min in NuPAGE transfer buffer (Invitrogen) with 20% methanol onto a 0.2 μm PVDF membrane (Cytiva). After blocking in 5% milk in TBST, primary antibodies FKBP5 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology (CST), 12210S, ULK (1:1000; CST, 8054), pULK (1:1000; CST, 5869), AKT (1:1000; CST, 9272), or LC3B (1:200; Santa Cruz Biotech, sc-376404) were incubated overnight (ON) in 5% milk in TBST at 4 °C. Membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000; CST, 7074S) and anti-mouse IgG HRP-linked (1:5000) at RT for 1 h in 5% milk TBST solution. Protein bands were detected via Chemiluminescent Detection Substrate (Sigma). A LI-COR Fc Imager was used for imaging and ImageJ software was used for analysis.

Isolation and culture of bone marrow macrophages

Bone marrow cells were isolated from femurs and tibias. Cells were incubated in RPMI-1640 with 2 mM L-Glutamine, containing 5% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and supplemented with 1% PenStrep and 50 ng/mL M-CSF (Biolegend). The medium was replenished after 72 h. Cells were ready for experiments on day 5.

Primary microglia culture

Primary microglia were isolated according to the published method [26] with few modifications. Briefly, primary cortical glial cultures were generated from postnatal day 2 mouse pups (C57BL/6 background) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. After 2 to 5 days, the media were replaced with fresh DMEM containing granulocyte M-CSF (2 ng/ml). Once microglia were abundant on top of the astrocytic monolayer, they were detached by orbital shaking at 200 rpm for 2 h at 37 °C. Cell pellets were collected by centrifugation at 440 g for 5 min, resuspended in a 1:1 ratio of fresh astrocyte culture media/old media, and seeded into chamber slides. The next day, the media were replaced with microglia treatment media supplemented with M-CSF (10 ng/ml). Primary microglia were treated with 10 μM SaFit2.

shBV2, BV2, and bone marrow-derived macrophage (BMDM) treatment

BMDMs were cultured at a density of 1.5 × 106 cells per well in 1 mL of complete RPMI-1640 (5% FBS, 1% PenStrep, 1% L-Glutamine). 10uM was used for SAFit2 treatment. A concentration of 10 ng/mL was used for LPS treatment and for cortisol treatment. 20 ng/mL was used for IFNg treatment. BV2 and shBV2 were cultured at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells per well in 1 mL of complete DMEM (10% FBS, 1% PenStrep, 1% L-Glutamine). 10 μM was used for SAFit2 treatment. A concentration of 10 ng/mL was used for LPS treatment and 100 ng/mL for cortisol treatment. 10–20 ng/mL was used for IFNg treatment.

Myelin purification and CFSE labeling

Myelin was isolated and purified by ultracentrifugation as established by the referenced protocol [27]. Myelin debris was thawed and pelleted at 14,800 g for 10 min at 4ºC and then resuspended in 2 mL sterile PBS. 1μL of 5 mM CFSE (Thermofisher) was added for a final concentration of 2.5 μM. The mixture was incubated at RT for 30 min protected from light. The myelin was pelleted and then washed 3X with 0.2 µm filter sterilized 100 mM glycine in PBS wash buffer. All myelin in vitro experiments hereafter used a concentration of 5 ug/mL.

BMDM, primary microglia and BV2 myelin uptake and degradation

BMDM or BV2 cell lines treated with or without SAFit2 were incubated with 5ug/mL CFSE myelin for different time points at 37ºC 5% CO2 for uptake analysis. Cells were washed with 2% FACS buffer to remove non-engulfed myelin debris and transferred into FACS tubes. Cells were stained with CD45-AF700, CD11b-BV421 and analyzed on the FACS Cytek Aurora machine (Cytek Biosciences). For degradation studies, BMDM or BV2 cell lines treated with or without SAFit2 were incubated with 5 ug/mL CFSE myelin for 2 h at 37ºC 5% CO2. Cells were then washed with 1X PBS to remove non-engulfed myelin debris. Fresh media was added to each well and then put back in the incubator. After different time points, cells were stained and analyzed as described above. Primary microglia myelin phagocytosis was assessed at the 24 h time point.

Biolegend legend plex

Serum cytokine analysis for CXCL10 and IFNg was determined using the commercially available fluorescent bead-based assay kit LEGENDplex (Biolegend) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum cytokine levels were analyzed using a Five Parameter Logistic (5PL) curve fit using Biolegend’s LEGENDplex software.

RNA-Seq data processing and differential expression analysis

BV2 and BMDM were lysed, and RNA isolation was prepared according to the Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74,104). Sample was checked for concentration and purity on the NanoDrop ND-1000 before sequencing. RNA sequencing data from multiple experimental comparisons were analyzed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) under various treatment conditions. Raw count matrices corresponding to distinct sample groups (e.g., SAFit2-treated vs BMDM control, shCTR vs sh96, and shCTR vs sh99) were imported into R (R version 2024.12.1) and processed separately using DESeq2—1.42.1. For each dataset, genes with low expression (total counts across samples ≤ 50) were filtered out to reduce noise. Sample conditions were defined as factors reflecting the experimental groups (e.g., treatment vs control), with the control condition set as the reference level. Differential expression analysis was conducted following the DESeq2 pipeline, including normalization, dispersion estimation, and model fitting based on the negative binomial distribution. Significantly DEGs were identified using thresholds of adjusted p-value (Benjamini–Hochberg correction) < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change ≥ 1. Volcano plots summarizing DEG distributions were generated using the EnhancedVolcano package with consistent cutoff values for adjusted p-value and fold change across comparisons.

Pseudobulk RNA-seq from blood and CSF compendium

Raw count matrices and corresponding metadata for people with MS (pwMS) and healthy control (HCs) from CSF and blood samples were extracted from an integrated Seurat object generated in our previously published compendium [28]. Raw counts were combined at the sample levels. All analyses were conducted in R version 2024.12.1, using Seurat—5.3.0, DESeq2—1.42.1, and limma—3.58.1. To reduce the impact of low-abundance transcripts, genes were retained if they exhibited at least 10 raw counts in a minimum of 5 samples (rowSums(counts(dds) ≥ 10) ≥ 5). Sample metadata, including condition (HCs, MS), disease classification, and study identifiers, were encoded as factors prior to dataset construction. Count matrices were rounded to integer values as required, and a DESeq2 dataset was constructed using DESeqDataSetFromMatrix(), with the design formula ~ study + condition to adjust for study-related batch effects. The condition variable was re-leveled to use "HC" as the reference group. For exploratory analyses, variance-stabilizing transformation (vst) was applied directly to the raw counts using the vst() function, and residual batch effects were corrected using limma::removeBatchEffect() based on the study covariate. Correction efficacy was evaluated through principal component analysis (PCA) before and after adjustment. Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 starting from the raw counts using the Wald test, and p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Data quality was assessed through inspection of dispersion estimates and PCA distributions stratified by condition.

Publicly available single-nuclei and bulk RNA-sequencing data

Data in raw count table format or Matrix were obtained from published studies: single nuclei by Absinta et al., from GSE180759 [29] and processed bulk RNA-sequencing data published by van den Bosch et al., from GSE234700 [30].

Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

To determine which pathways were significantly enriched in key cell types, genes with FDR < 0.1 or FDR < 0.05 were analyzed using the Ingenuity Pathway Analysis software (Qiagen, version 107,193,442) or the GSEA website, respectively.

Statistics

For all continuous outcome measures, data distribution was first evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test. When all groups met normality assumptions, parametric tests were applied: Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test for analyses in more than two groups. If one or more groups failed normality testing, or when sample sizes were limited, we used the corresponding non-parametric tests: Mann–Whitney U test for two groups or Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc correction for multiple groups. EAE experiments were analyzed separately for disease incidence, day of onset and disease severity. Cumulative clinical score was the sum of all scores from onset to the end of the experiment and were summarized using the mean ± SEM. Differences in disease incidence between treatment groups were analyzed by Fisher’s exact test. Disease clinical course was analyzed by two-way ANOVA in which the two factors were time and group. A P ≤ 0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10.4.1 and R-Studio.

Results

FKBP5 is upregulated in CSF monocytes and dendritic cells from MS patients

FKBP5 is classically associated with stress-related disorders [15–17], and its dysregulation has also been reported in MS [31, 32]. However, its functional role in MS-related neuroinflammation remains largely undefined. To investigate FKBP5 expression in immune cells involved in the MS-associated immune response, we leveraged our recently published single-cell transcriptomic compendium from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and CSF of neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative diseases [28]. We first examined FKBP5 expression across the major immune cell populations, including CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, B cells, natural killer cells, plasmacytoid dendritic cells, and myeloid cells. Consistent with previous reports [33] we found that FKBP5 was primarily expressed in both PBMCs and CSF by CD4 + T cells and myeloid cell clusters (Supplemental Fig. 1A).

To assess MS-associated transcriptional changes, we performed pseudo-bulk differential gene expression analysis in CD4 + and myeloid cell clusters of pwMS and HCs in the same dataset. FKBP5 emerged as one of the most significantly upregulated genes in the myeloid cluster from both PBMCs and CSF of pwMS (Supplementary Fig. 1B), with marked increases in CD14 + monocytes (padj = 0.04, log2FC = 1.2) and CD36⁺ conventional dendritic cell 2 (cDC2) (padj = 0.001, log2FC = 1.1) relative to HCs (Fig. 1A-E and Supplemental Fig. 1C, D). By contrast, FKBP5 expression remained unchanged in the CD4 + T cell cluster between pwMS and HCs (Supplemental Fig. 1E). Given the central role of CSF in MS diagnosis, we conducted pathway analyses on CSF-derived immune cell clusters that showed significant FKBP5 upregulation: CD14 + monocytes and CD36 + cDC2. Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) of the CD14⁺ monocyte transcriptome revealed enrichment in interferon-gamma signaling and macrophage activation pathways, consistent with a pro-inflammatory profile in MS myeloid cells [34] (Fig. 1F). These findings extend prior observations of FKBP5 upregulation in MS [9, 10], identifying monocytes and cDC2s as key cellular sources of FKBP5 in the CSF compartment. The enrichment of interferon pathways in FKBP5-upregulated myeloid cells highlights a tight link between FKBP5 and the interferon-driven inflammation that defines the MS CNS compartment.

FKBP5 is upregulated in microglia nodules from MS brain tissue

To contextualize FKBP5 findings across CNS cells, including microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and neurons, we analyzed publicly available transcriptomic datasets from healthy mouse and human brain tissue. These analyses revealed that FKBP5 expression is strikingly enriched in microglia and macrophages, with minimal expression in other CNS-resident cell types (Supplemental Fig. 1F) [35]. Given the prominent role of microglia in MS pathology, we next examined bulk RNA-seq data from post-mortem MS brain samples. Inflammatory HLA-DR⁺ microglia and infiltrating macrophages are hallmarks of active MS lesions, where they accumulate in nodular structures thought to precede lesion formation and mediate axonal damage and complement-dependent phagocytosis (GEO234700) [25]. Analysis of microglia from pwMS revealed significant FKBP5 upregulation in microglial nodules compared to normal-appearing white matter (NAWM) microglia from the same individuals (Fig. 1H), suggesting that FKBP5 dysregulation may be specific to HLA-DR⁺ ramified microglia.

To further define FKBP5 regulation across different MS lesions, we re-analyzed the single nuclei RNA-seq data from GSE180759. Considering all cells, FKBP5 was significantly upregulated in chronic-active lesion rims compared with inactive or lesion-core regions (Fig. 1I). This upregulation in chronic active lesion rims was primarily driven by microglia inflamed in MS (MIMS), a dominant glial population enlarged in MS lesion rim [29, 36] (Fig. 1J).

Together, these results support the hypothesis that FKBP5 is selectively upregulated in myeloid cells across peripheral and CNS compartments in MS, including lesion-associated MIMS and microglia nodules, underscoring the need to dissect its mechanistic role in shaping neuroinflammatory responses in the disease.

Fkbp5 is upregulated in microglia and macrophages from the spinal cord of diverse MS animal models

To leverage the MOG35-55 EAE model [37] for in vivo functional studies, we first investigated FKBP5 protein levels via western blot analysis of Percoll-isolated immune cells from the CNS of naïve and EAE mice at different time points. C57BL/6 mice were immunized with the MOG35-55 and brain and SC were isolated (Fig. 2A). FKBP5 protein was upregulated in the SC during both the effector (18 DPI) and chronic (28 DPI) phases of EAE compared to naïve SC tissue (Fig. 2B). Similarly, brain tissue from mice with EAE showed increased levels of FKBP5 protein expression compared to naive mice (Supplemental Fig. 2A). To identify the specific cell types expressing FKBP5 during EAE, we performed flow cytometric analysis of CNS-isolated cells from EAE and naïve mice. FKBP5 expression was predominantly observed in inflammatory microglia and myeloid infiltrates during EAE. A moderate increase in FKBP5 signal was also detected in resting microglia and T cells during EAE compared to naïve controls (Fig. 2C and Supplemental Fig. 2B-C). We further confirmed FKBP5 expression by immunofluorescence staining in the SC of naïve and EAE mice. In naïve SC, FKBP5 staining was dim in both gray and white matter (Supplemental Fig. 2D), whereas in the SC from EAE mice (disease score = 3.5), we observed distinct FKBP5 signals co-localized with Iba1 + microglia/macrophages (Fig. 2D). We next confirmed Fkbp5 upregulation in SC microglia and macrophages at the RNA level. C57BL/6 J mice were immunized and then, inflammatory microglia-macrophage populations (characterized as CD45 + CD11b + P2RY12-), were isolated at 15 DPI, corresponding to peak disease severity. Given the heterogeneity of EAE, we compared mice with high disease scores (4) to those with mild disease (1–2). Inflammatory microglia-macrophages from highly symptomatic mice exhibited dysregulation of 122 genes (FDR < 0.01), with 54 genes upregulated and 68 downregulated (Fig. 2E and Table S1). GO pathway analysis revealed enrichment in inflammatory pathways, including interferon gamma response, complement activation, and TNF-alpha signaling within the upregulated genes (Fig. 2F). Notably, Fkbp5 was among the most upregulated genes in the myeloid cells of highly symptomatic mice (Fig. 2G). To better reflect the clinical heterogeneity of MS and validate our findings across different EAE models, we leveraged published transcriptomic data from two different EAE models: SJL/J and A.SW mice, both immunized with MOG92 − 106 (GSE99300) [38]. Similar to the chronic EAE model, we observed a significant increase in Fkbp5 expression in the CNS of EAE SJL/J and A.SW mice compared to naïve controls (Fig. 2H). Interestingly, analysis of microglial cells isolated from the CNS of Biozzi ABH mice revealed a similar pattern: following immunization with lyophilized spinal cord homogenate, Fkbp5 was selectively upregulated in microglia only during the chronic phase compared to naïve mice (GSE194071) [39] (Fig. 2I).

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that FKBP5 is upregulated during EAE, with inflammatory microglia and macrophages as one of the primary cell types expressing FKBP5 in SC lesions.

Fkbp5 promotes inflammatory signaling in BV2 microglia cell line

To investigate the molecular pathways regulated by Fkbp5 in myeloid cells we first utilized BV2, a commonly used microglia cell line [40]. FKBP5 knockdown in BV2 cells was achieved using two distinct small interfering RNA (siRNA) or short hairpin RNA (shRNA) constructs (sh96 and sh99) for lentiviral transduction with comparison to a control lentiviral construct: shRNA (shCTR). Successful Fkbp5 downregulation (by ~ 70% compared to shCTR) at the RNA level was confirmed in both sh96 and sh99 cell lines (Supplemental Fig. 3A). However, only sh96 showed a downregulation of FKBP5 protein compared to shCTR (Supplemental Fig. 3B). Accordingly, subsequent analyses were performed on the sh96 clone line, hereafter referred to as BV2 with FKBP5 knockdown.

RNA-sequencing revealed 210 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in sh96 compared to shCTR cells (FDR < 0.05; 201 downregulated and 9 upregulated), shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 3A, Table S2). The IPA of the sh96 knockdown cells identified significant suppression of interferon-alpha/beta and -gamma signaling as the top canonical pathways, followed by glucocorticoid signaling, macrophage activation, phagosome formation, and complement pathways (Fig. 3B, C). To assess the functional impact of Fkbp5, we measured the phagocytic activity in sh96 and shCTR cells using an in vitro fluorescence-labeled myelin assay (carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester, CFSE, labelling). Loss of Fkbp5 exhibited a significantly increased phagocytic capacity compared to shCTR at several time-points (Fig. 3E, F). Moreover, to assess the effect of Fkbp5 knockdown on myelin degradation, BV2 with FKBP5 knockdown and shCTR were incubated with CFSE-labelled mouse myelin for 1 h and subsequently washed in order to remove extracellular myelin not phagocytized. Myelin content was then quantified after different hours (Fig. 3G). Remarkably, Fkbp5 loss showed an enhanced capacity to degrade myelin compared to shCTR, demonstrating that Fkbp5 is important in both uptake and degradation of myelin debris (Fig. 3G).

To test FKBP5-dependent inflammatory microglial functions we analyzed the capacity of FKBP5 to modulate the activation of BV2 after pre-stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-dependent macrophage activator, or cortisol, the classical immunosuppressant stress hormone and FKBP5 trigger. Inflammatory activation was evaluated by measuring major histocompatibility complex (MHC-II) expression upon cortisol or LPS 24-h stimulation. We found that sh96 cells displayed significantly lower MHC-II expression compared to shCTR after both cortisol and LPS stimulus (Fig. 3H). Finally, to test if FKBP5 affects T cell proliferation, shCTR or sh96 cells were co-cultured with anti-CD3—CD28-stimulated splenic T cells; sh96 reduced CD4 + proliferation to 20% versus 28% with shCTR (Fig. 3I).

These findings indicate that FKBP5 promotes microglial activation and reduces myelin phagocytosis, underscoring its role in regulating inflammation and debris clearance in the CNS.

SAFit2 modulates phagosome and macrophage activation pathways in BMDM

SAFit2, the gold-standard FKBP5 antagonist, exhibits high selectivity and moderate brain permeability [41]. To gain mechanistic insight into the pathways modulated by FKBP5 in primary myeloid cells we analyzed the transcriptomic profile of BMDM treated with SAFit2. RNA-seq identified 452 differentially expressed genes (FDR < 0.05; 298 downregulated and 154 upregulated genes) upon treatment with SAFit2 compared to untreated BMDM (Fig. 4A and Table S3). Given the enhanced myelin phagocytic capacity observed in BV2 lacking FKBP5, we hypothesized that pharmacological inhibition of FKBP5 with SAFit2 could promote the clearance of phagocytic cargo. SAFit2-treated BMDM showed a significant increase in myelin phagocytic ability compared to untreated controls (Fig. 4D). Although myelin degradation was modestly but significantly reduced in SAFit2-treated BMDMs, degradation increased over time in both groups (Fig. 4E). One of the standard degradative mechanisms for myelin proteins is the autophagy pathway [44]. By analyzing the level of LC3, ULK, p-ULK and AKT, we verified the contribution of SAFit2 in this pathway. SAFit2-treated BMDMs showed increased LC3 accumulation and reduced ULK expression, indicating an enhancement of autophagic processing and flux, consistent with increased degradation of myelin proteins [45] (Supplemental Fig. 4A, B), and confirming the importance of FKBP5 in regulating autophagy-mediated turnover of myelin components.

SAFit2 treatment of BMDMs mirrored the effects of Fkbp5 knockdown, suppressing classical macrophage activation (Fig. 4B, C). As expected, LPS stimulation increased MHC-II expression in untreated cells, whereas SAFit2 markedly reduced MHC-II levels in LPS-treated cells (Fig. 4F), consistent with inhibition of activation signaling. In contrast, cortisol decreased MHC-II expression in both untreated and SAFit2 treated BMDM (Fig. 4F). To test if SAFit2 regulation of BMDM might interfere with T cells proliferation, SAFit2 pre-treated macrophages were co-cultured with CD4 + T cells isolated from the spleen of naïve mice and stimulated with anti-CD3 and CD28. T cells cultured with untreated macrophages proliferated up to 60%, while CD4 + T cells co-cultured with SAFit2-treated BMDM only proliferated up to 20%, visualized as CFSE dilution (Fig. 4G). To test whether pharmacological inhibition of FKBP5 modulates BV2 microglial cells, we treated BV2 with SAFit2. SAFit2 treatment resulted in a similarly increased of myelin phagocytic activity (Fig. 4H, I), significantly downregulated MHC-II expression following 24 h LPS and cortisol stimulation (Fig. 4J) and slightly inhibited CD4 + T cell proliferation (Fig. 4K). We then isolated primary microglia and treated them with SAFit2. Similar to BV2 cells and BMDMs, SAFit2-treated microglia showed increased phagocytic capacity for myelin debris at 24 h (Fig. 4L-N).

Together these data demonstrate FKBP5 inhibition by SAFit2 suppresses inflammatory cell pathways in BMDM and BV2 cells while at the same time promotes myelin phagocytosis and phagosome degradation.

FKBP5 mediates IFNg-dependent Cxcl10 expression

SAFit2 treatment of BMDMs resulted in dysregulation of interferon gamma (IFNg) responsive genes (Fig. 4B), a pathway upregulated in monocytes from pwMS in our single-cell data (Fig. 1F) and found consistently elevated in the CSF from individuals with progressive MS [46, 47]. A comparative analysis revealed 22 downregulated genes shared between SAFit2-treated BMDMs and FKBP5-silenced BV2 microglia (sh96), as illustrated in the Venn diagram (Fig. 5A). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of these overlapping genes highlighted significant enhancement in the IFNg signaling pathway (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, IPA analysis predicted inhibition of IFNg and its downstream effectors, including STAT1 and IRF1, in both sh96 BV2 cells and SAFit2-treated BMDMs (Fig. 5C). GSEA analysis of non-overlapping genes showed cell type–specific pathway differences reflecting distinct mechanisms of FKBP5 modulation. One key IFNg target, Cxcl10 (also known as IFNg-induced protein, IP-10), is transcriptionally activated by STAT1 and plays a central role in immune cell recruitment [48]. We therefore assessed Cxcl10 expression following IFNg stimulation, with or without SAFit2 co-treatment. In BMDMs, IFNg induced a ~ 200-fold increase in Cxcl10 expression relative to controls, while co-treatment with SAFit2 reduced expression to less than half of baseline levels (Fig. 5D). A similar significant downregulation was observed in BV2 knockdown in FKBP5 compared to shCTR, but not when BV2 were treated with IFNg and SaFit2 (Fig. 5E , F). These findings support a role for FKBP5 in modulating the IFNg–CXCL10 signaling axis in myeloid cells. Blocking FKBP5 ameliorates EAE clinical course by decreasing the number of MHC-II + microglia and peripheral immune cell infiltrates

The FKBP5 inhibitor, SAFit2 has already been used in preclinical models of neuropathic pain and Parkinson disease [51, 52]. To assess the in vivo role of FKBP5 in a murine MS model, we administered SAFit2 (20 mg/kg, i.p.) daily from day 7 to day 11 post-immunization in mice with MOG35-55-induced EAE. Compared to vehicle-treated and untreated controls, SAFit2-treated mice exhibited a significant reduction in clinical disease severity during the induction and chronic phase (Fig. 6A). This improvement correlated with a marked reduction in SC inflammation and demyelination, as revealed by histopathological analysis (Fig. 6B-C, Supplemental Fig. 5A). Interestingly, serum corticosterone levels were also significantly decreased in SAFit2-treated mice relative to controls (111.1 ± 14.7 vs 225.3 ± 55.6 ng/mL), suggesting dampened stress-axis activation (Supplemental Fig. 5B). In parallel, Fkbp5 transcript levels were significantly reduced in the SC of SAFit2-treated animals (Supplemental Fig. 5C). Inflammatory responses within the CNS during EAE are characterized by activation of microglia cells, which can be best visualized by looking at the expression of MHC-II. Microglia do not normally express MHC-II in their resting or homeostatic phase [53, 54]. MHC class II expression within the CD45dimCD11b + P2RY12 + microglial compartment following SAFit2 treatment led to a significant reduction in the proportion of MHC-II + microglia relative to both control groups (Fig. 6F), suggesting reduced microglial activation. Moreover, microglia from SAFit2-treated mice displayed decreased expression of CD11c, a marker of disease-associated microglia (DAMs), and upregulated P2RY12, a purinergic receptor linked to homeostatic microglial function (Fig. 6G). Immunofluorescent staining confirmed a reduced number of Iba1 + MHC-II + microglia and macrophages in SC lesions of SAFit2-treated mice (Fig. 6H-I). Despite the reduced inflammatory response within the CNS, the peripheral immune compartment remained largely unchanged. Lymphoid populations in the spleen showed no significant differences between groups, while only splenic monocytes were significantly reduced in SAFit2-treated mice compared to controls (Supplemental Fig. 7A-D).

Lastly, we examined IFNg and complement signaling pathways, both of which were downregulated by SAFit2 in vitro in BMDM. We observed a trend toward reduced C4b expression in the SC of SAFit2-treated mice (Supplemental Fig. 5D). Additionally, serum levels of IFNg and its downstream chemokine CXCL10 were significantly reduced in the SAFit2 compared to vehicle mice, suggesting that FKBP5 inhibition may suppress IFNg-driven inflammatory cascades in vivo (Fig. 6J).

Together, these data provide evidence that blocking FKBP5 before the onset of the EAE clinical symptoms reduces the immune infiltrating cells in the SC of EAE mice and minimizes microglia/macrophages activation by downregulating MHC-II and IFNg levels.

FKBP5 inhibition decreases peripheral T cell responses and dampens antigen-presenting cell function during the EAE priming phase

Given the central role of CD4 + T cells in EAE priming and our finding that FKBP5 is also expressed within the T-cell compartment, we next examined whether FKBP5 inhibition directly alters autoreactive T-cell effector function. MOG-reactive T cells from immunized mice, restimulated ex vivo with cognate peptide, displayed a marked reduction in IFNg production when stimulated in the presence of SAFit2, together with a pronounced impairment in proliferative capacity (Supplemental Fig. 8A-C). These data indicate that FKBP5 supports robust activation and cytokine output in antigen-specific T cells.

To determine whether FKBP5 inhibition similarly affects in vivo T cell priming, we treated immunized mice with SAFit2 during the first five days after immunization and analyzed secondary lymphoid organs at 7 DPI, when encephalitogenic T cells are generated. SAFit2 treatment resulted in the reduction in MHC-II expression on both B cells and dendritic cells in draining lymph nodes (Supplemental Fig. 9C). However, myeloid subset frequencies were largely preserved (Supplemental Fig. 9A-B).

Importantly, lymph node CD4 + T cells from SAFit2-treated mice exhibited diminished GM-CSF production, and CD8 + T cells showed reduced IFNg production (Supplemental Fig. 8D-E), mirroring the defects observed in vitro. Together, these results demonstrate that FKBP5 inhibition dampens both antigen-presenting cell and early T-cell effector differentiation during the priming phase of EAE, likely contributing to the delayed onset and reduced disease severity observed in SAFit2-treated mice.

Discussion

In this paper, we demonstrate the critical role of the Fkbp5 gene in regulating macrophage and microglial function during neuroinflammation. Our transcriptomic analysis revealed that FKBP5 is required for microglial and macrophage activation in response to LPS and IFNg, highlighting its role in shaping myeloid-driven neuroinflammatory responses. Our data also indicate that Fkbp5 regulates macrophage and microglial phagocytosis of myelin debris. These pathways are integral to the neuroinflammatory process in EAE, and inhibition of FKBP5 with SAFit2 ameliorates disease by reducing CNS recruitment of inflammatory immune infiltrates, via the IFNg-CXCL10 axis. Our findings provide novel insights into the mechanisms by which myeloid cells regulate EAE neuroinflammation.

Two published datasets using different technologies have independently identified FKBP5 as significantly upregulated in peripheral and CSF myeloid cells of pwMS compared to HCs [55, 56]. Building on these findings, our single-cell compendium of CSF revealed FKBP5 enrichment specifically in CD14 + monocytes and CD36 + cDC2 clusters, two antigen-presenting cell populations implicated in MS pathogenesis. These CSF-resident myeloid cells were transcriptionally primed for MHC class II processing and IFN-driven pathways, supporting their active role in CNS immune surveillance and inflammatory signaling in MS.

FKBP5 regulates the interferon signaling cascade by promoting IKK complex assembly, NF-κB activation, and IFN production [20]. Given this role, the observed enrichment of interferon response pathways in myeloid cells from the CSF of pwMS supports a model in which FKBP5 acts as an upstream enhancer of chronic IFN signaling, potentially sustaining ongoing neuroinflammation. We also detected significant FKBP5 upregulation in microglial nodules, relative to microglia from NAWM. These nodular microglia, which are a histopathological hallmark of neuroinflammation, are believed to orchestrate lesion development through complement-mediated phagocytosis [30], a pathway markedly downregulated following inhibition of Fkbp5 in BMDM. Extending these observations to lesion pathology, we found robust FKBP5 expression in MIMS located at the rims of chronic-active lesions, confirming and expanding on the reported FKBP5 enrichment in broad-rim lesions by Klotz et al. [57]. Given the established role of complement in microglia–astrocyte communication and T cell recruitment [29], together with accumulating evidence of MIMS activity at chronic-active lesion edges, our data position FKBP5 as a central modulator of chronic neuroinflammation in MS. These findings highlight FKBP5 potential as a therapeutic target to suppress microglia-driven complement activation and promote resolution of inflammation within the MS brain.

Macrophages and microglia play a dichotomous role in MS and EAE pathology, contributing to lesion formation and neuroinflammation while also facilitating myelin debris clearance and supporting remyelination [58–60]. Our findings indicate that genetic ablation or pharmacological inhibition of Fkbp5 enhances myelin uptake and promotes its degradation through phagolysosomal pathways. This contrasts with a previous report of reduced zymosan beads uptake in Fkbp5-deficient microglia [20], a difference likely attributable to the distinct mechanisms involved: myelin clearance depends largely on complement and lipid-handling pathways, whereas zymosan is recognized via Toll-like receptor signaling [61]. In line with our genetic ablation of Fkbp5, pharmacologic inhibition of FKBP5 with SAFit2 increased myelin phagocytosis in primary myeloid cells, including BMDMs and primary microglia, suggesting that FKBP5 blockade augments lipid metabolism programs, upregulated in our transcriptomic data that facilitate myelin clearance. This is consistent with prior work showing that SAFit2 restores lipid signaling after nerve injury [62]. These results also align with recent observations in the cuprizone (CPZ) model, where Fkbp5 expression is upregulated early during demyelination, and Fkbp5 deletion delays demyelination and impairs remyelination following CPZ withdrawal [21]. Given our data implicating FKBP5 in regulating myelin uptake, degradation, and lipid metabolism, future studies employing microglia- or macrophage-specific Fkbp5 knockout mice, or SAFit2 treatment in the CPZ model, will be essential to define whether FKBP5 targeting can improve myelin clearance during active demyelination and enhance remyelination by modulating lipid and cholesterol homeostasis, two processes central to MS progression.

A key pathway dysregulated in Fkbp5 knockdown BV2 cells, consistent with findings in SAFit2-treated macrophages, was the IFNg signaling response, which was significantly diminished in the absence of FKBP5. While traditionally viewed as a proinflammatory and neurotoxic cytokine in the CNS, IFNg has emerged with both detrimental and protective effects depending on context [63]. IFNg can promote "M1" macrophage polarization to an inflammatory phenotype [64], induce microglial apoptosis, and at high concentrations, impair microglial neuroprotective functions by driving excessive TNFa production. In our study, BMDMs stimulated with IFNg followed by SAFit2 treatment exhibited downregulation of Cxcl10, a chemokine critical for T helper cell recruitment and blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity [65]. Interestingly, a pathogenic subset of monocytes expressing Cxcl10 was found to have a detrimental role in the pathology of EAE [65]. Consistently with these findings, mice treated with SAFit2 demonstrated a reduced number of infiltrating lymphocyte and myeloid cells, together with a decrease in IFNg and Cxcl10 expression in the serum, emphasizing the regulatory interplay between FKBP5-IFNg, and Cxcl10. Notably, these findings link back to our human scRNA-seq data, where Fkbp5-upregulated myeloid cells also exhibited increased expression of interferon pathway genes. Because FKBP5 is also expressed within the T-cell compartment, we assessed whether FKBP5 inhibition modulates autoreactive T-cell programs. SAFit2-treated MOG-reactive CD4 + T cells exhibited reduced GM-CSF production, a cytokine central to the encephalitogenic CD4 + T-cell response [66]. SAFit2 also reduced IFNg production in CD8 + T cells, a pathway relevant for both cytotoxic activity and the development of atypical EAE phenotypes [67]. These results indicate that FKBP5 shapes early autoreactive T-cell responses both directly and through its effects on myeloid–T-cell interactions.

Although this topic lies outside the primary focus of the present study, given the well-established role of FKBP5 in mood disorders and its regulatory function within the glucocorticoid signaling pathway [11, 12], it is critical to consider its potential contribution to the high prevalence of anxiety and depression observed in individuals with MS [19, 68]. Polymorphisms in FKBP5 have been associated with impaired GR sensitivity, altered stress responses, and increased vulnerability to mood disorders. These psychiatric conditions are frequently accompanied by a proinflammatory profile, including elevated circulating cytokines, dysregulated corticosterone levels, and T cell receptor dysfunctions, features that overlap with immunopathological mechanisms in MS [69, 70]. In this context, the marked upregulation of FKBP5 in myeloid cells from MS patients raises the possibility that FKBP5 might serve as a molecular connection between neuroinflammation and stress-related psychiatric comorbidities. Assessing the role of mood disorders as a predisposing factor or as an amplifier of MS progression may offer novel insights into the interplay between chronic inflammation and neuropsychiatric symptoms. These findings underscore the importance of integrating neuroimmune and neuropsychiatric axes in the study of MS pathogenesis.

Though our work and others show the expression of FKBP5 by macrophages and microglia and demonstrate that FKBP5 could modulate microglia activities in vitro and in vivo, further studies using conditional cell-specific knock-out mice are warranted to delineate the contribution of Fkbp5 in immune and CNS-resident populations. While our results indicate that FKBP5 also shapes early autoreactive T-cell responses, definitive demonstration of T-cell intrinsic mechanisms will require T-cell specific Fkbp5 deletion or adoptive transfer approaches. Moreover, as our findings are limited to the MOG35-55 EAE model, and given the heterogeneity of MS, additional validation of SAFit2 across complementary models of neuroinflammation will be essential prior to clinical translation. Furthermore, because of the connection between the FKBP5-GR pathways, it is necessary to identify how chronic stress and depression contribute to the anxiolytic effect of FKBP5, and to elucidate the impact of FKBP5 signaling on neuroinflammation in response to stress. As MS patients with depression show increased IFNg [71, 72], further investigations are required to elucidate the underlying mechanism and its contribution to MS pathogenesis. Given the protective effect of SAFit2 in mice and its clinical safety it might be worth testing whether SAFit2 would ameliorate psychiatric comorbidities in patients with MS.
