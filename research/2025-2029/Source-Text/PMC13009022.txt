Gut dysfunction commonly precedes motor symptoms in Parkinson’s disease (PD), but the mechanistic sequence of gut versus brain pathology remains unclear. This work aimed to define the timing of intestinal barrier dysfunction relative to central nervous system (CNS) changes in the A53T α-synuclein transgenic mouse model of PD. Functional and molecular assessments of the gastrointestinal tract (ileum and colon) were conducted at 12 and 36 weeks. We measured in vivo and ex vivo intestinal permeability, nutrient absorption, histomorphology, goblet cell density, and expression of MUC2 and Claudin-1. Inflammatory markers (CRP, TNF-α, CD45) were quantified in plasma and gut tissues. A53T mice exhibited increased intestinal permeability at 12 and 36 weeks, with transiently elevated ex vivo transepithelial electrical resistance (TER) at 12 weeks. Nutrient absorption remained intact. Morphological changes included widened villi and crypts, altered mucin expression, and early reductions in Claudin-1 in the ileum and the colon while inflammatory markers remained largely unchanged. These findings suggest that gut dysfunction precedes known central pathology in A53T mice, supporting further investigation into the gut as an early site of pathology and a potential therapeutic target in PD.

Introduction

The intestinal barrier maintains gastrointestinal (GI) and systemic homeostasis by regulating the selective passage of luminal contents, including antigens, nutrients, and bacteria, across the mucosa. This occurs via paracellular transport, governed by tight junctions (TJs), and transcellular transport (Florey 1962; Magnusson and Stjernström 1982; Schoultz and Keita 2020). The mucus layer, primarily composed of mucin-2 (MUC2) secreted by goblet cells, forms the first line of defence and prevents the penetration of microbes and toxins to protect the epithelium (Allen et al. 1984; McGuckinet al. 2011). TJ proteins, such as claudin-1, occludin, and zonulin occludens 1 (ZO-1), play a crucial role in regulating paracellular permeability (Giepmans and van Ijzendoorn 2009). Notably, claudin-1 forms a selective barrier that seals adjacent cells and anchors the TJ complex to the cytoskeleton (Suzuki 2013; Zeisel et al. 2019). Disruption of TJs can compromise barrier integrity, leading to inflammation and immune activation (Furuse et al. 2002; Turner 2009; Van Itallie and Anderson 2006).

GI dysfunction in Parkinson’s disease (PD) is well recognised (Derkinderen et al. 2011; Edwards et al. 1992), with ~ 81% of patients experiencing some degree of GI symptoms and > 50% suffering from constipation (Bock et al. 2022; Cloud and Greene 2011). Pathological α-synuclein (α-Syn) aggregates have been detected in the GI tract of PD patients (Beach et al. 2010; Braak et al. 2006; Shannon et al. 2012; Takeda et al. 1993; Wakabayashi et al. 1990, 1988), and increasing evidence implicates intestinal barrier dysfunction in PD pathogenesis (Clairembault et al. 2015; Forsyth et al. 2011). PD patients and animal models show increased gut permeability, reduced TJ expression, and enhanced microbial translocation, potentially contributing to systemic and neuroinflammation(Huang et al. 2021). These findings suggest that the GI tract may play a role in disease onset, progression and severity. Braak’s hypothesis posits that PD is initiated by a focal α-synuclein misfolding in the peripheral nervous system (e.g., enteric nervous system) or caudal brainstem (e.g., the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus and the olfactory structures) and then propagates in a stereotyped caudo rostral sequence through long projecting, often unmyelinated neurons, eventually involving the substantia nigra and cortical regions where clinical motor signs appear (Braak et al. 2003). The model suggests that an environmental neurotropic agent may gain entry via both nasal and gastric routes AKA “dual hit,” seeding α-synuclein pathology. As the disease advances through successive Braak stages, the burden of Lewy pathology correlates with worsening clinical severity, providing a framework for understanding the long presymptomatic phase and predicting disease progression (Braak et al., 2004, 2006).

Preclinical studies support this gut–brain connection. Rotenone-induced and LPS-induced models exhibit reduced claudin-1 expression, increased intestinal permeability, and α-Syn accumulation in the colon (Dodiya et al. 2020; Kelly et al. 2014). Other models including 6-OHDA and MPTP demonstrate TJ alterations and abnormal mucus production (Guo et al. 2023; Pellegrini et al. 2020). Clinical studies have reported similar findings, with increased gut permeability, reduced ZO-1 and occludin expression, and elevated inflammatory markers in PD patients (Clairembault et al. 2015; Davies et al. 1996; Devos et al. 2013; Forsyth et al. 2011; Perez-Pardo et al. 2019; Schwiertz et al. 2018).

Our previous work demonstrated that motor function and substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) neuronal density are preserved at 12 weeks in a transgenic mouse model overexpressing human A53T α-synuclein, with significant motor deficits and dopaminergic neuronal loss emerging between 32 and 40 weeks (Han et al. 2025). Thus, these time points represent pre-symptomatic and post-symptomatic stages of disease progression, respectively. Here, we aimed to characterise functional and cellular integrity of the intestinal barrier at these defined stages. By defining the onset and extent of barrier dysfunction relative to hallmark features of PD, this study contributes to growing efforts to elucidate the gut-brain axis in PD pathogenesis.

Methods

Ethics approval

All procedures involving mice were carried out with prior approval from the Florey Institute for Neuroscience and Mental Health Animal Ethics Committee (AEC No. 18–113 FINMH) and performed in accordance with the guidelines of the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC), Australian Code of Practice for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purposes.

Animals and experimental design

Animal housing and colony maintenance were performed as previously described (Han et al. 2025). Briefly, male B6; C3-Tg-Prnp/SNCA*A53T/83Vle/J transgenic mice (A53T) and wild-type (WT) littermates were generated from heterozygous breeders in a stable colony established from Jackson Laboratory stock. These mice overexpress human α-synuclein carrying the A53T mutation under control of the murine prion promoter (PrP), which drives expression throughout the central and peripheral nervous systems, including enteric neurons. Endogenous murine α-synuclein remains expressed in this model. All mice were group-housed with ad libitum access to food and water and genotyped by qPCR using tail biopsy to confirm transgene expression. A total of 40 male mice were used.

Sample sizes varied across experiments (range: n = 5–28 per group) due to differences in experimental design and tissue availability. For in vivo measures, repeated assessments were obtained from the same animals at multiple time points where appropriate. For molecular and histological analyses, we aimed for a minimum of n = 10 per group; however, occasional sample loss occurred due to technical limitations (e.g., insufficient plasma volume for ELISA assays, or exclusion of histological sections due to tissue damage or suboptimal orientation during embedding). Exact n-values for each experiment are specified in the corresponding figure legends.

In vivo FITC-dextran intestinal permeability test

Intestinal permeability was assessed in mice that had been fasted for 4 h, with free access to drinking water. Mice were orally administered 4 kDa fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran solution (50 mg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich, Australia) at 1% (w/v) body weight. One hour later, 100 µl of blood sample was collected via tail snip. Samples were spun at 4000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain plasma. Plasma was transferred to a black 96-well plate and diluted 1:1 with phosphate-buffered saline solution (PBS: 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM NaHPO2, and 1.8 mM KH2PO4). Intestinal permeability was assessed by fluorimetry on a FLUOstar Omega microplate reader (BMG Labtech; Australia) at 485/520 ex/em. Permeability is expressed as relative fluorescence units.

Bomb calorimetry

Faecal pellets were collected over a 24-h period, dehydrated for 24 h at 65 °C and sent to the Melbourne Mouse Metabolic Phenotyping Platform (MMMP) for bomb calorimetric analysis with a CAL3K-U oxygen bomb calorimeter using a CAL3K 4K-4 thread-type vessel (DDS Calorimeters, Randburg, South Africa).

Inflammatory markers analysis

Plasma samples were diluted 1:10 in plasma diluent, and TNF and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were quantified using mouse TNF (ab208348, Abcam) and CRP (DY1829, R&D systems) ELISA kits following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Tissue collection, processing and staining

Animals were anaesthetised with 100 mg/kg ketamine and 10 mg/kg xylazine via subcutaneous injection; terminal blood was collected via cardiac puncture after which mice were perfused with 50 mL of ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). GI tissues were collected and processed as previously described (Han et al. 2025). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Alcian blue staining were performed by the Melbourne Histology Platform (MHP).

For cross-sectional immunohistochemistry, antigen retrieval was performed by boiling tissue sections in a Tris–EDTA Buffer (10 mM Tris Base, 1 mM EDTA Solution, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 9.0), at 100 °C for 20 min, followed by cooling at room temperature (RT) for 20 min. Tissues were immunohistochemically labelled as previously described (Han et al. 2025) using rabbit-anti CD45 (1:200, Cat#Ab10558), Goat-anti Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP, 1:500, Cat# ab53554), rabbit-anti Claudin-1 (1:200, Cat#717,800) and mouse anti-CD326 (EpCAM), 1:500, Cat# 14–5791-85). Phosphorylated α-synuclein was detected using rabbit anti-phospho α-Syn (1:1000, Abcam Cat#ab51253), which recognises serine 129-phosphorylated α-synuclein in both human and murine proteins. Given the high sequence homology between species and the aggregation-associated epitope masking that can occur in pathological inclusions, this approach does not distinguish between transgenic human and endogenous murine α-synuclein. Accordingly, pS129 immunoreactivity was interpreted as a marker of disease-associated α-synuclein pathology rather than species-specific origin. Secondary antibodies used were donkey anti-rabbit Alexa 647 or 555 and donkey anti-goat 555 (all sourced from Life Technologies Australia, used at 1:500).

Ex vivo and in vivo intestinal permeability

Sections of distal ileum (~ 3 cm proximal to ileocecal junction) were collected into ice-cold Krebs solution (11.1 mM glucose, 118 mM NaCl, 4.8 mM KCl, 1.0 mM NaH2PO4, 1.2 mM MgSO4, 25 mM NaHCO3, 2.5 mM CaCl2, pH 7.4). Tissue was cut along the mesenteric border, and full-thickness sections were pinned onto Ussing chamber sliders (0.3 cm2 apertures, Physiological Instruments). Sliders were placed into two-part chambers (EasyMount Diffusion Chambers, Physiologic Instruments) with 5 mL of Krebs-glucose solution on the serosal side and 5 mL of Krebs-mannitol (11.1 mM) on the mucosal side to inhibit active transport and maintain osmotic balance. Solutions were kept at 37 °C and oxygenated with carbogen (5% CO2, 95% O2) to maintain pH. A multichannel voltage-current clamp (VCC MC6, Physiologic Instruments) was connected to each chamber using a set of four electrodes (2 voltage sensing and 2 current passing electrodes) and agar bridges (3% agarose/3 M KCl in the tip and backfilled with 3 M KCl) installed on opposite sides of the tissue. Voltage and short current (Isc) readings were acquired using a PowerLab amplifier and recorded using LabChart® 8 (both ADInstruments, Sydney, Australia).

Tissue was equilibrated for 30 min before clamping the voltage to 0. FITC-dextran (25 mg/ml) was added to the mucosal side of the chamber (200 µl), and samples (100 µl) were taken in duplicate from the serosal side at 0 and 120 min to assess intestinal permeability. Electrophysiological properties of the tissue were assessed by measuring transepithelial resistance (TER, Ω·cm2) following a 2-s pulse of 2 mV every 60 s throughout the experiment.

Western blot

Approximately 10–15 mg of snap-frozen ileum and colon tissues were homogenised in ice-cold Radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, 1 mM EDTA, and cOmplete (Roche) protease and phosSTOP (Roche) phosphatase inhibitors (1 mM PMSF, 1 μg/mL aprotinin, 1 μg/mL leupeptin, 1 mM benzamidine, 1 mM Na3VO4, 5 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 1 mM DTT, and 1 mM NaF). Protein concentrations were determined using the Bio-Rad DC Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Approximately 10 µg of protein extract was loaded onto a 26-well Criterion TGX Stain-Free gel (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, USA) and subjected to electrophoresis at 50 mV for 5 min, followed by 100 mV for 60 min. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes for 1 h at 100 mV. Membrane images were captured using the ChemiDoc MP system to measure total protein content. Membranes were blocked for 1 h at RT with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) (0.1%) followed by incubation with MUC2 primary antibody (rabbit, 1:1000, Abcam, Cat#ab272692, Cambridge, UK) overnight at 4 °C. MUC2 expression levels were visualised by incubation of the membrane with rabbit-HRP secondary antibody (711–035–152; diluted 1:20,000) for 1 h at RT, followed by detection using Clarity Western ECL substrate (Bio-Rad). Densitometric analysis of Western blots was conducted using Image Lab Software v5.2 (Bio-Rad), with protein expression levels normalised to total protein content. Optical density values are presented relative to the control group (12-week WT) and expressed as fold change.

Imaging and quantitative analysis

All images were captured using ZEISS AXIOSCAN 7 (Carl Zeiss, Sydney, NSW, Australia) using a 10 × air objective (0.5 numerical aperture). For fluorescent images, fluorophores were visualised using excitation filters for Alexa 405 (excitation wavelength 405 nm), Alexa 488 (excitation wavelength 473 nm), and Alexa 594 Red (excitation wavelength 559 nm). Quantification was performed using QuPath software (Bankhead et al. 2017).

For H&E-stained sections, gross morphological changes were assessed with QuPath. Distal ileum measurements included villus height, villus width, crypt depth and crypt width of 20 well-oriented full-length villi and crypts. In the distal colon, crypt depth and width of 20 full-length crypts were measured. Muscle thickness was measured in both distal ileum and distal colon. In the ileum, the relative mucosal surface per unit area (M) was calculated from the formula described by Kisielinski et al. (2002).M=(villus width×villus height)+villis width2+crypt width22-(villus width2)2villis width2+crypt width22

In Alcian blue-stained colon sections, the number of goblet cells was analysed in Fiji Software (Version 1.54f) (Schindelin et al. 2012) using a custom ImageJ macro script (Rowe and Han 2025). The analysis was manually restricted to regions of mucosa with intact crypts defined by regions of interest generated in QuPath Software (Version 0.5.1) (Bankhead et al. 2017). Individual goblet cells were segmented and counted using a custom Cellpose 2.0 model (Stringer et al. 2021). Total area of mucosa (µm2) was also measured. The number of goblet cells per mm2 mucosa was calculated using the following formula: Number of GCs/Mucosa (Area (µm2)) × 10002. Percentage area coverage of mucin staining in the mucosal area was also analysed with semi-automated annotation using the pixel classifier in QuPath Software (Version 0.5.1) (Bankhead et al. 2017).

The proportion of the mucosal area positive for CD45-immunoreactivity was quantified in QuPath Software (Version 0.5.1) in 6 images per preparation. CD45-immunopositive area was identified and measured by means of pixel classification and divided by the area of the mucosa. Average coverage of CD45-positive cells is presented as a percentage of the total mucosal area.

The fluorescence intensity of GFAP, Claudin-1 and EpCAM immunoreactivity in the myenteric plexus and the mucosa was quantified using mean fluorescence intensity in at least 3 regions of interest per sample. All images were analysed in a blinded manner using QuPath software (Version 0.5.1) (Bankhead et al. 2017).

Statistics analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Comparisons between groups were performed using two-way or one-way ANOVA with the Uncorrected Fisher’s LSD test for multiple comparisons. Analyses and outlier detection were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), and any data points identified as statistical outliers were excluded prior to analysis. P values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Gut barrier function and digestive efficiency

To assess gut barrier integrity and digestive function in A53T mice, we evaluated intestinal permeability and energy absorption at 12 and 36 weeks. Ex vivo permeability assays in the distal ileum show no significant differences in FITC-dextran flux between genotypes at either 12 or 36 weeks (Fig. 1a). However, intestinal resistance is significantly increased in A53T mice at 12 weeks, suggesting reduced paracellular permeability, a difference not observed in older animals (Fig. 1b). Despite these barrier changes, no genotype differences are observed in body weight at either time point (Fig. 1d). Analysis of faecal caloric density by bomb calorimetry revealed no genotype effect, but a significant age-related increase in faecal caloric density is observed across both groups (Fig. 1e).

Assessment of local and systemic inflammation and glial markers

To evaluate whether inflammation contributes to early gut barrier dysfunction in A53T mice, we assessed systemic inflammatory markers and local gut inflammation. No genotype-dependent differences were observed in plasma CRP, TNF-α, IL-6, or LBP levels at either 12 or 36 weeks (Fig. 2a–d), although age-related reductions in CRP were evident in both genotypes, and IL-6 levels increased with age in A53T mice. Locally, CD45 and GFAP immunoreactivity in the myenteric plexus and mucosa of the ileum and colon showed no significant differences between WT and A53T mice at either time point (Fig. 2e–h), indicating an absence of overt gliosis or immune cell infiltration. Morphological changes in the intestinal mucosa

Histological analysis of H&E-stained ileum and colon sections revealed selective genotype- and age-dependent changes. At 12 weeks, A53T mice exhibited significantly increased villus width in the ileum and crypt width in the colon compared to WT (Fig. 3a, e). No genotype-dependent differences were observed in ileal crypt depth, muscle thickness, villus height, or villus height-to-crypt depth ratio, nor in colonic crypt depth, muscle thickness, or mucosal surface area (Fig. 3b–d, F–H). Age-related changes were noted in WT mice, with increased crypt depth and villus height in the ileum (Fig. 3b, d). In A53T mice, colonic muscle thickness increased with age, while crypt width decreased (Fig. 3e, g). Representative images illustrate structural changes (Fig. 3i) and phosphorylated α-synuclein accumulation in the A53T colon at 36 weeks (Fig. 3j). In this model, human A53T α-synuclein is expressed throughout the nervous system, including enteric neurons, and the pS129 antibody recognises phosphorylated α-synuclein irrespective of species; therefore, the signal observed likely reflects a combination of overexpressed human A53T α-synuclein and recruited endogenous murine α-synuclein within pathology-associated conformers. Phospho-αSyn immunoreactivity in A53T mice was confined to enteric nervous system layers of the gut wall and co-localised with the pan-neuronal marker PGP9.5, confirming neuronal localisation. Goblet cell density and mucin expression

Goblet cell density in the colon was quantified using Alcian blue staining, a technique that selectively stains acidic mucins produced by goblet cells. Goblet cell density did not differ between genotypes but declined significantly with age in A53T mice (Fig. 4a, b). For a more comprehensive assessment of mucin expression, the total area coverage of mucin staining by Alcian blue in and around goblet cells was quantified in relation to colonic mucosal area. Mucin coverage in the colon was elevated in A53T mice at 12 weeks but declined by 36 weeks (Fig. 4c, d). Western blot analysis of MUC2 revealed reduced expression in the ileum of A53T mice at 36 weeks, with an age-related decline (Fig. 4e, g). In the colon, MUC2 expression was lower in A53T mice at 12 weeks but returned to WT levels by 36 weeks (Fig. 4f). Discussion

Growing evidence implicates intestinal barrier dysfunction as a critical factor in PD pathogenesis (Clairembault et al. 2015; Schwiertz et al. 2018). This study provides a multifaceted assessment of intestinal barrier function in the A53T mouse model of PD, revealing early and region-specific alterations that may precede neurodegeneration. Using complementary in vivo and ex vivo approaches, we identified increased intestinal permeability, reduced tight junction expression, and intestinal mucus disturbances in A53T mice from 12 weeks of age.

Consistent with previous reports in PD patients (Forsyth et al. 2011; Schwiertz et al. 2018), our in vivo FITC-dextran assays revealed increased gut permeability in A53T mice at both 12 and 36 weeks. While ex vivo FITC flux in the ileum did not differ between groups, we observed a transient increase in transepithelial resistance (TER) at 12 weeks, which likely reflects altered ion permeability rather than a direct measure of macromolecular barrier integrity. Although this increase in TER may initially appear contradictory to the increased in vivo FITC permeability, it is worth noting that TER does not necessarily correlate with paracellular permeability measured by tracers such as FITC-dextran. These two parameters can diverge due to different types of paracellular pathways (Schoultz and Keita 2020; Shen et al. 2011). This discrepancy may also stem from regional variability in permeability along the intestine, as Ussing chambers assess only a small, localised section of tissue. To explore potential explanations for this divergence, we next examined structural features that could influence TER. Notably, the increase in TER at 12 weeks coincided with an increase in villus width in the ileum of A53T mice, which may reflect structural or molecular changes in tight junctions regulating ion flux (Shen 2012). These findings underscore the complexity of assessing barrier function and suggest regional or molecular-size-specific permeability changes.

Despite increased in vivo permeability, A53T mice maintained body weight and faecal caloric density comparable to WT mice, suggesting preserved nutrient absorption, possibly via compensatory epithelial adaptations (Moon 2011; Wu et al. 2022). Supporting this, morphological analysis revealed structural changes in A53T mice including increased villus and crypt width and altered muscle thickness, features often associated with stress-induced remodelling(Van Der Flier and Clevers 2009). However, age-related increases in crypt depth and villus height, typically observed as part of normal intestinal maturation, were evident only in WT mice, suggesting a potential impairment of developmental or adaptive remodelling processes in A53T mice (Huygelen et al. 2014; Suzuki et al. 2022).

Analysis of mucin dynamics revealed a reduction in MUC2 expression in the colon at 12 weeks and in the ileum at 36 weeks, alongside age-dependent reductions in goblet cell density. Goblet cells are key structural components of the intestinal barrier and play a crucial role in maintaining epithelial integrity through mucus secretion (Deplancke and Gaskins 2001; Dupont et al. 2014; Groschwitz and Hogan 2009; Kim and Khan 2013). Thus, these changes likely reflect compromised mucus barrier integrity, consistent with prior reports of altered mucin composition in PD patients and rat models of PD (Bellini et al. 2023; Pellegrini et al. 2020). Notably, although we observed altered mucin expression, Alcian blue staining has inherent limitations in precisely quantifying goblet cells, and tissue processing may have affected mucin layer preservation. Further work using unprocessed tissues and additional mucin markers is needed to validate these findings.

The observed reduction in MUC2 expression and goblet cell density raises important questions about how mucus barrier changes intersect with microbiome dynamics in early PD. Early cross-sectional studies first established that PD is associated with altered gut microbial composition (Scheperjans et al. 2015). Subsequent longitudinal and meta-analytic analyses indicate that PD patients exhibit a characteristic microbiome signature at baseline, including enrichment of mucin-degrading taxa such as Akkermansia and depletion of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing genera like Lachnospiraceae and Faecalibacterium. These changes persist over time and predict clinical progression, supporting a model in which microbial alterations precede or accompany early disease evolution rather than being solely a consequence of barrier breakdown(Aho et al. 2019; Romano et al. 2021). Consistently, PD cohorts show reduced SCFA levels and enrichment of microbial functions linked to host-cell interaction (Unger et al. 2016), while short prebiotic interventions that restore SCFA producers improve gastrointestinal symptoms (Bedarf et al. 2025; Romano et al. 2025). Together, these findings point to a bidirectional relationship in which early dysbiosis may weaken the mucus layer and promote expansion of mucin-degrading bacteria, which in turn exacerbate barrier dysfunction. Future studies integrating microbiome profiling with mucosal and epithelial assessments and employing advanced approaches such as intravital imaging of bacterial translocation will be critical to disentangle these interactions.

In contrast with previous reports (Pellegrini et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2022), markers of systemic inflammation and local gut immune activation were largely unaltered in the present study. While our study did not show robust genotype-specific inflammatory changes either locally or systemically, Pellegrini and colleagues reported early activation of enteric glia and increased colonic IL-1β and TNF levels in A53T mice from 3 months of age, along with elevated plasma LPS and LBP, indicating systemic exposure to gut-derived endotoxins. These discrepancies may reflect differences in tissue processing and analytical sensitivity: Pellegrini et al. used snap-frozen colon tissue for cytokine quantification, whereas our study employed fixed paraffin-embedded sections and focused on CD45 as a pan-leukocyte marker. It is possible that subtle shifts in specific immune cell populations occurred without net changes in total leukocyte density, and that regional or cell-type-specific inflammatory responses were not captured by our approach. These considerations highlight the importance of methodological, temporal, and anatomical specificity when assessing gut inflammation in PD models.

Plasma levels of CRP, TNF-α, IL-6, and LBP showed no significant genotype differences at either 12 or 36 weeks in this study, although IL-6 increased with age in A53T mice, and CRP declined with age in both genotypes. These findings suggest that inflammatory changes in this model may be subtle, transient, or restricted to specific disease stages or subpopulations, aligning with evidence that gut inflammation in PD is not universal. Notably, only a subset of PD patients show an “enteric pro-inflammatory profile” with elevated inflammatory cytokines and glial markers in the colon, which peaks early and stabilises over time(Devos et al. 2013). Similarly, neuroinflammation in PD microglia plateaus early but drives progression via persistent mechanisms (Gerhard et al. 2006).

Together, these data suggest that early intestinal barrier dysfunction in this model occurs independently of overt inflammation or gliosis, although subtle systemic immune alterations, such as age-related IL-6 increases, may emerge later. Our findings underscore the temporal variability of inflammatory responses in PD and the importance of aligning assessments with specific disease stages or subsets of the patient population. While our immune profiling was limited to a focused panel, future studies incorporating broader immune and glial markers will be critical to fully capture localised inflammatory dynamics.

The marked reduction in claudin‑1 expression in both ileum and colon reinforces the concept that epithelial barrier compromise occurs early and independently of overt inflammation. Claudin‑1 is a key sealing component of the tight junction complex, and its loss is consistent with increased paracellular permeability observed in vivo, even in the absence of broad immune activation. Similar reductions in tight junction proteins have been reported in PD patients and experimental models, where disruption of claudin‑based junctions facilitates microbial translocation and epithelial stress(Clairembault et al. 2015; Dodiya et al. 2020; Forsyth et al. 2011; Schwiertz et al. 2018). These findings complement our EpCAM data, which indicate additional epithelial remodelling, and together suggest that structural changes at the mucosal interface may precede and potentially prime later inflammatory or neurodegenerative processes. Despite extensive optimisation, including testing multiple antibodies and varying retrieval conditions, we were unable to obtain specific or reproducible staining for ZO-1 or occludin via western blot and fluorescent immunohistochemistry. This likely reflects the sensitivity of these proteins to fixation and paraffin embedding, as well as their relatively low or dynamic expression in distal gut regions. Future studies using fresh-frozen tissue or RNA-based approaches will be important to fully profile tight junction proteins.

These findings align with Pellegrini et al. (2022), who demonstrate that early enteric α-synuclein accumulation in A53T mice compromises intestinal epithelial barrier integrity via caspase-1-dependent inflammasome signalling, leading to reduced expression of tight junction proteins such as ZO-1 and occludin. This work and ours support the notion that loss of epithelial integrity in the intestinal barrier may be a primary event in PD pathogenesis, independent of overt neurodegeneration.

Taken together, our findings highlight a temporal progression of intestinal barrier dysfunction in A53T mice, beginning with early TJ disruption and mucin alterations, followed by increased in vivo permeability in the absence of overt immune activation. Morphological remodelling of the intestinal mucosa further underscores the complex interplay between structural and epithelial changes in the gut. These results suggest that epithelial barrier defects may represent an initiating factor or facilitator of downstream pathological processes in PD.

These findings are consistent with previous characterisations of the A53T model, which indicate that neurodegeneration in the CNS of A53T mice typically manifests at 32–48 weeks (Giasson et al. 2003, Han et al. 2025, Mondal et al., 2021, Oaks et al. 2013, Paumier et al. 2013) while gut manifestations present between 12 and 20 weeks (Han et al. 2025). This temporal framework supports our interpretation that intestinal barrier dysfunction occurs early in disease progression, potentially preceding and contributing to later neurodegenerative changes.

Future studies should broaden TJ protein analysis beyond claudin-1 to include ZO-1, occludin, and other claudins, several of which have been shown to undergo transient reductions in young transgenic mice (Pellegrini et al. 2022), to better characterise the molecular basis of barrier disruption. Future studies should also incorporate faecal and tissue-based biomarkers such as calprotectin, alpha-1-antitrypsin, and zonulin, which have shown promise in PD cohorts (Schwiertz et al. 2018), to complement functional assays and provide a more comprehensive assessment of mucosal barrier integrity. Additionally, longitudinal assessment of subtle immune changes, including innate immune signalling and neuroimmune crosstalk in the enteric nervous system, will be important to clarify how early gut pathology may influence later CNS dysfunction.

Notably, as motor deficits and dopaminergic cell loss in A53T mice emerge well after the onset of gut barrier changes, our findings raise the possibility that early epithelial dysfunction may contribute to downstream neurodegeneration. This highlights the gut as a potential target, not only for biomarker discovery but also for early intervention strategies aimed at slowing or preventing motor symptom onset in PD.
