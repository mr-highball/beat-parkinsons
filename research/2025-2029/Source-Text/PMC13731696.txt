Background

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is characterized by disabling motor and non-motor symptoms. Although pharmacological and rehabilitation interventions improve many motor and non-motor symptoms, persistent impairments commonly remain in domains, including speech, swallowing, gait, and balance, particularly as the disease progresses. For this reason, over the past few years, alternative rehabilitative approaches, such as singing-based interventions, have been used with the aim of addressing symptoms, such as speech impairment, swallowing difficulties, respiratory dysfunction, and mood.

Objective

We aim to review the evidences on the efficacy of singing interventions on motor and non-motor symptoms in patients with PD.

Methods

A comprehensive literature search of PubMed and Google Scholar was conducted for interventional studies published until December 2025. Results were summarized into five symptom domains: motor function, voice quality, breathing, swallowing, and quality of life.

Results

Twenty-six studies involving 1057 patients with PD were included. Singing-based interventions were associated with improvements in motor symptoms, gait, posture, voice quality, respiratory control, and maximum phonation time. Several studies also reported enhanced psychological well-being, social connectedness, and quality of life, with reductions in stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms. Evidence regarding swallowing outcomes was limited and inconsistent. Considerable heterogeneity in study design, intervention protocols, and outcome measures precluded meta-analysis.

Conclusion

Singing is a promising adjunct therapy for PD rehabilitation, particularly in domains less responsive to pharmacological treatment. Further high-quality research is needed to establish standardized protocols and assess long-term benefits.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a chronic condition characterized by cardinal motor symptoms, such as rigidity, tremor, and bradykinesia (slowness of movement) [1, 2]. In addition to these well-known motor dysfunctions, non-motor features are present and may precede motor ones. Among the non-motor symptoms, the most common are mood changes, including anxiety, apathy, and depression, as well as autonomic disturbances such as urinary urgency and constipation [3–5]. Moreover, impairment in voice and speech quality and reduction of facial expressivity are common, impacting people’s personal and social quality of life. [6].

Because of the combination of these heterogeneous clinical features, PD should be regarded as a complex, wide-spectrum disease that requires a comprehensive, multidimensional evaluation and management of the patient. [7, 8].

Despite the availability of pharmacological treatments for PD, significant limitations remain in addressing the full spectrum of symptoms, particularly in the domains of non-motor functioning and axial symptoms which include speech, swallowing, gait, and balance issues [8, 9]. Over the last few years, several studies have shown that rehabilitative therapy, including physical exercise, art, dance, and music therapy, has a good impact on those PD manifestations less responding to Levodopa [1].

Over the past two decades, a substantial evidence base has established multidisciplinary rehabilitation as an integral component of PD management. Physical therapy is recommended as a first-line intervention for improving gait, balance, mobility and motor function, with high-quality evidence from systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and the International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society (MDS) evidence review supporting its efficacy [10]. Occupational therapy also contributes to improvements in activities of daily living, participation, and quality of life, particularly when delivered within multidisciplinary rehabilitation programs [10]. Speech rehabilitation is similarly supported by a robust evidence base. Lee Silverman Voice Treatment (LSVT LOUD), evaluated in five randomized-controlled trials and subsequent systematic reviews and meta-analyses, has demonstrated significant improvements in vocal loudness, speech intelligibility, and communicative participation [11]. Importantly, LSVT LOUD is based on principles of intensive amplitude-oriented motor training that emphasize motor skill acquisition, recalibration of self-perceived vocal effort through sensorimotor integration, and neuroplasticity, rather than strengthening the speech musculature alone. Swallowing rehabilitation has also advanced, with behavioral interventions such as expiratory muscle strength training demonstrating improvements in cough effectiveness, respiratory muscle performance, and selected swallowing outcomes, although the certainty of evidence remains lower than for speech rehabilitation because of the relatively small number of high-quality trials [12]. Despite these advances, many individuals with PD continue to experience persistent communication, swallowing and mobility limitations, incomplete response to rehabilitation, or reduced access to specialist services as the disease progresses. Consequently, complementary interventions such as singing remain of interest. Music engages distributed auditory, motor, cognitive, and limbic networks, and may support rehabilitation through auditory–motor coupling, rhythmic cueing, reward, motor learning, and activity-dependent neuroplasticity [13]. Singing represents a particularly multimodal form of musical activity, integrating respiratory control, phonation, articulation, posture, auditory feedback, cognitive engagement, and social participation, and may therefore complement established evidence-based rehabilitation approaches.

However, there is still fragmented evidence about singing therapy in PD, with studies varying in design, outcome measures, and sample characteristics. The available studies aimed to evaluate the effects of singing on various aspects of PD, including motor function, voice quality, swallowing, and quality of life (QoL). To assess the evidence of the effect of singing on PD symptoms and the main outcome measures used to date in the different studies, we conducted a systematic literature review. Specifically, the present review focuses on the effects of singing on both motor and non-motor symptoms, as well as its overall impact on patients’ QoL. By synthesizing the available evidence, the study seeks to provide a comprehensive understanding of the potential benefits and limitations of singing therapy in PD rehabilitation.

Methods

We conducted a systematic review of all available studies on singing interventions in patients with PD. A systematic search was performed using two electronic databases—PubMed and Google Scholar. Search covered the period until 30th December 2025, to capture the full development and evolution of singing-based interventions in PD. Relevant keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, including “Parkinson’s disease,” “sing,” “singing,” “voice,” and “music,” were used for the initial search. We also integrated our research with a manual screening of all references lists kept from retrieved articles, to capture any study not identified during the database search and ensure the inclusion of all potentially relevant studies.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined to guide the selection of studies for this systematic review. Studies were included in this review if they:Explicitly examined the effects of singing interventions in PD;

Were published in peer-reviewed journals in the English language;

Presented original interventional data (e.g., randomized-controlled trials, cohort studies, pilot studies, case reports, or qualitative research)

We excluded studies that were reviews or meta-analyses, conference abstracts, editorials, commentaries, or letters to the editor.

Methodological characteristics of included studies were evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal frameworks appropriate to each study design. Given the substantial heterogeneity of study designs and the exploratory nature of many included studies, the appraisal was used to inform interpretation of the evidence rather than to generate quantitative quality scores or exclude studies. A summary of the methodological strengths and limitations of each study is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

For each of the selected study, we collected the following data: methods, sample size, presence of a control group, duration of the intervention, and outcome measures.

We have organized the results into five thematic categories, to perform a structured synthesis of the mentioned articles and facilitate the classification of all measured outcome measures. Indeed, each category corresponded to a specific domain of PD symptomatology targeted by singing-based interventions. We identified the following categories: [1] Singing and Motor Functions, [2] Singing and Voice Quality, [3] Singing and Breathing Functions, [4] Singing and Swallowing, and [5] Singing, sspsychological well-being and Quality of Life (QoL).

Results

In PubMed, the initial search yielded 103 articles. After removing studies unrelated to singing interventions and screening for eligibility, 87 articles remained. Of these, 26 were selected for review based on the above-outlined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The Google Scholar search returned 37 articles; however, 35 of them did not meet the inclusion criteria. The other 2 articles retrieved from Google Scholar were duplicates already identified in the PubMed search. When looking at the study design, there was considerable methodological heterogeneity, including 5 randomized-controlled trials (RCTs), 16 open-label or uncontrolled interventional studies, and 5 case reports or qualitative investigations. The methodologies and experimental protocols varied substantially, leading to considerable heterogeneity in terms of interventions, outcome measures, and follow-up durations. Common methodological strengths included the use of validated outcome measures and detailed descriptions of singing interventions. However, several studies were limited by the absence of control groups, lack of randomization or blinding, short follow-up periods, and single-center recruitment, limiting the generalisability of the findings. A summary of the methodological characteristics, strengths, and limitations of each study is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Because of this heterogeneity, we decided against a formal meta-analysis and we systematically synthesized results, focusing on the main methodological features, quality assessments, and reported outcomes.

Table 2 shows details of the included studies characteristics.

Singing and motor functions

Seven studies have demonstrated that singing-based interventions may positively affect motor functions in patients with PD. For instance, Lee et al. [14] found that even a single session of therapeutic singing with semi-occluded vocal tract exercises suggested potential alleviations of motor symptoms as measured by the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale, Part III (UPDRS-III). In a similar single-session design, Stegemöller et al. [15] reported significant reductions in rigidity and bradykinesia, along with lowered cortisol levels. Expanding on these findings, Stegemöller et al. [16] tested a remote version of this intervention using prerecorded videos over 8 weeks to investigate the effects of a single one-hour group therapeutic singing session and reported statistically significant improvements in posture, tremor, and gait on the MDS-UPDRS.

Further studies show equally promising results. Butala et al. [17] found significant improvements in motor functions after 24 weeks in a randomized crossover trial of patients participating in weekly group singing. In line with this, Stegemöller et al. [18] observed significant improvements in motor scores, with a decrease in UPDRS-III score after 8 weeks, indicating potential neuromuscular benefits of singing-based interventions. Patients also seem to perceive the benefits of singing; indeed, a study [19] reported perceived improvements in posture, movement coordination, and breathing among participants in choral singing therapy.

Singing has also been proven to help gait. For example, Harrison et al. [20] explored singing as a rhythmic cueing method for gait, revealing reduced gait variability, particularly in step time and single support time, during walking while singing.

Overall, these findings suggest that singing may support motor symptom improvements in patients with PD, particularly in fine motor skills, posture and gait.

Singing and voice quality

Thirteen studies have investigated the impact of singing-based interventions on the voice quality of patients with PD, which include various factors, namely voice intensity, loudness, pitch, phonation time, jitter and shimmer, and, lastly, perceived voice control, and related confidence.

Studies focusing on singing and voice quality more broadly have yielded mixed results. To examine the short-term benefits, Lee et al., (146) examined the effects of semi-occluded vocal tract (SOVT) exercises in combination with singing. SOVT exercises involve exercises which included partial vocal tract occlusion, such as by phonating through a straw, lip trills, or humming, while vocalizing. SOVT exercises aim to improve vocal efficiency by narrowing the vocal tract to reduce vocal fold stress, promote easier and more balanced vibration, and increase acoustic output with less effort. The study findings revealed significant post-session improvements in the Acoustic Voice Quality Index for both the SOVT + singing and singing-only groups, indicating that even a single session can enhance vocal clarity and efficiency.

Shih et al. [21] provided 12 weekly 90-min singing sessions to 15 PD participants but reported no significant changes in vocal quality during natural speech, suggesting that the kind of intervention associated with higher intensity and duration, and the type of therapeutic method may play critical roles in achieving measurable improvements for patients.

Complementing these findings, Butala et al.’s [17] crossover trial (Parkinsonics) found that singing participants demonstrated gains in factors including conversational loudness, suggesting real-world benefits of vocal engagement. In contrast, however, the above-mentioned study by Shih et al. [21] found no significant changes in vocal loudness. Finally, Mohseni et al. [22] compared singing, speech therapy, and a combined approach across three matched groups. Their findings showed that vocal loudness increased most in the speech therapy and combined approach groups, suggesting that singing should be combined with speech therapy to enhance conversational loudness and voice quality overall.

In addition to voice intensity and conversational loudness, two studies also examine how singing interventions affect the pitch control and range of patients with PD. Good et al. [23] assessed two participant groups (N=22), patients with PD and age-matched controls, both of whom took part in a 12-week community-choir program, and found improvement in the lowest pitch. Mohseni et al. [22] provided further evidence that pitch range improved in both the singing and combined intervention groups, reiterating the recommendation that singing should be combined with speech therapy to yield better results for patients.

Kim et al. [24] compared singing and speaking tasks in patients with PD and cerebellar disease, finding that PD participants demonstrated better pitch and rhythm accuracy during singing. However, the above-mentioned study by Shih et al. [21] found no significant changes in pitch range following 12 90-min singing sessions, suggesting inconsistency possibly due to different designs and factors such as duration and intensity of the intervention.

Another factor that was investigated by five studies was maximum phonation time (MPT), a key marker of respiratory and vocal endurance. Di Benedetto et al. [25] combined group speech therapy and choral singing over 13 weeks in a cohort of 20 participants and found significant improvement in MPT, subglottic pressure, and prosody during reading tasks, suggesting that singing can enhance both breath support and expressive speech. Similarly, Stegemöller et al. [26] delivered an 8-week in-person singing program to 27 PD patients and reported significant improvements in MPT and respiratory pressure. Subsequently, Stegemöller et al. [27] study found that in eight participants, both MPT and respiratory control improved after engaging in prerecorded singing lessons. In a larger 12-month randomized trial, Tamplin et al. [28] also identified MPT improvements in groups receiving weekly or monthly singing sessions, with statistically significant improvements just in the singing groups, reinforcing the efficacy of consistent vocal practice. Finally, Good et al. [23] found that community-choir groups (N=22) also showed significant increases in MPT, further supporting the benefits of singing-based interventions for the phonatory control of patients with PD.

Four studies (mean number of participants 23, duration range 8–24 weeks) also explored jitter and shimmer outcomes in singing-based interventions, with two reporting no significant changes [17, 26] and two showing improvements [22, 23].

Finally, three studies explored perceived vocal control and communicative confidence in PD patients undergoing singing interventions, emphasizing subjective benefits despite limited objective acoustic changes. Brown and Stegemöller [29] found participants preferred singing over expiratory muscle strength training (EMST) for its perceived vocal benefits. Similarly, two studies [30, 31] reported improved vocal strength, intelligibility, and social confidence through qualitative interviews.

Singing and breathing functions

Assessment of respiratory function was an outcome measures in five studies. Indeed, breathing, lung strength, and diaphragm mobility are often compromised in PD for several reasons including for example deficit in the posture, restrictive ventilatory dysfunctions [32–34]. In light of this, singing protocols are typically designed with the aim of improving diaphragm use and lungs’ capacity.

Stegemöller et al. [26] found significant gains in maximum inspiratory and expiratory pressure, as well as phonation time, after an 8-week singing program, suggesting that singing strengthens the respiratory musculature. This was supported by another study [28] that investigated the impact of weekly singing sessions on patients with PD, reporting significant improvements in maximum expiratory pressure.

Furthermore, there are two studies that highlight the positive qualitative feedback of the perceived impact of therapeutic singing on respiratory functions [29, 30]. They documented positive participant experience in qualitative interviews, with patients reporting improved breathing strength after partaking in expiratory muscle strength training and group singing.

The immediate benefit of remote singing-based interventions for breathing functions has also been highlighted by Stegemöller et al. [27]. The study assessed the feasibility of the remote delivery of an 8-week singing programme, which showed an immediate improvement in the respiratory abilities of patients. Collectively, these studies underline the potential of singing to enhance the respiratory control and breathing functions of patients with PD.

Singing and swallowing

Three studies investigated the effects of singing interventions on swallowing function in patients with PD. Stegemöller et al. [18] evaluated the impact of an eight-week group singing program using surface electromyography (EMG). EMG showed increased laryngeal muscle activity, possibly standing for a major airway protection during swallowing, but none of these observed an improvement in self-reported swallowing.

Yeo et al. [35] implemented a 12-week individualized singing protocol and assessed swallowing using the Videofluoroscopic Dysphagia Scale (VDS). In that study there was an improvement of phonation time, but it was not linked to any videofluoroscopy changes. Another study [36] conducted a controlled trial with advanced-stage PD patients, comparing a 6-week singing-based intervention to a home-based oral motor exercise control group. While the singing group maintained their swallowing function and the control group experienced a decline, no significant difference was found on the VDS.

Singing, psychological well-being and quality of life

Overall, there are 16 research studies consistently demonstrating that singing interventions can significantly enhance quality of life (QoL) and psychological well-being in PD. Of these projects, seven controlled trials provide strong evidence for these effects. For instance, Tamplin et al. [28] highlighted the importance of intervention frequency, built up on weekly sessions, on the improvement of emotional, communicative, and social interaction, and they made it possible with comparisons with a group which underwent less frequent lessons and control conditions. That result was furtherly investigated by the same group in 2020 [37]. In this case, they showed that weekly group singing sessions not only improved voice-related QoL but also reduced stress and depression among both patients and caregivers. Two shorter-term studies [14, 38] observed significant reductions in depressive symptoms (at the Geriatric Depression Scale, Voice Handicap Index, and Beck Depression Inventory-II).

Also Butala et al. [17] reported sustained improvements in emotional well-being and body discomfort in the “Parkinsonics” crossover trial. Additionally, Pacchetti et al. [39] demonstrated that music therapy, including singing, produced superior outcomes in emotional well-being and daily functioning compared to physical therapy. This was in line with another study [26] reporting a subjective improvement in QoL. Furthermore, seven qualitative and mixed-methods studies highlight the emotional depth and social significance of singing in PD. Abell et al. [31] identified key themes, such as restored identity, enhanced mood, and social connectedness, while Hersh et al. [40] reported that songwriting and singing fostered self-worth and stronger interpersonal bonds. Similarly, Fogg-Rogers et al. [19] found that group singing stabilized emotions and encouraged social participation, and Stegemöller et al. [30] described singing as consistently uplifting and emotionally enriching.

Supporting this, Yeo et al. [35] showed that singing-enhanced swallowing therapy reduced anxiety around eating and communication while boosting confidence. Brown and Stegemöller [29] further emphasized that participants preferred singing over other therapeutic methods due to its enjoyment and emotional benefits. Finally, QoL outcomes and their way of changing were also investigated in relationship with the format of the intervention. Tamplin et al. [41] tested the ParkinSong Online telehealth program and, despite no significant QoL score changes, reported high engagement and satisfaction, suggesting emotional and social benefits. Differently, Rong and Benson [42] proposed intergenerational choirs as a means to foster motivation, identity, and connection. On the contrary, Shih et al. [21] found limited QoL improvements in a small in-person trial, likely due to intervention intensity. Irons et al. (2021) [43] confirmed that singing groups reduce stress, anxiety, and stigma internationally, with particularly strong outcomes in South Korea.

Collectively, the 16 findings underline that singing interventions offer holistic benefits, encompassing emotional, social, and physical domains, which support both psychological well-being and broader QoL for individuals living with PD.

Discussion

Singing-based interventions represent a promising multimodal and complementary rehabilitation approach for people with PD. In addition to the cardinal motor features of PD, including bradykinesia, rigidity, gait disturbance, and postural instability, speech and voice impairment, dysphagia, respiratory dysfunction, and mood symptoms can substantially affect patients’ quality of life. Although evidence-based rehabilitation approaches, including physical and occupational therapy, behavioral speech therapy, swallowing rehabilitation, and respiratory training, can improve several of these domains, symptoms may persist or progress over the course of the disease [44]. Moreover, speech, swallowing, and other axial symptoms often respond incompletely to dopaminergic therapy and may, in some individuals, be adversely affected by advanced treatments, including deep brain stimulation. These persistent and multidimensional rehabilitation needs support continued investigation of complementary non-pharmacological interventions that may be integrated within multidisciplinary PD care.

Within this context, our findings suggest that singing-based interventions may provide measurable benefits across several domains relevant to PD. Singing simultaneously engages respiratory control, phonation, articulation, auditory feedback, sensorimotor integration, and cognitive and emotional processes, offering a distinctive multimodal form of skilled motor practice. The potential role of singing should therefore not be considered as an alternative to established evidence-based rehabilitation, but rather as a complementary approach that may target overlapping functional domains and provide additional opportunities for intensive, engaging, and socially meaningful practice. Importantly, access to and engagement with singing opportunities may also be shaped by structural and societal factors, as many community-based choirs and singing groups operate outside formal healthcare systems and may not be routinely integrated into or supported by PD care pathways.

The reviewed studies generally showed and highlighted improvements in voice quality (e.g., loudness, pitch range, phonation time), respiratory control and overall QoL [27, 28, 40].

These findings emphasize the utility of singing training in improving not only voice loudness, as initially assumed, but also breathing functions. Such utility, moreover, has been shown to increase when alternative singing rehabilitation is combined with targeted speech, breathing and swallowing-oriented forms of therapy. Doing this, with a customized approach based on patients’ symptoms, would enhance therapeutic outcomes holistically.

Nevertheless, singing training has not yet been established as an official form of therapeutic intervention in PD, due to the lack of consistent results regarding which outcomes benefit most from it and which protocol is best suited for PD. For instance, voice can improve in loudness and intensity, but effects on speech remain unclear. Some improvements have been found in intelligibility and prosody while reading aloud, but no changes during spontaneous or natural speech have been demonstrated [21, 22], raising the question of whether singing has more effects on automatic verbal behaviors. Therefore, more comparative studies are needed, involving PD patients and healthy controls and with a stronger focus on comparing reading with natural speaking, in order to distinguish whether both spontaneous and planned speech would benefit from singing training.

Another gap is the lack of studies comparing speech impairments between different neurodegenerative diseases, for example parkinsonian and non-parkinsonian (e.g., cerebellar or upper motor neuron dysarthrias). Such studies would help understanding possible pathogenetic mechanisms of dysarthria. At the same time, evidence on the impact of singing on swallowing functions remains still unclear since the lack of standardized measures to assess it. Only a few studies have addressed this domain directly [17, 18]; however, they did so without a validated protocol. This gap highlights the need to validate objective methods and outcome measures to assess swallowing and then compare any change before and after a singing training protocol.

Finally, comparisons between online and face-to-face singing interventions should be interpreted with caution, as differences in social interaction, group dynamics, acoustic quality, technological factors, participant engagement, and accessibility may all influence therapeutic outcomes independently of the singing intervention itself.

Limitations

We specifically aimed to synthesize the evidence on singing-based interventions in PD and was not designed to assess their comparative effectiveness against established rehabilitation approaches. Accordingly, direct comparisons with physical, occupational, speech, swallowing, or respiratory rehabilitation interventions were beyond the scope of the present review. Although the findings should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that singing is equivalent or superior to established evidence-based therapies, they highlight its potential as a complementary, multimodal intervention that may engage overlapping respiratory, phonatory, sensorimotor, cognitive, and psychosocial processes. Future comparative effectiveness and adequately powered controlled studies are needed to determine whether singing provides additional or domain-specific benefits when integrated with established rehabilitation programs.

Another major limitation of the current literature lies in the small sample sizes, most studies involved fewer than 50 participants, and the predominance of non-randomized, non-blinded interventional designs. These study designs not only limit causal inference but also weaken the overall strength of the collected evidence. A formal item-level methodological quality appraisal was not undertaken because of the substantial heterogeneity of the included study designs. Instead, the methodological strengths and limitations of each study were systematically reviewed and summarized (Supplementary Table 1). Consequently, the findings should be interpreted with caution, particularly those derived from small proof-of-concept studies and uncontrolled interventions. These methodological limitations reduce the certainty of the available evidence despite generally encouraging findings.

Future directions

Therefore, further intervention-development and pilot studies are needed to address current gaps, better define the therapeutic targets, content, and dosage of singing-based interventions, and identify appropriate standardized outcome measures. These foundational data should subsequently inform the design of adequately powered randomized-controlled trials to validate efficacy and minimize bias.

Also, trials comparing PD with other neurological conditions are needed, with the aim of determining disease-specific versus broader neurological mechanisms.

Future research should also investigate the impact of singing-based interventions on caregiver well-being and the patient–caregiver relationship, as these may contribute to the overall therapeutic benefits of singing.

Finally, innovative delivery methods, including tele-rehabilitation and hybrid models, should be explored more to improve accessibility and promote long-term adherence to singing-based interventions.

Conclusion

In this systematic review, we aimed to point out evidence collected about the use of singing-based interventions as a complementary rehabilitation strategy for patients with PD. Singing can provide benefits in voice quality, respiratory control, and quality of life PD patients, domains often insufficiently addressed by pharmacological therapies. Effects on spontaneous speech intelligibility, prosody, and swallowing remain still unclear, highlighting the need for further refined investigations. Future studies will also need standardized, objective assessment tools and outcome measures. Finally, we can conclude that singing represents a promising, enjoyable and socially engaging intervention that can enrich PD holistic care and address unmet needs in its classical treatment methods.
