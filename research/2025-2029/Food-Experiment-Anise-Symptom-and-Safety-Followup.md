# Anise seed symptom and safety evidence, 2025–2029

## Ground anise capsules and composite menopausal symptoms in a clinical trial

Sixty menopausal women received anise or placebo for eight weeks. The first report describes weekly safety interviews and states that there were no adverse effects. It provides total symptom-index and mood measures with no isolated insomnia, fatigue or daytime sleepiness estimate. [Primary trial abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42152642/) · [Local source text](Source-Text/2026-Begum-Ground-Anise-Menopausal-Safety.txt)

## Companion report identifies mechanically ground anise fruit and symptom totals

The October 2026 report identifies authenticated room-dried Pimpinella anisum fruits milled through an 80-mesh screen. Participants received 667 mg per capsule, three capsules twice per day, or matched milled-rice capsules. Its analysis reuses data attributed to the 60-person Begum trial. The total Modified Kupperman Index fell more in the anise group. The report states no side effects and presents no separate insomnia, sleepiness or fatigue estimate. The 2025 review of the dissertation gives three capsules daily and calls side effects reported, without naming an event. The full dissertation or public registration record is needed to reconcile reporting. [Primary CC BY article and preparation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13653034/) · [Local source text](Source-Text/PMC13653034.txt)

## Companion supplement documents an insomnia item

Table S1 of the trial’s companion supplement explicitly lists insomnia among the individual Modified Kupperman Index symptom scores. The supplement describes the fields and machine-learning labels, without participant data or an arm-level insomnia result. The main article reports total scores. This is an assessed human endpoint with an unreleased estimate. ## Methanolic anise seed extract and experimental nociception

Jawed and colleagues first published November 30, 2025. The original abstract reports methanolic Pimpinella anisum seed extract in experimental hot-plate, tail and writhing nociception paradigms. The abstract gives evidence of changed nociceptive behavior and describes inflammation and fever tests. Full numerical effects, administration context and statistical details of individual experiments remain open. The extract in these models supplies no direct patient estimate of pain after eating ground anise.
