# Habitual green tea intake and nonmotor outcomes in Parkinson’s disease

Sakakima and colleagues compared 105 community-dwelling older people with Parkinson’s by tea-use group and analyzed daily green tea quantity in 78 daily drinkers. Across three quantity groups, binary hyposmia prevalence ranged from 66.7% to 83.3% (p=0.519), and mean walking speed ranged from 0.85 to 0.90 m/s (p=0.812). The hyposmia comparison addresses prevalence rather than symptom severity. The observational groups reflect habitual intake with no standardized brewing protocol. [Primary report, PMID 42816813, Tables 1–2](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/02601060261493252?rfr_dat=cr_pub++0pubmed&rfr_id=ori%3Arid%3Acrossref.org&url_ver=Z39.88-2003)

Tables 3–4 report cognition, hallucinations and psychosis, depressed mood, anxious mood, apathy, sleep problems, and constipation. Sleep problems and constipation showed inverse correlations with tea intake, with adjusted odds ratios of 0.57 and 0.71, respectively, in the selected models. The report gives no separate fatigue or handwriting result. The evidence addresses adjacent outcomes for the frozen green tea fatigue and handwriting claims. [Primary report, Tables 3–5](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261493252)

## Commercial green tea and acute task fatigue in healthy men

Kurosaka et al. reported a study in 22 healthy young men in July 2025. All participants completed sessions with no drink, water, and commercially available green tea in that fixed order on the same day. The tea and water sessions each provided 210 mL. In supplement Table S4, mean drowsiness scores changed from 9.50 to 8.27 in the tea session and 8.59 to 10.14 with water. The article reports within-session p-values of .0693 and .037, respectively. Mean local pain or dullness increased in all three conditions, from 7.05 to 7.91 with tea and 6.59 to 7.23 with water. The fatigue analyses give no between-condition change estimate with uncertainty. The experiment measured acute task responses outside Parkinson’s and supplies indirect, mixed context for `0948`. ## Tea and coffee in cohorts with isolated REM sleep behavior disorder

Yuan and colleagues reported results January 6, 2025 from 141 followed participants in a Beijing cohort. Thirty-six developed a clinical α-synucleinopathy, including 21 diagnosed with Parkinson’s. Ever drinking tea was associated with a lower adjusted hazard of pooled conversion (HR 0.35, 95% CI 0.17–0.73). The daily tea comparison was similar, and the continuous cumulative tea amount and coffee comparisons were inconclusive. Tea varieties were pooled. [Primary report, Table 1 and Figure 3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41531-024-00856-2)

Xie and colleagues reported a separate cohort October 16, 2026. Forty-five of 287 participants with isolated REM sleep behavior disorder developed an α-synucleinopathy over a median 4.2 years. Higher coffee consumption was associated with lower pooled conversion hazard (adjusted HR 0.30, 95% CI 0.11–0.78). The threshold analysis compared consumption less than daily with daily consumption (HR 3.08, 95% CI 1.09–8.68). These observational comparisons concern the onset of a diagnosed disorder after isolated REM sleep behavior disorder. They give no estimates of coffee or tea effects on symptom severity in people already diagnosed with Parkinson’s. [Primary report and exposure comparisons](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mds.70562) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42888868/)
