# Observations for 1865-1869

## Major observations

- The archive starts with an English eponym in print and with bedside efforts to distinguish classic shaking palsy from look-alikes. Trousseau documented conspicuous slowness when shaking was slight, while Ordenstein compared the observed tremor and course of two different disorders. [Naming](../History-and-Context.md) · [Clinical sources](../Clinical-and-Diagnosis.md)

## Minor observations

- One-sided presentation, fatigue, cognitive changes, and pneumonia were already topics in the case literature. [Clinical sources](../Clinical-and-Diagnosis.md) · [Daily life](../Nonmotor-and-Daily-Life.md)
- A single case recorded worse shaking after tea, coffee, and spirits. Sanders’s textbook listed a supportive regimen and conflicting results with electricity. [Food and nutrition](../Food-and-Nutrition.md) · [Devices](../Procedures-and-Devices.md)

## Comparison with pre-1865 sources

- James Parkinson’s 1817 essay described the shaking palsy through six case histories. The Charcot and Vulpian series used the French eponym in 1861. The 1865–1869 sources document English usage and more explicit clinical differentiation. [Parkinson’s essay](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/23777) · [Charcot and Vulpian facsimile](https://www.fichier-pdf.fr/2014/03/09/charcot-vulpian/) · [Naming correspondence](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41582-019-0160-x)

## Collection gaps

- No comparative dietary evidence or confirmed remedy has been collected for this bucket. Sanders’s textbook bibliography points to earlier case reports for verification.
