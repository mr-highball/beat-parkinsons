Reported direction: Longstanding customary use as a urinary-tract flushing aquaretic/diuretic, carminative, and kidney-stone folk remedy; accompanied by traditional pregnancy cautions for apiol-rich seed/oil and zero Parkinson's disease clinical efficacy claims.
Authenticity: Traceable historical herbal text (Grieve 1931, HTTP 200 verified at botanical.com/botanical/mgmh/p/parsle09.html) corroborated by peer-reviewed 2024 community prevalence data (PMC11576924, n=395 in Hail, Saudi Arabia) and renal review (PMC11672790).
Efficacy evidence: Customary traditional practice and community prevalence survey without controlled urinary-urgency or PD endpoints; notably, a randomized human crossover trial of 1,200 mL/day parsley leaf tea (Alyami & Rabah 2011, PMID:21566309) showed no significant change in 24-hour urine volume.

Native to the central and eastern Mediterranean basin, Petroselinum crispum (parsley) has been documented since Dioscorides' De Materia Medica (1st century CE) and compiled in Mrs. M. Grieve's A Modern Herbal (1931) as a traditional carminative and urinary-tract flushing diuretic (aquaretic) used for gravel, kidney stones, and mild lower-urinary complaints. Contemporary Middle Eastern ethnobotanical practice reflects this continuity: in a 2024 cross-sectional survey of 395 adults in Hail, Saudi Arabia (Aldarwish et al., Cureus, PMC11576924), 44.6% (176/395) reported using parsley infusions or preparations to prevent or manage kidney stones. Traditional European phytotherapy monographs (German Commission E / ESCOP) distinguish culinary parsley leaf from concentrated seed/essential oil fractions rich in apiol and myristicin (contraindicated in pregnancy due to uterotonic toxicity and in renal/cardiac edema) and flag wild poison hemlock (Conium maculatum) misidentification hazards.
