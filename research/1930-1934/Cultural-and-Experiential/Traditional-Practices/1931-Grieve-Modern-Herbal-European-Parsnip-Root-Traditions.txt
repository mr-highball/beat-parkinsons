In A Modern Herbal (1931), Mrs. M. Grieve compiled centuries of European and British culinary-herbal accounts of Pastinaca sativa, drawing on John Gerard (1597) and Nicholas Culpeper (1653). Historical herbalists explicitly distinguished the mild, sweet cultivated garden parsnip ('tame', prized as a starchy winter restorative food, Lenten dish, and base for rustic Irish/British parsnip beer, wine, and marmalades) from the acrid wild parsnip, whose root and seed decoctions were traditionally employed as diuretics for kidney gravel/visceral obstructions, carminatives for colic and side-stitches, and remedies for intermittent fevers. Regional European folk traditions (Serbia, Italy) similarly recorded root or leaf infusions for digestion and diuresis (Khatua et al., 2026, PMC13150342). Crucially, Pastinaca sativa root holds NO European Medicines Agency (EMA/HMPC) traditional herbal monograph today, is regulated strictly as a culinary vegetable, and carries well-documented contact phytophotodermatitis risks from furanocoumarin-rich wild parsnip sap.
