# Food and Nutrition for 2000–2004

### 2004 — Correspondence on a mostly plant-based diet in Parkinson’s

Roberta H. Schwartz published a one-page letter in 2004 commenting on McCarty’s dietary risk hypothesis. The indexed record supplies no abstract. Perea-Sasiaín and Hanfling Schwartz identify it as the earlier report for the male case in their 2014 publication, which describes a near-vegan dietary change during levodopa treatment. The 2004 publisher body was restricted during review, so the earlier observations and any differences from the expanded account remain unverified. [Primary 2004 bibliographic record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15193373/) · [Publisher DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mehy.2004.01.016) · [Expanded 2014 account](../2010-2014/Food-Experiment-Food-Followup.md#two-dietary-case-histories-from-colombia)

### 2000 — Japanese survey of foods reported difficult to swallow

Hiro Ogoshi’s article on food development for an aging population is the source cited for a questionnaire of 358 older adults and 243 middle-aged adults. The original article’s bibliographic record is available through Japan Science and Technology Agency. Ogoshi’s 2007 publication reproduces the rankings and discusses baked sweet potato. The original article body has yet to be verified. [2000 bibliographic record](https://jglobal.jst.go.jp/detail?JGLOBAL_ID=200902151390819900) · [2007 author report, Table 1 and reference 4](../2005-2009/Food-Experiment-Food-Followup.md#baked-sweet-potato-in-a-japanese-swallowing-questionnaire)

### 2000 — Coffee, caffeine, and incidence in the Honolulu cohort

Ross and colleagues studied incident Parkinson’s disease in a cohort of men followed for decades. Higher coffee and total caffeine intake were associated with lower incidence, including after accounting for smoking. The cohort design captured intake before diagnosis, with residual confounding and prodromal behavior still possible. [Original publication via PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10819950/)

### 2001 — Caffeine associations differed between men and women

Ascherio and colleagues analyzed two prospective U.S. cohorts. Higher caffeine intake was associated with lower incidence among men. Among women, a U-shaped association had its lowest observed risk at intermediate intake. Decaffeinated coffee did not show the same male association. [Original publication via PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11456310/)

### 2001 — Lightheadedness during standing and after a liquid meal

Mehagnoul-Schipper and colleagues compared 17 patients with Parkinson’s disease or parkinsonism with 17 controls. The standing test took place before a standardized drink containing Nutrical and lactose-free whole milk. In the separate meal test, postprandial hypotension occurred in 14 patients and seven controls. Two patients scored in the combined dizziness, lightheadedness or restlessness category after the drink. Levodopa and placebo periods showed no clear difference in post-meal symptoms. The study did not test standing after the drink or include brown rice. [Primary report, December 2001](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/56.12.M749) · [Full paper, Methods and Results](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=b9856f22b075bd8e998ae4d259a19684cba52f05&repid=rep1&type=pdf)

### 2002 — Dietary vitamin E and supplements in prospective cohorts

Zhang and colleagues found lower Parkinson’s incidence with higher vitamin E from foods in two U.S. cohorts. Supplemental vitamins E and C and carotenoids lacked similar associations. Food composition and correlated behaviors leave the active exposure uncertain. [Original publication via PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12391343/)

### 2002 — Higher dairy intake associated with incidence among men

Chen and colleagues assessed diet before diagnosis in two prospective cohorts. Higher dairy intake was associated with Parkinson’s incidence among men. An analogous association was not clear among women, and several nutrients from nondairy sources did not account for the male finding. [Original publication via PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12447934/)

### 2003 — Iron and manganese in a population case-control study

Powers and colleagues found higher odds of Parkinson’s disease with higher estimated dietary iron, particularly when manganese intake was also high. Fats and antioxidants showed no strong associations in this study. Retrospective dietary measurement and correlated exposures make the result hypothesis-generating. [Original publication via PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12796527/)

### 2003 — Alcohol consumption and Parkinson’s incidence

Hernán and colleagues examined alcohol intake in two prospective cohorts with 415 incident cases. Overall intake, wine, and spirits showed no clear association with incidence. Some beer categories showed an inverse association, with uncertainty about beverage-specific findings and residual confounding. [Original publication via PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12891669/)

### 2004 — Single-occasion Mucuna pruriens crossover study

Katzenschlager and colleagues compared a levodopa-containing seed powder with standard levodopa plus a decarboxylase inhibitor in eight people with fluctuating Parkinson’s disease. The higher tested Mucuna exposure produced earlier onset and longer on time. A participant withdrew after vomiting. The acute design could not establish long-term comparative efficacy, tolerability, or equivalence across preparations. [Original publication via PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15548480/)
