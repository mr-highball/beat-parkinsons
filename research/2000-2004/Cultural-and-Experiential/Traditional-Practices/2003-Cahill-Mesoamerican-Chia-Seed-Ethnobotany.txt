Title: Ethnobotany of chia (Salvia hispanica L.) soaked beverages for endurance, hydration, and digestion
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://doi.org/10.1663/0013-0001(2003)057[0604:EOCSHL]2.0.CO;2
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2003-12
Date basis: Published issue date of Economic Botany 57(4):604-618 (December 2003); historical Nahua/Aztec and Tarahumara practices predate publication by centuries.
Reviewed: 2026-11-25 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional culinary-medicinal stamina, hydration, and mucilaginous digestive uses; no Parkinson-specific clinical efficacy claim.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed ethnobotanical monograph (Cahill 2003, Economic Botany 57(4):604-618) corroborated by Ullah et al. 2016 (PMC4926888).
Efficacy evidence: Traditional dietary context only; controlled human endurance crossovers (Illian 2011; Nieman 2015) found null athletic performance effects and PD clinical trials are absent.

Ethnobotanical documentation (Cahill 2003, Economic Botany 57(4):604-618, DOI 10.1663/0013-0001(2003)057[0604:EOCSHL]2.0.CO;2; corroborated in Ullah et al. 2016, PMID:27413203 / PMC4926888) traces Salvia hispanica L. (chian / chia) across pre-Columbian Mesoamerican (Aztec/Nahua) and Rarámuri/Tarahumara foodways in southern Mexico and northern Guatemala.

Traditional preparation notably relied on SOAKING whole or ground chia seeds in water (e.g., chia fresca / iskiate) to form a hydrated mucilaginous beverage consumed for sustained physical endurance, hydration, and digestive comfort, as well as poultices and eye-mucilage rinses.

Chronological and evidentiary boundaries: Publication date (2003) is distinct from pre-Columbian/colonial historical attributions (e.g., Codex Mendoza). Traditional endurance use does not establish efficacy for Parkinson's fatigue or other neurological symptoms, and modern controlled running crossovers in athletes (Illian 2011; Nieman 2015) showed no performance advantage over carbohydrate or water controls.
