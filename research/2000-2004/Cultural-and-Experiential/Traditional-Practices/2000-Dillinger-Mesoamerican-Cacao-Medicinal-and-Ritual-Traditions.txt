Dillinger et al. (2000, Journal of Nutrition, PMID:10917925) surveyed Mesoamerican and 16th-to-20th century European and New Spain documents tracing ritual and medicinal uses of cacao (Theobroma cacao) originating among the Olmec, Maya, and Mexica (Aztec). Early colonial records include the 1552 Badianus Codex citing cacao flowers for fatigue and the 1590 Florentine Codex prescribing cacao beans blended with maize and tlacoxochitl (Calliandra anomala) for fever, shortness of breath, and faintness. Across more than 100 recorded historical medicinal roles, three recurrent traditional themes emerged: restoring weight in emaciated patients, stimulating the nervous systems of apathetic, exhausted, or mentally fatigued individuals, and supporting digestion and bowel elimination. Publication date (2000) is explicitly separated from claimed historical age (pre-Columbian through early 20th century), and traditional bitter cacao infusions or spiced mixtures differ substantially from modern solid plain dark chocolate bars. None of these historical accounts assessed diagnosed Parkinson's disease.
