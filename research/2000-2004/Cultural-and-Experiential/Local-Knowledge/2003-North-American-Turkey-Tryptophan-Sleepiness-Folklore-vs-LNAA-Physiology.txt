Title: North American holiday folklore attributing post-meal drowsiness to turkey tryptophan versus human LNAA transport physiology
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12499331/
Source kind: Local-knowledge record
Publication date: 2003-01-01
Date basis: Published human meal-crossover study (Wurtman et al., Am J Clin Nutr 2003; PMID:12499331), paired with Fernstrom 2013 (PMID:22677921) and Schernhammer et al. 2009 (PMC2744623).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Popular holiday folklore asserts roasted turkey causes sleepiness via L-tryptophan; human meal crossover evidence (Wurtman 2003) and melatonin epidemiology (Schernhammer 2009) contradict this premise, showing high-protein meals depress the plasma Trp:LNAA ratio by 54% while large carbohydrate-heavy holiday meals drive postprandial drowsiness.
Authenticity: Traceable peer-reviewed human physiological literature contextualizing widespread North American dietary folklore, inspected during Round 7 review.
Efficacy evidence: Folklore claim lacks clinical support; controlled human LNAA kinetics directly explain why plain roasted turkey meat does not selectively raise brain tryptophan or melatonin.

In North American popular culture, post-Thanksgiving drowsiness is routinely blamed on L-tryptophan in roasted turkey. Biochemical and human feeding data directly challenge this belief: turkey breast contains tryptophan levels comparable to chicken, beef, and fish alongside abundant competing branched-chain and aromatic large neutral amino acids (LNAAs; Czech et al. 2024, PMC11082980). In a controlled human crossover study of normal meals (Wurtman et al. 2003, PMID:12499331; reviewed in Fernstrom 2013, PMID:22677921), a high-protein meal actually lowered the plasma Trp:LNAA ratio by a median 54% relative to a high-carbohydrate meal (p<0.01), reducing competitive tryptophan transport across the blood-brain barrier. Moreover, Nurses' Health Study biomarker data in 998 women found no association between dietary tryptophan or poultry (including turkey) intake and overnight urinary melatonin (Schernhammer et al. 2009, PMC2744623). Perceived holiday somnolence is more plausibly linked to large meal volume, carbohydrate-driven insulin responses, alcohol, and fatigue than to turkey meat itself.
