Abstract

Purpose

Healthy food consumption involves regular fruit intake. Inadequate fruit consumption is associated with disease burdens such as cancer, hypertension,
stroke, and diabetes. As such, an individual may choose to avoid health repercussions by adjusting his/her lifestyle. Individuals with long-term healthy
lifestyle habits are likely high fruit consumers. However, few studies seem to have focused on the relationship certain aggregated lifestyle factors i.e. screen
time; physical exercise; work, and sleep have with fruit consumption. Thus, the study sought to investigate the relationship between these aggregated
lifestyle factors and fruit consumption of university students in Uganda.

Methods

The study used a correlational survey that was quantitative in nature. The study population covered 59,998 university students. We sampled 403 students
using proportionate stratiﬁed random sampling. We collected data through face-to-face interviews using a questionnaire. We analyzed the data using SPSS
(V23) and AMOS (V23) software for correlational and Conﬁrmatory factor tests.

Results

The results revealed that a university student’s lifestyle signiﬁcantly inﬂuenced his/her fruit consumption (β = 5.470, t-value = 5.481, p-value = 0.000). The
squared Multiple Correlation is 0.176.

Conclusion

Considering the health beneﬁts of fruit consumption and basing on the study results, health educators need to encourage the fruit consumption through
adjusting one’s lifestyle (exercises, screen time, work life and sleep time).

1.0 Introduction
Food is consumed for different reasons that include acquisition of health [1]. Healthy food consumption involves regular fruit intake [2]. Inadequate fruit
consumption is associated with disease burdens such as cancer, hypertension, stroke, and diabetes [3]. In order to reduce these disease burdens,
governments have employed various policies that include the establishment health care facilities but often get mixed results [4]. The World Health
Organization, on its part, suggests a global action plan that partly requires governments to encourage their populations to increase fruit consumption [5] to at
least ﬁve daily servings (200g a day) for a healthy body [6].

Scholars, on their part, suggest the use of theoretical explanations to understand certain happenings [7] and thus aid the future successful behavioral
adjustment [8]. Cockerham [9], for instance, advanced the Health Lifestyle theory to explain the actions behind individuals trying to live a healthy life. The
Health Lifestyle Theory asserts that an individual will choose (be predisposed) to act in a particular way because of life chances availed to him by his social
status [9]. In other words, one’s social position provides the options from which he/she chooses his/her lifestyle options. The choices then create behaviours
that either positively or negatively inﬂuence the person’s mental and physical body that form a lifestyle. Accordingly, an individual may choose to avoid health
repercussions by adjusting his/her lifestyle [9].

1.1 Lifestyle

Every individual has an environment in which they live and interact. Anandarajan & Arunadevi [10] refer to the ‘interface between the individual and the
immediate environment’ as lifestyle. Lifestyle represents one’s lived pattern of spending time and money [11]. It is a lived pattern as shown by one’s activities,
interests and opinions [12]. This pattern includes, work [10], screen time, physical activity, and sleep [13]. Screen time is the time one spends watching
television, using computers, playing video games, surﬁng the internet [14], talking, and texting on mobile phones [15]. Physical activity [16] is any physical
body movement that results from muscle movement [17]. Sleep comprises the duration one takes during the day of sleep [18]. Understanding an individual’s
lifestyle leads to a possible understanding of related activities [10].
1.2 Lifestyle and Fruit consumption

According to Wallace et al. [2], individuals with long-term healthy lifestyle habits are likely high fruit consumers. For instance, scholars report that the more
people watch television (TV) during mealtime the less they consume healthy foods like fruits [19]. On the other hand, Chan et al. [20] reported that no
association existed between playing electronic games and eating fruits. Joo et al. [21], additionally, reported that the longer the hours a youth exercised, the
more fruits and vegetables they consumed and the less snacks they ate. This is conforms with Shatwan et al., [22] research that concluded that the more
college students increased their physical activity the more they followed a fruit diet. In a Finish adolescents study, Heikkilä et al., [23], reported that the more
physical sporting activities one undertakes, the more fruits he/she consumed. Manz et al., [24], similarly reports that the more children and adolescents
exercised the more fruits they ate. However, Koehler et al. [25] reports that high physical activity levels have no association with fruit consumption. In
addition, Kehoe et al. [26] study of Indian women revealed that a heavy and busy work schedule is associated with decreased fruit intake. Similarly, Cao et al.
[27] study of school children in China revealed that short sleep durations were associated with less fruit consumption among girls. Theorell-Haglöw et al.’s
[28] study of adults, relatedly, revealed that the shorter the sleep and the poorer the sleep quality sleep an adult gets, the less likely they adhere to a fruit diet.

On the contrary, Krietsch et al. [29] systematic review revealed that several studies indicated a no association between total sleep time and fruit and snack
consumption among the youth. This research therefore hypothesizes that:
There is a positive relationship between lifestyle and fruit consumption of university students in Uganda.
Despite several empirical studies applying the Health Lifestyle Theory to preventive health behaviors [30], few seem to have focused on the relationship
certain aggregated lifestyle factors i.e. screen time, physical exercise, work and sleep have with fruit consumption [9]. The available studies seem to apply
the Health Lifestyle Theory on life expectancy [31]; and educational attainment [32]. Additionally, these studies mainly focus on the elderly within Europe, Asia
and the United States of America [33–35] with seemingly no studies on the African Continent. The available studies, also, portray a seemingly inconclusive
stand on the relationship between these lifestyle factors and fruit consumption [29]. To ﬁll this gap, this study investigates the relationship between the
aggregated lifestyle factors – screen time, physical exercise, work, sleep – and fruit consumption of university students in Uganda.

2.0 Methods
To test the study hypotheses and to determine the relationship between lifestyle and fruit consumption, we used a correlational survey approach. The study
population totaled to 59,998 Makerere and Kyambogo Universities’ students situated within Uganda as at December 2022 [36, 37]. Uganda is situated in East
Africa, west of Kenya and north of Tanzania. Uganda possesses one of the youngest world populations.
We selected 403 students as sample based on Yamane’s [38] formulae using the stratiﬁed random sampling method. The university student population was
the basis of the strata. We chose students based on their will to join in the study. We acquired an ethical clearance from Makerere University Business
School’s Faculty of Graduate Studies and research before data collection. We distributed 580 questionnaires and received 425 questionnaires and then
discarded 22 unusable ones since according to Hair et al. [39], at least 30% items were not ﬁlled. Hence, we got a 69.48% response rate [40]. We collected
data using adapted questionnaire items that we later tested for both reliability and validity. To assess reliability, we pilot tested 55 questionnaires at the
Islamic University in Uganda (situated along the Mbale-Soroti road in Uganda’s eastern region around one mile north of Mbale's central business district). The
Cronbach’s alpha coeﬃcient for all scales had coeﬃcients above 0.7.
The ﬁnal instrument comprised demographic items; including age, religion, gender, education, residential status, partner lived with, student nationality, region
of origin and the constructs of lifestyle and fruit consumption:
We measured lifestyle in terms of screen time, physical exercising, work life, and sleep time. We operationalized screen-time as the time one spends
watching television, e-mailing, playing video games, internet use [14], talking and texting on mobile phones [15]. We used adapted scales [41, 42] for
measurement. We added ‘talking on phone’ as a scale item. Responses ranged from ‘0 hours’ to ‘more than 5 hours a day’ of screen media usage. We
operationalized physical exercising as the time a person spends physically moving his body resulting from muscle movement [17]. We measured it using
items: Aerobics, Jogging or running, playing soccer, swimming, riding a bicycle and dancing adapted from Kowalski, Crocker, and Kowalski, [43]. We added
the item weightlifting on the instrument. Responses ranged from ‘0 hours’ to ‘more than 5 hours a day’ of physical exercising. We operationalized ‘work life’ to
comprise of ‘the time a university student takes working or studying’ [44]. We adapted the questionnaire items [45]. We further divided ‘studying’ to include
‘reading at home’ and ‘working on lecture assignments’. Responses ranged from ‘0 hours’ to ‘more than 5 hours a day’ of doing Lecture assignments and
housework. We operationalized sleep time as the duration an individual takes during the day asleep [18]. We adapted the questionnaire items [45]. The items
included: sleeping during the weekends in a semester and sleeping during the weekends in a holiday. Responses ranged from ‘4 hours or less’ to ’11 hours or
more’ of sleep.
Fruit Consumption corresponds to the act of taking fruits through the mouth to the body [46]. We adapted the Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) [47] and
included fruit items; pineapples, apples, and watermelon [2] that are largely consumed in Uganda. We required respondents to indicate their fruit
consumption frequency within the preceding year using scale anchors that ranged from ‘Never or less than once a month’ to ‘more than 6 times a day’.

4.0 Discussion

Fruit consumption is associated with various health beneﬁts. Literature suggests that one’s lifestyle possibly inﬂuences his/her fruit consumption. This study,
hence, sought to investigate the relationship between lifestyle and fruit consumption among university students in Uganda. The ﬁnding revealed that lifestyle
has a signiﬁcant positive inﬂuence on fruits’ consumption among university students.

However, distinctive lifestyle factors, speciﬁcally, related differently with fruit consumption. First, the result means that the more time a university student
spends doing exercises the more they shall eat fruits. In other words when one goes for a jogging session then they will likely eat either pineapples or
mangoes or apples or watermelons. This is probably because of the body’s need to refresh as well as replace lost energy after an exercise session. This
concurs with Shatwan et al. [22] ﬁndings that physically active college students were high fruit consumers. The ﬁnding, also, means that the more screen
time a student experiences, the more likely he consumes fruits. It implies that playing video games facilitates the consumption of pineapples and apples
among university students in Uganda. This is probably due to students taking these fruits as snacks having been preoccupied with video games, which
hinders their urge to prepare meals or look for already prepared meals at the time. This result contradicts Fan et al. [51] who stated that the more
adolescents played video games, the less fruits they consumed. The ﬁnding, likewise, implies that the more a student does house chores the more they eat
fruits. This suggests that the more students do house work like cleaning, washing clothes, mopping they the more mangoes and watermelons they eat. This
is probably due to the fruits availability within and around their residences that make it easy to eat these fruits during work breaks. This ﬁnding however
contradicts Kehoe et al.’s [26] study of Indian women that reported their reduced fruit consumption was partly attributed to heavy workload during the day.
The result, also, indicates that the longer a student spends doing class work the less they consume fruits. In other words the more time a student spends
completing lecture assignments, the less they eat mangoes and apples. This is probably because doing lecture assignments for long hours a day occupies
the time that would otherwise be invested in accessing and eating of fruits. This result concurs with Hilger-Kolb and Diehl [52] who showed that a fruit
consumption decline among German university students was attributed to lack of time that studying creates. The ﬁnding, likewise, shows that the more time
a student sleeps the less they consume fruits. This means that the longer the sleep hours students get the less mangoes and apples they eat. This is
probably because they skip the time for purchasing these fruits. This result concurs with Holmes et al. [53] who states that short sleep durations in preschool
aged children is related to the increased fruit intake. It however contradicts Theorell-Haglöw et al. [28] who asserted that the more sleep time adults receive
the more they consume fruits.

4.1 Implications

This study has both academic and policy implications. From an academic standpoint, there is a signiﬁcant positive relationship between lifestyle and fruit
consumption. The ﬁnding supports the Health Lifestyle Theory [9], which assumes that an individual’s choice of action will result from their life chances that
are availed by his/her social status. The students chose to eat fruits given that they largely lived on off campus residences where they could easily ﬁnd these
foods. These ﬁndings also have important policy implications. The results show a continuous need for consumer education regarding health beneﬁts of
adhering to a fruit diet through one’s lifestyle. Either university management or government bodies should promote the health promotion campaigns.
4.2 Limitation and future research

The present study is not without limitations, but in turn offers opportunity for future studies. The study results are based on a case study of university
student’s in Uganda. There is need for further extension of these studies to other countries and populations before making generalizations. The study results
opened a way for assessing fruit consumption in Uganda. Further, it would be valuable when future researchers consider other lifestyle factors’ effects on
fruit consumption. Consumers’ demographics might also have a different effect on their fruit consumption, which future studies should investigate.

5.0 Conclusion

We conducted this study to investigate the relationship between lifestyle and fruit consumption among university students in Uganda. The study results lead
to the conclusion that a university student’s lifestyle positively relates to his/her fruit consumption. However, the associations between different lifestyle
factors and fruit consumption varies. In other words, the time spent using screen-gadgets; physically exercising; working and sleeping may inﬂuence a
university student’s consumption of fruits like mangoes, apples, pineapples, and watermelons. This study provides preliminary evidence for the association
of certain lifestyle factors and fruit consumption, speciﬁcally among university students in Uganda. However future research should focus on the effect other
lifestyle factors have on fruit consumption.
