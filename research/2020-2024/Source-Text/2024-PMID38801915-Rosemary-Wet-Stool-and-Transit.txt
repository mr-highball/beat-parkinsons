Title: Rosemary leaf extract and two distinct bowel models
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38801915/
Original preprint: https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-3955507/v1
PMID: 38801915
DOI: 10.1016/j.jep.2024.118395
Preprint DOI: 10.21203/rs.3.rs-3955507/v1
Evidence period: 2020-2024
Date basis: 2024 original preprint and journal publication. The journal posted its version online on 2024-05-25.
Review basis: Original preprint full text, sections 2.1, 2.6–2.7, 3.3–3.4 and figure captions, checked against the journal abstract. Treat these as a single family.
Source type: Controlled animal and isolated-tissue experiments.

Naqvi and colleagues tested a hydroethanolic rosemary leaf extract. The laxative assay administered three lower levels orally to fasted BALB/c mice (six per group). The protocol describes no prior induction of constipation. The outcome was a wet-stool percentage reported as 19.71 ± 1.16% in the vehicle group, and 31.57 ± 3.81%, 41.50 ± 3.52% and 68.01 ± 8.67% in the three extract groups (author-reported p<.001 for each). Fecal consistency and frequency also contributed to a separate laxation score.

In a separate castor-oil diarrhea model, the investigators used higher extract levels and an injection route. Charcoal traveled 36.47 cm in the sham group and 75.52 cm in the diarrhea control. At two higher extract levels it traveled 46.49 and 27.82 cm. The authors report reduced intestinal secretion and diarrhea scores, with details in sections 2.7 and 3.4. These are distinct routes, dose ranges and assays, so the signs do not demonstrate a crossover at any particular oral exposure.

For Round 1 claim 0943, increased wet-stool counts establish an oral experimental signal for an extract. The opposite transit sign and absence of a tested link from ground rosemary intake to composition, dose and constipation severity in Parkinson disease leave the culinary direction open.
