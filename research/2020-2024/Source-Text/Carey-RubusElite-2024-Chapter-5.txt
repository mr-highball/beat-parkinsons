    1.   Introduction

The following two chapters will outline and report the results of key outcomes from a
collaborative,     multi-disciplinary,      multi-institution      proof    of    concept     human
randomised     controlled    trial (RCT)      investigating    the recovery      and   performance
efficacy of the RubusElite prototype beverage, a culmination of research efforts on the
RubusElite project. This RCT is a result of close collaboration between the School of
Food and Nutritional Sciences UCC and the Department of Sport and Exercise Science
SETU Waterford.

This preface will provide further information about the design of the RCT and will
also inform on the research findings and future analyses led by other research groups
as part of the RubusElite Project. This research was critical to support and facilitate
the current PhD but are not explicitly presented as results of this PhD thesis.



    2.   RubusElite Beverage Design

The RubusElite beverage designed as part of this project was ultimately created by the
RubusElite food science team. The nutritional specifications resulted from multiple
iterations of a ‘Paper Prototype’ provided to the Food Science team following findings
from Chapters 2, 3 and 4 of this PhD thesis. This paper prototype acts as an ideal
nutritional profile with key targets for the food science team to meet within their
product stability and acceptability challenges. An example of one of these iterations is
presented    in Table     1. Further     factors   related   to beverage      stability, digestion,
bioavailability and bioacessibility were accounted for in further preclinical trials led
by the nutritional biochemistry team and the food science team. Much of the results of
these investigations have been recently published peer reviewed journals (van de
Langerijt, O’Mahony and Crowley, 2022; van de Langerijt et al., 2023) and in the PhD
thesis of Dr Tessa van de Langerijt of the UCC RubusElite Food Science Team.

Protein boluses of 30g have been suggested to be sufficient to maximally stimulate
muscle protein synthesis in both young and old healthy males (Symons et al., 2009).
It has been suggested that >1000mg of polyphenols is required to elicit recovery
enhancing effects (Bowtell and Kelly, 2019). As a result, during the collaborative
development of PHPM, the UCC food science team were provided a nutritional target
of a dosage of 30g protein and 750mg polyphenols per 500ml, which would be

protein and a milk with negligible protein content.

    3. Subclassification and Characterisation of Study Beverages

        3.1 Polyphenol Subclassification

The polyphenol     subclasses   present   in the FDBP      used to   create the   RubusElite
prototype were identified by Dr Dilip Rai and team at Teagasc Food Research Centre
Ashtown.    The   methodology     used   is presented    below   and   the full   polyphenol
subclassification is presented in Table 3.

HPLC-QTOF mass spectrometry data revealed the presence of fifteen dominant
phenolic compounds in the freeze-dried blackberry purée (Table 3). The polyphenols
were tentatively identified using literature sources, MS/MS fragmentation patterns and
accurate mass measurements. These polyphenols can be classified in to five distinctive
categories, namely anthocyanins, proanthocyanidins, ellagitannins, flavonoids, and
phenolic acids. The identification of the twelve polyphenols in the purée was achieved
using commercial standards. The primary polyphenolic subclasses identified within
the PHPM intervention were the anthocyanin Cyanidin-3-O-glucoside at 218.28 ±
16.02mg/100g DW and the ellagitannins Sanguiin H-6 and Lambertianin C which
were present    in concentrations     of 105.96    ± 16.83mg/100g       DW    and 102.71     ±
18.00mg/100g DW respectively.



3.2 Importance     of Subclassification     and Comparison       to Previous    Performance
Nutrition RCTs

This RCT is the first of its kind to investigate the effects of a polyphenol rich
nutritional intervention on recovery from EIMD that has monitored the stability of the
phenolic content throughout the duration of the RCT and provides the full polyphenol
subclassification   of the    intervention   product.   The   anthocyanin     Cyanidin-3-O-
glucoside, and the ellagitannins Lambertianin C and Sanguiin H-6 were identified as
the predominant polyphenols in the FDBP used in PHPM (See RubusElite RCT
Preface Table 3.). There is an incredibly wide range of compounds which fall under
the classification of polyphenolic compounds, within the flavonoid subclass alone
there are over 6000 compounds identified to date (Panche, Diwan and Chandra, 2016).
Different polyphenol compositions can result in differing bioavailability and differing

                                                                                          195

[PDF page 6]



physiological responses and as a result it is vital to carry out appropriate classification
of subclasses present to identify potential active compounds which provide ergogenic
properties (Pandey and Rizvi, 2009; Bowtell and Kelly, 2019). However, this is rarely
included in published post exercise recovery RCTs using polyphenol interventions as
evidenced by the systematic review and meta-analysis presented in Chapter 4 (Carey,
Lucey and Doyle, 2021).

A considerable proportion of the FDBP used in PHPM this study was the anthocyanin
compound,    cyanidin-3-O-glucoside.    From   the literature, the   most   researched
anthocyanin rich polyphenolic intervention is Montmorency tart cherry juice and its
associated extracts (Beals et al., 2017; Bell et al., 2016; Connolly et al., 2006;
Howatson et al., 2010; Ortega et al., 2020). A comprehensive systematic review and
meta-analysis of 14 muscle recovery RCTs showed a significant benefit of tart cherry
products on recovery following strenuous exercise (Hill et al., 2021). Tart cherry juice
has been shown to contain a high proportion of anthocyanins in its phenolic profile,
however a full characterisation of other subclasses present has yet to be published
(Keane et al., 2016). A RCT displayed an anthocyanin rich blackcurrant nectar was
effective at improving recovery from EIMD in 24 healthy participants (Hutchison et
al., 2016). It appears that anthocyanin rich polyphenol interventions may be effective
at reducing the impact of EIMD however further full classification of polyphenol-
based interventions is required. This aligns well with the results of the present RCT.

The PHPM also contained a significant ellagitannin content as well as anthocyanins.
A RCT    that provided    16 recreationally active   males   with 1300mg/day     of an
ellagitannin rich pomegranate extract containing 95.5% ellagitannins demonstrated
enhanced recovery of muscle strength post exercise (Trombold et al., 2010). The
precise compounds and method of characterisation of this intervention product was
not reported however so it is unclear whether this contained the same ellagitannin
subclasses as were abundant in the current intervention. The high anthocyanin and
ellagitannin content of the FDBP used in the PHPM in the current RCT suggests a
favourable polyphenolic composition to support post exercise recovery from EIMD.

Other polyphenolic    subclasses have   shown   efficacy for post   exercise recovery.
Morgan et al., (2018) investigated the role of polyphenol rich cacao mucilage on post
exercise recovery in 10 recreationally active males and reported characterisation
including predominantly     catechin   and flavanol    polyphenols   present   in their

intervention however their total phenolic dosage was considerably lower than the
current study (154mg/day) (Morgan et al., 2018). Another RCT reported that daily
consumption of three 500mg capsules of a polyphenol rich supplement containing
predominantly ellagic acid and the xanthone α-mangostin lowered muscle pain post
exercise in 13 recreationally active athletes (Romain et al., 2017).



   4. RubusElite Beverage Polyphenol Quantification

The total phenolic content of the freeze-dried blackberry puree (FDBP) used in the
RubusElite beverage   was assessed by   the RubusElite   Food Science   team and
monitored monthly using the Folin Ciolcateu method during the RCT to assess for
polyphenol degradation due to long term storage of the FDBP (Singleton, Orthofer
and Lamuela-Raventós, 1999). The stability of this FDBP is presented in Figure 1.

There was no significant change in total measured polyphenol content of the FDBP
across timepoints as assessed by repeated measures ANOVA (P = 0.298). Average of
all TPC replicates across all timepoints   during the study   showed an   average
polyphenol content of 28.3 ± 2.9 mg/g of freeze-dried powder which results in an
effective polyphenol intervention of 1413 ± 147 mg per day (50g of FDBP).

The evidence underpinning dosage recommendations to elicit recovery benefits is
largely equivocal and based on narrative assessment of the literature (Bowtell and
Kelly, 2019; Carey, Lucey and Doyle, 2021). Even when polyphenol content has been
quantified in previously published polyphenol interventions it is not monitored for
stability despite the clear need to monitor dosage stability during RCTs due to the
potential for polyphenols to degrade during storage or for there to be batch differences
in phenolic content (Srivastava et al., 2007; Howard et al., 2012; Oracz, Zyzelewicz
and Nebesny, 2015). In the current RCT there is a slight downward trend across time
of total phenolic concentration (TPC) of FDBP, there was no significant difference of
polyphenol content month to month across the duration of the study and all study
participants randomised to the intervention group were provided a theoretical effective
dosage of active compounds (RubusElite Preface Figure 1.). TPC analysis of the
FDBP shows an average dosage provided across the study of a total phenolic content
of ~1400mg per day for the PHPM group.

   5. Beverage composition conclusion
The extensive characterisation of the polyphenol content in PHPM during this study
is a much-needed addition to the evidence surrounding polyphenols and post exercise
recovery. In particular, the combination of complete subclassification, quantification
of polyphenol content and the monitoring of the stability of this polyphenol content
throughout the RCT sets this RCT apart from much of the published RCTs to date.
This will help considerably with informing future recommendations for polyphenol
intake to support post exercise recovery.

   6. Further RCT Outcomes

In addition to the outcomes reported in chapters 5 and 6; due to the emerging role of
polyphenols on the gut microbiome and the clear links between the microbiome and
central stress processing   and   exercise related   outcomes,   further samples    and
measurements were collected as part of this RCT to investigate the impact of the
RubusElite beverage    prototype on   central stress processing and   gut microbiome
composition.  This also impacted on the inclusion criteria of the RCT with it being
required that participants not be consuming prebiotics or probiotics, have a digestive
disorder or to have taken antibiotics in the previous two months. The analysis of these
outcomes is being led by APC Microbiome Ireland and as a result are not being
presented as core outcomes of this PhD thesis (see Figure 2).

During this RCT stool samples were collected prior to the intervention period and at
the test visit following 7 days of beverage consumption to assess for changes in gut
microbial composition. Saliva samples were collected at waking on the morning of the
baseline visit and at the test visit to assess for changes in the cortisol awakening
response. Saliva samples were also collected immediately following exercise to assess
for differences in post exercise cortisol response. Blood samples were collected at
baseline, test visit, 24 hours   and 48   hours post   exercise to assess   changes in
inflammatory response. Various validated questionnaires and visual analogue scales
were administered to participants to assess for changes in levels of anxiety and
psychological stress prior to and at the end of the intervention period. All these
outcomes are   currently being analysed at APC Microbiome         Ireland with results
pending.

1.    Introduction

1.1 What is Exercise Induced Muscle Damage?

Exercise induced muscle damage (EIMD) is the temporary decline in muscle function
that occurs following intensive exercise, particularly that which includes considerable
eccentric loading of the muscle (Clarkson and Tremblay, 1988; Owens et al., 2018).
This reduction in muscle function results in meaningful declines in key performance
outcomes such as reduced muscle strength and power which typically lasts between
five- and seven-days post exercise depending on the magnitude of the insult (Owens
et al., 2018; Sayers & Clarkson, 2001; Warren et al., 1999). EIMD is also associated
with considerable increases in post exercise muscle soreness which typically peaks
between 24- and 48-hours post exercise, often referred to as delayed onset muscle
soreness (DOMS) (Cheung et al., 2003; Nosaka et al., 2002).

These deleterious post exercise outcomes occur as a result of microtrauma to the
structure of the muscle fibre, following mechanical force placed on the muscle fibre
(Clarkson and   Sayers, 1999;   Markus    et al., 2021). This   damage   results in an
inflammatory cascade which can also result in secondary damage due to excessive
production of reactive   oxygen species (Owens et al., 2018). This damage, both
mechanical   and biochemical,   results in considerable    harm to   the efficiency of
excitation contraction coupling of the muscle meaning considerable reduction to its
performance capabilities (Owens et al., 2018).

Nutritional solutions are well established to be one of the prime candidates to reduce
the negative impact of EIMD (Urso and Clarkson, 2003; Howatson and Van Someren,
2012; Harty et al., 2019; Markus et al., 2021). Many of these nutritional interventions
have been proposed due to their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory action which has
been hypothesised to reduce the negative biochemical impact of excess reactive
oxygen species production on the muscle post exercise (Harty et al., 2019).. Other
interventions supporting   post exercise   recovery   apply   a different mechanism,
focussing on the provision of amino acid substrate to support muscle repair and
regeneration following EIMD such as protein and BCAA supplementation (Howatson
et al., 2012; Harty et al., 2019; Pearson, Hind and Macnaughton, 2022).

1.2 Role of Polyphenols in Recovery from EIMD

Polyphenols are non-nutritive bioactive phytochemicals found in fruits, teas and other
plant-based food sources (Manach et al., 2004). They are well known for their potent
antioxidants effects and as a result have been linked with various health related
outcomes     such   as improved      lipid   profiles, reduced     blood    pressure    and   insulin
resistance among others (Scalbert, Johnson and Saltmarsh, 2005; Kanti and Syed,
2009; Sureda et al., 2014; Cory et al., 2018; Heneghan et al., 2018). They have also
been suggested to improve recovery from EIMD (Myburgh, Kruger and Smith, 2012;
Myburgh, 2014; Bowtell and Kelly, 2019). The antioxidant action of polyphenols has
been linked with upregulation of cell signalling stress response pathways which
control   the endogenous       antioxidant    response    such    as the   NRF2     – Electrophile
Response Element pathway (Kanner, 2020; Zhou et al., 2019). The digestion and
absorption of polyphenols compared to other antioxidants is notably poorer, and as a
result the interaction with cell signalling pathways such as NRF2 have been attributed
to interaction with the gut microbiome (Bowtell and Kelly, 2019; Sorrenti et al., 2020).

There   have    been    numerous      randomised      controlled    trials (RCTs)      which    have
investigated the role of polyphenol rich interventions on recovery from EIMD such as
the use of tart cherry juice, tea extracts and berry fruits and extracts (Beals et al., 2017;
Connolly et al., 2006; Howatson et al., 2010; Herrlinger et al., 2015; McAnulty et al.,
2004; McLeay et al., 2012; Romain et al., 2017; Yarahmadi et al., 2014). As part of
this PhD thesis a meta-analysis of polyphenol interventions on recovery from EIMD
is presented earlier in this thesis (Carey, Lucey and Doyle 2021) (chapter 4). Further
systematic    reviews    and   meta-analyses      also reinforce     the role    for fruit   derived
polyphenol rich interventions to support post exercise recovery from EIMD (Doma,
Gahreman and Connor, 2020; Ortega et al., 2020; Rickards et al., 2021). Another
meta-analysis has shown that anthocyanin rich whole fruit including pomegranates,
tart cherries   and   wild   blueberries     among    others   can   improve     exercise    induced
oxidative stress and inflammation (Bloedon et al., 2019). While there appears to be
scientific evidence supporting the use of polyphenols for recovery from EIMD, the
most appropriate polyphenol source and ingestion regimen is required to achieve such
benefits to recovery remains unclear. One narrative review of RCTs suggested that a
dosage of at least 1000mg/day, at least three days prior to and three days following
exercise   is required    to   elicit recovery    advantages,     however     empirical    evidence

[PDF page 17]



underpinning this claim is limited (Bowtell and Kelly, 2019). While polyphenol
sources such as tart cherry juices and tea extracts are some of the most extensively
researched, it is unclear whether these sources contain preferential subclasses of
polyphenols required to support post exercise recovery (Bowtell and Kelly, 2019;
Carey,   Lucey    and   Doyle,   2021).    The   precise   characterisation     of many     of these
interventions is distinctly lacking as is evidenced in the previous chapter in this thesis
(chapter 4) (Carey, Lucey and Doyle, 2021). It is clear that the explicit defining of
polyphenol     subclasses     and   dosages     is a   trial feature     which    requires    further
investigation and greater reporting to allow for a greater understanding of the precise
role of the specific polyphenol subclasses in post exercise recovery (Bowtell and
Kelly, 2019).

The   source    of polyphenols       in this   RCT     was   selected    as blackberries     (Rubus
Fruticosus). Blackberries contain a unique polyphenolic composition predominantly
containing anthocyanins cyanidin-3-O-glucoside and cyanidin-3-O-rutinoside, as well
as considerable ellagitannin and catechin polyphenols (Tzima et al., 2023).
Anthocyanins are a class that have been noted particularly for their ability to augment
post exercise recovery with the anthocyanins from Montmorency tart cherries shown
to consistently improve post exercise recovery from following strenuous exercise (Hill
et al., 2021). Whole fruits rich in anthocyanins were recently shown to also reduce
exercise induced oxidative stress and inflammation in a large meta-analysis of RCTs
(Bloedon et al., 2019).
Pomegranate ellagitannin polyphenols have also been investigated for their ability to
enhance post exercise recovery in RCTs, showing positive effects (Trombold et al.,
2010, 2011). Tea catechins from green tea have also been shown to improve post
exercise recovery of muscle soreness as well as oxidative stress and inflammation
(Herrlinger et al., 2015, Jówko et al., 2015, Sugita et al., 2016). Despite this favourable
polyphenolic composition, blackberry polyphenols have yet to be investigated within
the context of post exercise recovery.


    1.3 Dairy Protein and Exercise

Dietary protein is established as playing a considerable role in the muscular response
to exercise (Phillips, 2007; Burd et al., 2009; Morton, McGlory and Phillips, 2015).
The two primary methods by which muscle protein breakdown and synthesis can be

                                                                                                  207

[PDF page 18]



shifted to a balance favouring synthesis are feeding of dietary protein and exercise
(Burd et al., 2009). Essential amino acids provide essential substrate for the formation
and repair of muscle tissue post exercise (Church et al., 2020). The amino acid leucine
has been shown to not only act as a substrate to support post exercise muscular
remodelling but also to act as a key signalling molecule in the process of muscle
protein synthesis through its interaction with the mTor pathway (Holowaty et al.,
2023). Dairy protein has been shown to be particularly rich in essential amino acids
and leucine in particular compared to other protein sources (Vliet, Burd and van Loon,
2015). Along with this, the digestibility of dairy proteins has been shown to be superior
to many other protein sources, particularly non-animal-based sources (Vliet, Burd and
van Loon, 2015; Dupont and Tomé, 2020). As such dairy protein represents one of the
most effective sources of protein to support muscle remodelling and repair post
exercise. While investigations into the role of protein supplementation on the rate of
recovery from EIMD have been mixed (Pasiakos, Lieberman and McLellan, 2014;
Pearson, Hind and Macnaughton, 2022), the carbohydrate-to-protein blend present in
bovine milk has been suggested to be an effective tool to support post exercise
recovery with a number of randomised controlled trials reporting benefits for post
exercise recovery from EIMD (Cockburn et al., 2008; Cockburn, Bell and Stevenson,
2013; Rankin, Stevenson and Cockburn, 2015).



   1.4 Combining Protein with Polyphenols

In theory there is considerable merit for the combination of polyphenols and milk to
support post exercise   recovery given    the previously discussed    efficacy of both
compounds to improve recovery from EIMD and the added benefit of dairy protein on
muscle protein synthesis to support the post exercise repair and remodelling of muscle
tissue. It is also plausible that protein rich food matrices such as milk may act as an
effective delivery vehicle for polyphenols to the gut due to the potential protective
effect of dairy protein on polyphenols through encapsulation of polyphenols within
the protein structure (van de Langerijt et al., 2023). Despite this, the combination of
protein and polyphenols in recovery from EIMD has only been studied in two RCTs
to date (Nieman et al., 2013; Pavis et al., 2021). One of these RCTs investigated the
impact of a   blueberry and   tea-based polyphenol    treatment with   soy protein   on
inflammation and oxidative stress surrounding a three-day intensive running period in

                                                                                   208

[PDF page 19]



31 long distance runners (Nieman et al., 2013). Twenty grams of the polyphenol
enriched soy protein powder was consumed twice per day for a total of 17 days. The
total polyphenol concentration was 2136mg per day. This RCT observed no impact of
the treatment on markers of inflammation and oxidative stress during or following this
period. The other RCT investigated the impact of a pomegranate polyphenol enriched
protein beverage containing 211mg of polyphenols and 20g protein once per day for
14 days surrounding a bout of 300 eccentric leg extensions (Pavis et al., 2021). This
RCT observed significant improvements in post exercise recovery of muscle strength,
soreness and   creatine kinase   over the   168 hours   post exercise   in 18   healthy
recreationally active males (Pavis et al., 2021).

While only two studies have investigated polyphenol protein combinations on exercise
induced muscle    damage,   other RCTs    have   investigated different post   exercise
outcomes. One such RCT investigated the role of the 2136mg polyphenol and 20g soy
protein powder twice per day for 17 days on outcomes for immune function post
exercise, showing delayed virus replication but no effects on bacterial infections in 31
long distance runners (Ahmed et al., 2014). Another RCT investigated the impact of
a pre-exercise bolus of a 20g blend of whey, casein and pea protein combined with
650mg pomegranate polyphenols and pre-sleep bolus 18g protein blend alongside
480mg tart cherry juice to enhance post exercise myofibrillar protein synthesis rates
and functional adaptation    to resistance training over   an 11-week    period in   15
recreationally active healthy    adults (Pavis   et al., 2022).   This study   showed
improvements to early functional adaptation to the first 10 resistance training sessions
and increases in muscle protein synthesis associated with ingestion of their polyphenol
protein beverage (Pavis et al., 2022).

No study to date has investigated a milk polyphenol blend on post exercise recovery
from EIMD. However, one study has investigated the role of bovine milk enriched
with 490mg     of flavonoid   polyphenols    on exercise   induced   intestinal injury,
permeability and inflammation in 12 recreational cyclists (Kung et al., 2022). This
study showed no impact of the beverage which contained a polyphenol blend derived
from blueberry,   cocoa and    green tea on   post exercise   intestinal and digestive
outcomes.

There has also been research suggesting that using a protein rich food source for
polyphenol delivery, may enhance the bioavailability of polyphenols with one study

                                                                                   209

[PDF page 20]


showing in-vitro protection of the polyphenol epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) when
delivered in a complex with soy protein (Zhou et al., 2020). A recent review of studies
investigating polyphenol protein interactions has suggested that protein polyphenol
complexes and compounds provide potential for stronger antioxidant action however
this depends on the protein polyphenol ratio and type of polyphenol (Sun, Sarteshnizi
and Udenigwe, 2022).
This current RCT aims to investigate the role of a blackberry polyphenol enriched high
protein milk on post exercise recovery as assessed by muscle strength and muscle
soreness when compared to an equivalent commercial high protein milk and a low
protein milk control. Secondary outcomes include the impact of this polyphenol
enriched high protein                 milk on exercise induced oxidative stress and antioxidant
capacity.

    2.   Methods

    2.1  Study Overview & Recruitment

The   RubusElite     RCT    was   designed     as a parallel    proof   of concept     single   blind
randomised controlled trial. This RCT encompassed a collaborative approach which
incorporated multiple departments across multiple institutions. Supportive evidence
which provides valuable context for this RCT but is not presented as work specific to
this PhD thesis is presented in RubusElite RCT Preface.

Moderately active male participants between the ages of 18 and 45 were recruited by
a convenience      sampling    approach.     This included     advertisement      on social    media
platforms, word-of-mouth, and electronically distributed flyers. Sample size was
determined in line with previously published parallel designed polyphenol RCTs
(Beals et al., 2017; Connolly et al., 2006; Howatson et al., 2010; Herrlinger et al.,
2015; McAnulty et al., 2004; McLeay et al., 2012; Romain et al., 2017; Yarahmadi et
al., 2014). A study protocol overview figure is presented in Figure 1. The study took
place   at the   human     performance      laboratories    of the   South    East   Technological
University, Waterford, Ireland, between June 2022 and May 2023. This RCT was
granted ethical approval by the research ethics board of South East Technological
University, Waterford (ref: WIT2021REC044, January 2022) (Appendix 13). This
RCT protocol was registered with www.clinicaltrials.gov, ID number NCT05463835.



    2.2 Participant Screening

Prospective participants were sent a pre-screening questionnaire to determine their
initial eligibility   following     first contact    (Appendix      14).   In   this questionnaire
participants were asked various questions regarding their eligibility to take part in the
study. Eligible participants were required to be biologically male, between 18 and 45
years of age, free from chronic illness or current injury, free from landing related lower
limb injury history, not currently undertaking a plyometric training programme, not
have a history of international sporting competition and not having taken either
probiotic supplements or antibiotics in the past two months. They were also asked
about   their ability   and   willingness    to comply      with   various   key   study   protocols
including avoidance of selected polyphenol rich foods for the duration of the study
(Appendix 19), avoidance of non-medically prescribed nutritional supplements and

                                                                                                  211

[PDF page 22]



nutraceuticals for the duration of the study, avoidance of strenuous exercise in the 48
hours prior to and following the study test day. If a participant met each of the above
criteria, they were invited to attend an onsite study screening visit.
During the follow-on onsite screening visit, participants received a verbal overview of
the study along with a participant information leaflet (PIL) (Appendix 15). Participants
were given the opportunity to ask questions, they were then asked to fill out a brief
health questionnaire to ensure they could exercise at maximal intensity. Participants
were then asked to provide their written consent prior to any study protocols being
undertaken (Appendix 16). Participants then undertook a graded exercise test-to-
exhaustion where peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak) was assessed. The test was carried
out on a Lode Corvial CPET cycle ergometer (lode B.V. Groningen, Netherlands)
which was pre-programmed to start at 80W and increase by 20 watts every minute
until volitional exhaustion. At each stage, heart rate, and Rate of Perceived Exertion
(Borg, 1970) were recorded. Participants were instructed to cycle until volitional
exhaustion while keeping their cadence above 65rpm throughout the test. Strong
verbal encouragement was provided throughout the test. Oxygen consumption was
measured using a MOXUS Modular Metabolic System (AEI Technologies, Texas,
USA). Eligible participants were required to be moderately active with a VO2peak
between 35.0 and 55.0 ml/kg/min which corresponded to having a baseline level of
aerobic fitness sufficient to complete the study protocol but not having underwent high
level endurance exercise training. This was determined based on normative data from
males aged between 18 and 45 (Myers et al., 2017). Once the participant achieved the
required score in the VO2peak graded exercise test they were deemed eligible and
enrolled on the study. Participants were randomly assigned in a parallel fashion to one
of three   groups   using a   block   randomisation    method    via the   online tool
www.sealedenvelope.com (Sealed Envelope, London, United Kingdom).

      2.4 Baseline Study Visit
During the baseline study visit, participants attended the exercise lab following a 12-
hour fast to provide a baseline venous blood sample taken by a trained phlebotomist.
Following this, participants were provided with a breakfast voucher for the university
canteen and instructed to purchase breakfast ad libitum however they would be
required to repeat this breakfast for each of the following study visits (test visit, 24h
post exercise and 48h post exercise). Participants were then provided with 6 days of
their assigned beverage, instructions outlining the beverage consumption protocol and
a custom designed compliance form on which participants were requested to mark off
the time of each beverage consumption or whether a dose was missed (Appendix 17).
All participants were required to have an adherence of at least 80% to be included in
study analysis.

                                                                                                                                                  214

[PDF page 25]



    2.5 Test Study Visit

On day 7 of beverage consumption, participants returned to the laboratory in a fasted
state and provided a venous blood sample. Following this they consumed an identical
breakfast to the baseline day. Upon arrival back to the lab following breakfast they
underwent a baseline 24-hour interview administered dietary recall with a trained
nutritionist according to a multi pass technique (DAPA Measurement Toolkit). This
resulting dietary data was analysed using Nutritics Research Edition v5.87 software
(Nutritics, Dublin, Ireland) to generate nutrient intake data.

Participants then underwent a baseline muscle strength assessment using an isometric
mid-thigh pull (IMTP) protocol. Following the IMTP participants underwent a muscle
damage induction protocol involving 100 drop jumps from a height of 0.6m. Full
muscle damage and muscle strength protocols are described below. Immediately post
muscle damage a repeat IMTP protocol was completed, and participants ranked their
level of lower limb muscle soreness on a 0-10 visual analogue scale while undertaking
a bodyweight squat movement to approximately 90° knee angle. Participants were
then provided with their study beverages for that study day and were asked to consume
their first beverage within 1 hour of the completion of the study visit.



    2.6 Muscle Damage Protocol and Strength Measurement

The exercise-induced muscle damage induction protocol consisted of five sets of
twenty drop jumps from a height of 0.6m. Participants were provided with a 2-minute
rest period between each set and participants were given 10 seconds to perform each
repetition. Wooden boxes with rubber gripping on top, were constructed into a 0.6m
platform with a 0.22m intermediate step to the rear. The participant ascended to the
platform using the intermediate step and stepped off from the box landing on the floor
with both feet. Upon landing the participant was instructed to immediately and fluidly
descend to an approximate knee angle of 90° followed by an immediate maximal
vertical jump. Two trained assessors provided verbal feedback during the entire
protocol based on visual observations from two differing angles to ensure both safe
and consistent     completion     of the   study   protocol.   All   participants   completed     the
protocol with the same flooring conditions. This protocol was first described in
(Miyama and Nosaka, 2004) and has since been used in multiple intervention RCTs

                                                                                                  215

[PDF page 26]



investigating exercise induced muscle damage which have shown it to be a reliable,
repeatable and sufficient methodology for the generation of sufficient muscle damage
(Miyama and Nosaka, 2004; Goodall and Howatson, 2008; Howatson, Goodall and
Someren,   2009;   Howatson     et al., 2012;   Clifford et   al., 2016). This    protocol is
demonstrated in Figure 3.





Figure 3. Muscle damage induction protocol, drop jumps from 0.6m, 5x20 repetitions.

Muscle strength was measured using an IMTP protocol on BTS Bioengineering Force
Podiums (BTS Bioengineering, Garbagnate Milanese MI, Italy), with data capture
completed by 3Digivec software (BTS Bioengineering, Garbagnate Milanese MI,
Italy). Isometric mid-thigh pulls followed the protocol proposed by Comfort et al.,
(2019). Participants were initially assessed to determine the appropriate bar height
demonstrating the IMTP exercise form and then measuring hip and knee angles using
a digital goniometer. Hip angles were required to be between 140° and 150° while the
knee angle was required to be between 125° and 145°. Bar height and participant
position was manipulated until a comfortable position meeting the required angles was
obtained. Participants    then underwent     a standardised    warm-up     procedure   which
involved 10 bodyweight squats and 20m of walking bodyweight lunges. The second
phase of the warm-up involved 3 sets of 3 repetitions of 3 second duration IMTPs at
increasing intensity which simultaneously acted as a familiarisation protocol to make
the participants comfortable with the procedure and equipment. The first set was
completed at 50% of perceived maximal effort, the following at 75% and 90%
respectively. Participants were provided 1 minute of passive recovery between each
warm-up set. Following completion of the warm-up, participants were given 2.5
minutes passive recovery prior to the first maximal IMTP. For all warm-up and
maximal IMTPs participants were strapped securely to a fixed barbell using Figure-8
                                                                                          216

[PDF page 27]



weightlifting straps and were provided with powdered chalk to enhance grip. A
standardised coaching cue was used for all participants to “drive your feet into the
ground as hard and fast as possible”. Participants were instructed to avoid pretension
on the bar during the pre-pull phase and to avoid a countermovement during the start
of the pull. Participants provided 3 seconds of ‘quiet standing’ prior to the initiation
of the maximal IMTP to ensure a clear baseline prior to commencing force production.
Participants were counted down from 3 and given strong verbal encouragement
throughout a 5-second-long pull. Participants were given 2.5 minutes rest following
each IMTP. Participants completed at least 2 IMTPs, if a participant improved by
greater than 250N    between   their first and second   IMTP   a third repetition was
completed. The IMTP measurements were taken at baseline, immediately post-muscle
damage, 24h post-muscle damage, and 48h post-muscle damage (Figure 1).

   2.7 Soreness and Biomarker Assessment
Subjective measures of muscle soreness have been proposed as appropriate supportive
evidence to that of test-retest performance parameters in accordance with EFSA
guidance for substantiation of food claims related to muscle recovery (Turck et al.,
2018). Visual analogue scale measurements of muscle pain have been suggested to be
a better representative of muscle pain during delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS),
than pressure pain tests (Wing Yin Lau, Muthalib and Nosaka, 2013). Muscle soreness
was assessed using a visual analogue scale, immediately, 24 hours, 48 hours and 72
hours post exercise (Appendix 18.). On each occasion the participant was asked to rate
their muscle soreness following a bodyweight squat to an approximate knee angle of
90° on a scale from zero to ten, where zero was labelled as ‘no pain’ and ten was
labelled as ‘extreme pain’.
Secondary outcomes included blood biomarkers of muscle damage and oxidative
stress, such   as protein   carbonyls,   8-iso PGF2α     (8-isoprostane), FRAP      and
malondialdehyde (MDA). Fasting intravenous blood samples were collected by a
trained phlebotomist at baseline, test exercise day, 24 hours and 48 hours post exercise.
Plasma protein carbonyls was analysed by using Protein Carbonyl Colorimetric Assay
Kit (Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). Serum MDA was measured
using a CheKine Lipid Peroxidation Assay Kit (Abbkine, Wuhan, China). Plasma 8-
iso PGF2α    was   measured    using RayBio     Isoprostane (8-iso   PGF2α)    Enzyme
Immunoassay    Kit (RayBiotech,    Georgia, USA).    All samples    were measured    in
                                                                                   217

[PDF page 28]



duplicate within the same batch. Inter-assay variability and intra-assay variability were
determined to ensure consistency and reproducibility of biomarker measurements was
<15%.

The ferric reducing power of serum samples was quantified using the FRAP assay
adapted for a 96 well plate. For the standard curve, a stock solution of 3.4mM
FeSO4.7H2O was prepared and serial diluted in a final volume of 40µL. Samples
(20µL) were diluted with 20µL distilled water in a 96 well plate. FRAP standard
reagent, 300µL (20mL of 300mM acetate buffer, 2mL 10mM TPTZ and 2mL H2O)
was added to each of the standards and, for samples, 300µL (20mL 300mM acetate
buffer, 2mL 10mM TPTZ and 2mL 20mM FeCl3.6H2O) was added. Samples were
left in the dark for 20 minutes, and absorbance was measured at 595nm using a plate
reader (Varioskan Flash, ThermoScientific).



   2.8 Data Analysis
All statistical procedures were carried out in SPSS v.28.0.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp).
Descriptive analysis was conducted for all outcomes at each timepoint. All variables
underwent normality testing using the Shapiro-Wilk test. One-way ANOVAs were
used to assess baseline comparability of demographic factors and dietary data across
treatment groups.   As   part of   one-way   ANOVA       protocols, Levenes    test for
homogeneity of variances was conducted. Linear mixed models repeated measures
analysis of variance (MMRM) were applied to examine the effects of treatment, time
and treatment*time in outcomes of recovery from exercise induced muscle damage,
including recovery of muscle strength, soreness and biomarkers of oxidative stress. In
the application of this model several assumptions were assessed, and adjustments
made when violations were observed. Within-subject observations were assessed for
correlation using intraclass correlation coefficients. Residuals    were assessed   for
normal distribution using a Shapiro-Wilk test and log transformation conducted where
this was violated. Mauchly’s sphericity test was conducted, and a Greenhouse-Geisser
correction was   applied where   appropriate. Additionally,   various covariates were
integrated into the model, with Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) scores being used
to evaluate the model fit as each covariate was introduced. Data adjusted for covariates
is presented as mean ± SEM, while unadjusted data is presented as mean ± SD. All
data that was adjusted is presented in unadjusted form in Appendix 20. Bonferroni

215.0N or 21.9kg in muscle strength (p<0.001). This decline in muscle strength
persisted to 24 hours (mean difference= -273.3N or -27.9kg, p<0.001) and 48 hours
post-exercise (mean difference= -295.7N or -30.2kg, p<0.001). This indicates that in
the overall group, the exercise was sufficient to significantly generate EIMD.

The type of beverage consumed had a significant impact on muscle strength recovery
over the 48-hour post-exercise period (P = 0.006). Participants who consumed PHPM

had significantly greater relative muscle strength over the 48-hour period post exercise
compared to those who consumed LPM with a mean difference of 222.4N or 22.7kg
(P=0.005). However, there were no significant differences between PHPM and HPM

significant changes to post exercise strength suggesting that participants had not fully
recovered 48 hours following exercise (P = 0.406), nor did we find any interaction
between time and the type of beverage consumed suggesting that while the magnitude
of muscle strength decrement was impacted by the study beverage, the rate of recovery
was not (P = 0.478).

PHPM showed an advantage in retaining muscle strength over LPM in aiding within
the 48-hour post exercise period. This effect was particularly apparent at 24 hours
post-exercise. HPM may improve recovery of strength at 24 hours post exercise
compared with LPM.

   4   Discussion

This single blind, parallel proof of concept RCT is the first to demonstrate that 10 days
of ingestion of a blackberry polyphenol enriched high protein milk has the propensity
to improve post exercise recovery following an intensive exercise bout. Consuming
PHPM twice per day at a daily dosage of ~1400mg polyphenols and ~60g milk protein
resulted in significantly improved recovery of skeletal muscle strength compared to a
control low protein milk product. In addition to this, PHPM significantly attenuated
the muscle soreness response post exercise compared to both a commercial HPM and
LPM control. HPM showed isolated effect on recovery of muscle strength compared
to LPM during peak EIMD.



       4.1. Induction of Muscle damage
This RCT used the method to 5x20 drop jumps from a height of 60cm to induce EIMD
as first reported by Miyama and Nosaka (2004). This brought about significant muscle
damage as is shown by the increases in muscle soreness in the 48 hours post exercise
and the reductions of muscle strength post exercise in all three treatment groups. This
muscle damage was shown to be significant in pooled data with muscle strength
significantly reduced compared with baseline at all timepoints post exercise. In the
LPM control group muscle strength decrements on average peaked at the 24-hour post
exercise timepoint with this group on average 16.8% weaker than their pre muscle
damage   IMTP   muscle   strength measurement.    This overall decrement    is broadly
comparable to that of the placebo/control groups of other RCTs using this method to
induce muscle damage (between 15% and 23% reductions) however many of these
observed the greatest post exercise strength decrements immediately post exercise
rather than 24 hours post exercise which was shown in this study (Goodall and
Howatson, 2008; Howatson, Goodall and Someren, 2009; Clifford et al., 2016). These
studies did use other methods of isometric muscle strength measures and not IMTP,
the variation in lower limb muscle recruitment may account partly for this.






                                                                                   227

[PDF page 38]



         4.2 Recovery of Muscle Strength

                  4.2.1 RCT Design and muscle strength measurement

Outcomes in this study were derived with reference to the published guidance on the
scientific requirements for European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) health claims
related to muscle function and performance (Turck et al., 2018). They recommend
test-retest assessment on performance parameters such as muscle strength after an
initial strenuous bout as the most appropriate method to assess muscle recovery claims
(Turck et al., 2018). Muscle strength has been suggested to be the most appropriate
method of post exercise assessment of EIMD following eccentrically loaded exercise
(Warren et al., 1999). IMTP measurements of muscle strength has been shown to be
valid, reliable and sensitive method of assessing muscle strength when appropriate
protocols are undertaken (Comfort et al., 2019).

                  4.2.2 Effect of treatments on recovery of muscle strength

In the current RCT, PHPM significantly improved overall recovery of post exercise
muscle strength when compared with LPM control however there was no significant
difference in recovery of muscle strength observed between PHPM and HPM groups.
Overall, there was no significant difference between HPM and LPM however when
taken in isolation HPM improved muscle strength at 24h post exercise suggesting
possible benefit of HPM during the peak of EIMD.

                  4.2.3 Previous polyphenol RCTs on recovery of muscle strength

Polyphenol rich interventions of various dosages from numerous sources have been
shown to improve post exercise recovery of muscle strength however this is the first
study to demonstrate this ergogenic effect is at least maintained when delivered as part
of a high    protein   dairy   matrix    (Myburgh,     Kruger    and   Smith,   2012;    Herrlinger,
Chirouzes and Ceddia, 2015; Romain et al., 2017). The current RCT is the first to
show that when provided in a dairy matrix, the muscle strength recovery benefits are
at minimum maintained. The only RCT to date to provide a polyphenol intervention
in a dairy matrix surrounding exercise provided 12 cyclists with a dosage of 490mg of
polyphenols from mixed flavonoid rich sources with 240ml of shelf stable milk for 15
days in the lead up to 60 minutes of intensive laboratory cycling (Kung et al., 2022).
This study    however     showed     no   significant   effect of   treatment    on   post exercise
intestinal permeability or intestinal injury (Kung et al., 2022).

                                                                                                  228

[PDF page 39]



               4.2.4 Previous dairy protein RCTs on recovery of muscle strength

HPM alone showed no significant improvement in recovery of muscle strength when
compared with LPM apart from at 24h post exercise in isolation. Milk has been
suggested to be a strong candidate as a post exercise recovery drink due to its
approximate   isotonic properties   and water    content for   rehydration, source   of
carbohydrate for replenishment of glycogen stores and a good protein source for post
exercise muscle protein synthesis and recovery from EIMD (James et al., 2018). One
RCT showed that consumption of 500ml of milk may limit the decrements of muscle
function after damaging exercise however their sample size was small (n=14) and as
such only used inferential statistics (Cockburn, Bell and Stevenson, 2013). This study
also did not report the precise protein content of the milk used in the intervention.
Another RCT investigating milk on post exercise recovery showed that ingestion of
500ml of milk containing 17g of protein consumed post eccentric exercise attenuated
losses of muscle strength compared with an energy matched carbohydrate solution in
32 team sport players (Rankin, Stevenson and Cockburn, 2015). While the effects of
milk on post exercise recovery have shown promising results in previous RCTs no
such study has investigated high protein milk in the range of 30g of protein on recovery
of muscle function post exercise.

               4.2.5 Recovery of muscle strength conclusion

In summary the current study showed that PHPM enhanced post exercise recovery of
muscle strength greater than that of a low protein milk control. However, there was no
significant difference in recovery of muscle strength between polyphenol rich high
protein milk and high protein milk alone. High protein milk may provide isolated
benefits to recovery of muscle strength 24h post exercise.



       4.3 Recovery of Muscle Pain

               4.3.1 RCT Design and Muscle Soreness Measurement

EFSAs guidance for health claims substantiation to support muscle recovery states
that subjective measures of perceived muscle soreness are appropriate to be used as
supportive evidence to that of muscle performance parameters such as muscle strength
(Turck et al., 2018). Visual analogue scales such as the one used in this study have
been shown to be valid and reliable measures of musculoskeletal pain (Boonstra et al.,
                                                                                   229

[PDF page 40]



2008). Visual analogue scales have also been suggested to be more appropriate
measures of the time course of changes in delayed onset muscle soreness post exercise
compared with pressure pain threshold tests (Lau, Muthalib and Nosaka, 2013).
               4.3.2 Effect of treatments on recovery of muscle soreness

In this study PHPM showed a significant effect on recovery of muscle soreness post
exercise with significantly reduced perceived muscle soreness compared with both
LPM and HPM. No significant difference was observed between HPM and LPM. This
suggests that the protein carbohydrate blend of HPM alone had no impact on post
exercise recovery   of muscle   pain however   enriching this   HPM   with blackberry
polyphenols significantly improved post exercise pain response.

               4.3.3 Comparison with previous studies on muscle soreness

Polyphenol rich interventions have been shown to significantly improve post exercise
muscle soreness in multiple meta-analysis (Doma, Gahreman and Connor, 2020;
Carey, Lucey and Doyle, 2021; Rickards et al., 2021). This study is the first study to
demonstrate   that this effect is at minimum     maintained   when   the polyphenolic
intervention is provided in a high protein matrix. Interestingly there was no impact of
high protein milk on muscle soreness post exercise. This contrasts with the findings
of Rankin and colleagues who observed a likely beneficial effect of milk on post
exercise recovery of muscle soreness over a similar 72-hour period post exercise, this
was despite a smaller dosage of milk protein (17g) and similar carbohydrate content
(24g) (Rankin, Stevenson and Cockburn, 2015). However, another study investigating
the role of milk on post exercise saw an unclear effect of milk on post exercise
recovery of muscle pain despite observing likely beneficial effects in recovery of
functional performance parameters (Cockburn, Bell and Stevenson, 2013).

               4.3.4 Recovery of muscle soreness conclusion

Blackberry polyphenols when added to high protein milk confer added benefits to the
magnitude   of post-exercise   muscle   soreness. When     combined    with the   well-
established benefits of dairy protein for enhancement of muscle protein synthesis, and
the effect of PHPM to enhance recovery of muscle strength, PHPM represents a strong
candidate to be included in post exercise recovery regimens.

       4.4 Biomarkers of Oxidative Stress and Antioxidant Status

               4.4.1 RCT Design and Outcome measurement

Due to the role of polyphenols in mediating reactive oxygen species and the propensity
for EIMD to be exacerbated by excessive production of reactive oxygen species, it
was deemed pertinent to monitor the both the antioxidant activity (FRAP) and the
oxidative stress (MDA, Protein carbonyl and 8-iso PGF2α) response surrounding
EIMD during this study. MDA and 8-iso PGF2α represent measures of lipid oxidation
while protein carbonyls determine protein oxidation.

               4.4.2 Treatment effect on lipid and protein oxidation

No significant impact of any treatment was observed for either MDA or 8-iso PGF2α
surrounding the intensive exercise bout. Across all measures of oxidative stress, there
was no significant effect of PHPM compared with LPM control. This is despite
previous research   reporting that anthocyanin    rich whole   fruit were effective at
reducing oxidative stress across multiple biomarkers including protein carbonyls
(Bloedon et al., 2019). However it must be noted that this effect was shown to be small
in a meta-analysis containing 366 participants and as such this comparatively small
RCT may have been underpowered to identify an effect (Bloedon et al., 2019).

Interestingly however, there was a significant reduced protein carbonyl response
associated with HPM compared with that of the PHPM group. While some dairy
products have been shown to exhibit antioxidant properties associated with milk
proteins (Khan et al., 2019), it is unclear why these would elicit an effect on the HPM
group and not the PHPM group, particularly since enhanced recovery of muscle
strength and soreness was observed in PHPM group and not HPM in comparison to
LPM. This also contrasts with another study which has investigated the effect of 500ml
of milk on post exercise protein carbonyl response to repeated cycling sprints but
found no effect (Rankin et al., 2017). Curiously there was also a significant difference
suggesting a greater antioxidant activity of the LPM when compared with the PHPM
group. While polyphenols are well known for their antioxidant ability it has been
previously observed   that polyphenols   may   in certain circumstances    exhibit pro-
oxidant effects, predominantly associated with high dosages or interactions with metal
ions (Yordi et al., 2012). Considering the high dosage of polyphenols used in this
study, it is not beyond the realms of possibility that a pro-oxidant effect was elicited

                                                                                   231

[PDF page 42]



by the PHPM. It has also been shown that total antioxidant capacity assays such as
FRAP only assess the effect of small molecular antioxidants in plasma and do not
account   for intracellular    antioxidant    enzymes     such    as superoxide     dismutase     and
peroxiredoxin, which are regulated by key targets of polyphenols such as the NRF2
electrophile response element (Bowtell and Kelly, 2019). This may explain the lack
of enhancement of antioxidant action exhibited in FRAP assays associated with PHPM
yet positive improvements in muscle strength and muscle soreness recovery although
further research is required to confirm this.


         4.5 Limitations

Due to the exploratory proof of concept nature of this RCT, the sample size used was
relatively small. This may have been underpowered to detect a treatment effect in
biomarkers of oxidative stress which have been noted to be small in a previous meta-
analysis (Bloedon et al., 2019). Despite this, the sample size used in this study is in
line, and in many cases larger than many other RCTs investigating the impact of
polyphenols     and   dairy   protein   on   recovery    from   EIMD      due   to the   costly   and
burdensome nature of exercise related randomised controlled trials.

Due to the food-based design of this study it was not possible to use an identical
placebo control as part of this study design. Despite this, due to the parallel nature of
this RCT participants were not aware of the characteristics of other study beverages,
only that three “dairy-based beverages” were being assessed. No further details of the
nutritional composition of any intervention arm were provided to participants until all
study   procedures     had   been   concluded.     Due    to the   non-identical     nature   of the
intervention beverages and the requirement for researchers to prepare study beverages
during certain participant visits it was impossible to employ a double-blind procedure
during this RCT.
While the protein content (between HPM and PHPM) and the total energy content
were approximately matched across treatments, it is possible that the variation in
carbohydrate     content   (table   1.) may    have   influenced     performance     in the   EIMD
induction protocol and thus influenced the damage induced. However, 24-hour dietary
recall data from the period immediately prior to EIMD induction protocol shows


                                                                                                  232

[PDF page 43]



almost identical CHO intake across groups (table 3), and as such it is unlikely to have
influenced findings.

The parallel design of this study may have introduced additional heterogeneity to the
results of this study due to baseline differences across groups. However, crossover
designed RCTs which normally are undertaken to account for this variation run into
considerable issues due to the repeated bout effect when used in EIMD recovery
RCTs. The    repeated bout   effect is the phenomena    post intensive   unaccustomed
exercise where the body undergoes pronounced adaptation in a super-compensatory
manner to protect itself from a future similar bout of exercise (McHugh, 2003;
Hyldahl, Chen and Nosaka, 2017). This poses considerable issues due to the inability
to standardise muscle damage induction between study arms due to the adaptation of
the body following the first bout of exercise and as such a parallel design is the most
appropriate design for this type of RCT.

Although procedures were used to standardise exercise instruction, demonstration and
equipment used during the muscle damaging protocol, no quantifiable method was
used to ensure standardised muscle damage was induced for every participant. Future
studies may consider camera-based systems to assess squat depth at the bottom of
landing or force plate systems to assess landing forces exerted where resources allow.

All participants in this study were instructed to abstain from strenuous exercise for 48
hours prior to muscle damage induction to ensure muscle soreness and strength
decrements were only a result of study protocols. However, muscle soreness was not
assessed at baseline and as such it is possible that there was residual soreness that may
have occurred prior to the study protocols.

While the original study to propose this muscle damage induction protocol suggested
to complete the exercise barefoot due to the effect of shoes on landing mechanics
(Miyama    and Nosaka,    2004),   pilot testing in the   present facilities observed
considerable foot pain following 100 drop jumps barefoot due to the rigid nature of
the concrete flooring and determined it to be more appropriate to allow footwear to be
used. This may however have reduced total muscle damage experienced and added
variation to the forces experienced across participants due to varying cushioning effect
of shoes worn.

               4.6 Future Directions and Research Priorities

One of the strengths of this RCT is the comprehensive characterisation of study
intervention beverages particularly the polyphenol content of the PHPM intervention.
This is a particular weakness in previous RCT protocols investigating polyphenol
interventions and future research investigating other polyphenol sources should aim
to adopt similar characterisation protocols (Carey, Lucey and Doyle, 2021). This will
allow for greater   understanding   of indicative   polyphenol   dosage and    subclass
recommendations.    This   RCT    has focussed    on the   complementary     effect of
polyphenols and milk protein on post exercise recovery from EIMD, however future
research is required to evaluate the effect of long-term consumption of polyphenol
interventions on exercise induced adaptation. Other antioxidant supplementation such
as vitamin C and E during longer term exercise training has shown propensity to blunt
training adaptation in   certain population   groups (Gomez-Cabrera      et al., 2015;
Bjørnsen et   al., 2016). While   early research   suggests this may    not extend   to
polyphenols, this needs further clarification to inform recommendations for use of
polyphenols during intense training periods (Beyer et al., 2017; Pavis et al., 2022).
Should the use of polyphenols during training not blunt adaptation, further research
will be warranted to investigate the impact polyphenol protein blends such the PHPM
beverage on muscular adaptation. One study has investigated the role of a protein
polyphenol blend during exercise training showing improvements in muscle protein
synthesis but have suggested this may only apply to early onset of training and may
not be ergogenic for longer term adaptation (Pavis et al., 2022). Further investigations
in this realm, while dependant on confirming no blunting of training adaptation, could
provide fascinating insight into the co-consumption of polyphenols and protein to
support exercise performance and adaptation in the longer term.



       5. Conclusion

This proof-of-concept RCT is the first to observe that 10 days of consumption of a
high protein milk   enriched   with blackberry   polyphenols   can provide    enhanced
recovery from EIMD following a single intensive exercise bout. In particular, PHPM
showed significantly improved maintenance of muscle strength compared to LPM
over the 48h post exercise and reductions in muscle soreness over the 72 hours post

                                                                                   234

[PDF page 45]


exercise when compared with both a high protein milk alone and a control low protein
milk. PHPM also showed improved recovery of muscle strength over the 48-hour
period post exercise compared with a low protein control. This polyphenol rich
intervention was particularly rich in the anthocyanin cyanidin-3-O-glucoside, and the
ellagitannins lambertianin C   and sanguiin   H-6. The    synergistic effects of berry
polyphenols and milk protein for skeletal muscle and exercise provide an exciting area
for future research and a promising avenue to support athletes following intensive
exercise.
