Primary Abstract:
AIMS: Little is known regarding the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of menthol, the active ingredient in peppermint oil (PMO). Our aim was to investigate the pharmacokinetics of menthol at 3 dose levels in children and determine their effects on gut motility and transit.
METHODS: Thirty children ages 7-12 years with functional abdominal pain underwent wireless motility capsule (WMC) testing. Approximately 1 week later they were randomized to 180, 360 or 540 mg of enteric coated PMO (10 participants per dose). Menthol pharmacokinetics were determined via blood sampling over 24 hours. They then took their respective dose of PMO (180 mg once, 180 mg twice or 180 mg thrice daily) for 1 week during which time the WMC test was repeated.
RESULTS: Evaluable area under the plasma concentration vs. time curve (AUClast ) data were available in 29 of 30 participants. A direct linear relationship (apparent dose-proportionality for systemic menthol exposure) was observed between PMO dose and menthol systemic exposure with mean elimination half-life 2.1, 3.5 and 4.6 hours for the 180, 360 and 540 mg doses, respectively. WMC technical issues precluded complete motility data in all participants. Colonic transit time was inversely related to AUClast (P = .003); transit time in other regions was not affected. In contrast, stomach, small bowel and whole gut (but not colonic) contractility positively correlated with menthol AUClast (P < .05).
CONCLUSION: Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of menthol derived from PMO demonstrated apparent dose-proportionality. A higher dose of PMO may be needed to achieve maximal gut response. www.clinicaltrials.gov NCT03295747.

Verified Round 6 Primary Facts & Critical Appraisal:
Open-label dose-escalation pharmacokinetic and wireless motility capsule (WMC) study (PMC8863319; NCT03295747; Houston, USA; n=30 children with FAP, no placebo control) testing 180, 360, or 540 mg enteric-coated peppermint oil for 1 week. FULL-TEXT AUDIT (Tables 1 & 3): Whole-cohort stool frequency tended to DECREASE during peppermint oil (1.0 +/- 0.5 -> 0.8 +/- 0.4 stools/day, p=0.055), and colonic transit time was NOT accelerated (p=0.64; numerically prolonged from 16.4 to 37.6 h in the 360 mg arm, p=0.06). Confirms peppermint does not relieve constipation and may slow gut contractions.

[MATERIALS AND METHODS] Children ages 7–12 years with FAP (paediatric Rome III criteria) were recruited from a large healthcare network based in Houston, TX, USA consisting of primary and tertiary care.14 Informed consent was obtained from parents and assent from children. The study was approved by the Baylor College of Medicine Institutional Review Board and registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03295747). Medical records were screened for ICD-10 codes for abdominal pain and functional abdominal pain. Subjects then were screened via telephone using a modified paediatric Rome III questionnaire.14,15 Children were excluded if screening or review revealed a significant chronic medical condition (e.g., coeliac disease, cystic fibrosis), chronic vomiting, unexplained weight loss, haematochezia, major GI tract surgery, significant developmental delay, an organic GI disorder and/or use of antibiotics or probiotics within the prior month. Study participants fulfilling criteria maintained baseline daily validated pain and stooling diaries for 2 weeks.16,17 Children recorded the number and severity of pain episodes (the latter on a 0–10 scale with 10 being the most severe) and the number of stools passed per day. Confirmation of the diagnosis of FAP was based on the responses to the pain and stool diaries.15 Prior to both study visits, participants fasted 8 hours and medications that could affect menthol PK or GI motility were stopped at least 5 days before. At Study Visit 1, participants ingested a wireless motility capsule (WMC; SmartPill, Medtronic Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA) following ingestion of a standardized meal (SmartBar, Medtronic Inc.) as described by the manufacturer. [METHODS] Total (i.e., conjugated and unconjugated) menthol in plasma was measured by gas chromatography mass spectrometry, as described previously13,18 with several modifications.19 Briefly, to 0.5 mL of the patient’s heparinized plasma or controls or calibrators, 20 μL internal standard (menthol-d4, 10 μg/mL; Toronto Research Chemical, North York, ON, Canada), 25 μL β-glucuronidase (90 000 U/mL; Sigma-Aldrich Corp., St. Louis, MO, USA; Type H-3 from Helix pomatia) and 10 μL sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.8) were added. The mixture was incubated overnight in air-tight tubes in a 37◦C water bath and into each tube, 150 μL 0.4 M phosphate buffer (pH 4.8) was added. Menthol was extracted in 0.5 mL methylene chloride by rocking the tubes for 5 minutes. The mixture was centrifuged at 2000 g for 5 minutes. The methylene chloride layer was transferred to an auto-sampler vial and a 2 μL extract was injected onto the gas chromatography mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) installed with ZB-1MS 15 m × 250 mm × 0.25 μm column (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA). Ions (m/z) monitored for quantification and identification were 138, 123, 95 for menthol and 142, 127, 99 for menthol-d4. The data were analysed using Target Software (Thru-Put Systems, Orlando, Florida, USA). The quantitative ions were used to construct standard curves of the peak area ratios (calibrator/internal standard pair). The assay was linear within the range of concentrations evaluated 5–1000 ng/mL (R2 > .99). For all standards, the intra-assay and interassay coefficients of variation were <10%. All data were inspected visually prior to analysis. PK analyses were conducted using Kinetica version 5.0 (Thermo Electron, Philadelphia, PA, USA).
