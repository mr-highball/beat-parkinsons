Title: TRPA1 agonists and bladder nociception in female rats suggest potential for nutraceutical benefit from cinnamon
Source: https://www.mdpi.com/1661-3821/3/1/12
DOI: 10.3390/nutraceuticals3010012
Publication date: 2023
Date basis: Publisher's volume 3, issue 1.
Checked: 2026-11-24
Review depth: full_text
Source locator: Experiment 3 methods, Results section 3.5 and Figure 3. The original article was also discussed for claim 0916 in research/2020-2024/Food-Experiment-Cinnamon-Sesame.md.

Preparation and endpoint
Anesthetized female rats with induced acute bladder inflammation and separate rats without inflammation received ground Ceylon cinnamon bark in saline by oral gavage or saline alone. Researchers measured an electromyographic reflex to bladder distension. This experiment studied a nociception proxy, with separate animal arms for bladder inflammation.

Within-model effects
The higher oral cinnamon dose had a lower distension response than saline in rats with inflamed bladders (F(1,9) = 8.256, p = .018). In rats without inflammation the same dose had a higher response than saline (F(1,8) = 6.641, p = .033). The lower oral dose did not reach statistical significance in either comparison. The isolated cinnamaldehyde bladder infusion is a different intervention within the same publication and does not add an independent source family.

Transfer limits
Culinary powder is closer to the frozen preparation than injected cinnamaldehyde. Its high-dose forced administration, anesthetized animal population, induced bladder inflammation and reflex outcome limit transfer to self-reported Parkinson pain. Both signs belong to one controlled animal study. The evidence does not provide a clinical Parkinson pain estimate or an observed oral-cinnamon urinary-urgency rate.
