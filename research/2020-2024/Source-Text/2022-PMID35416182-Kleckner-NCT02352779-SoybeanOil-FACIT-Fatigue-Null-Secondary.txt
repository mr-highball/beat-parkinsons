Primary Abstract:
Cancer-related fatigue is a prevalent, debilitating, and persistent condition. Mitochondrial dysfunction is a putative contributor to cancer-related fatigue, but relationships between mitochondrial function and cancer-related fatigue are not well understood. We investigated the relationships between mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) gene expression and cancer-related fatigue, as well as the effects of fish and soybean oil supplementation on these relationships. A secondary analysis was performed on data from a randomized controlled trial of breast cancer survivors 4-36 months posttreatment with moderate-severe cancer-related fatigue. Participants were randomized to take 6 g fish oil, 6 g soybean oil, or 3 g each daily for 6 weeks. At pre- and postintervention, participants completed the Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy-Fatigue questionnaire and provided whole blood for assessment of mtDNA gene expression. The expression of 12 protein-encoding genes was reduced to a single dimension using principal component analysis for use in regression analysis. Relationships between mtDNA expression and cancer-related fatigue were assessed using linear regression. Among 68 participants, cancer-related fatigue improved and expression of all mtDNA genes decreased over 6 weeks with no effect of treatment group on either outcome. Participants with lower baseline mtDNA gene expression had greater improvements in cancer-related fatigue. No significant associations were observed between mtDNA gene expression and cancer-related fatigue at baseline or changes in mtDNA gene expression and changes in cancer-related fatigue. Data from this exploratory study add to the growing literature that mitochondrial dysfunction may contribute to the etiology and pathophysiology of cancer-related fatigue.

[METHODS] A phase II nationwide RCT was conducted through the [X] NCORP Research Base to evaluate the effects of soybean oil and/or fish oil on CRF in breast cancer survivors; the full methods and primary aims were described previously (Peppone et al., 2019). Briefly, female breast cancer survivors were recruited 4–36 months post-treatment with surgery, radiation, and/or chemotherapy. Participants were eligible if they self-reported CRF ≥4/10 on a visual analog scale anchored with 0=not present to 10=as bad as you can imagine. They were randomized 1:1:1 to a daily supplement regimen for 6 weeks: 6 g fish oil, 6 g soybean oil, or 3 g of each. The institutional review boards at each of the five participating sites and [X] approved this study, and all participants provided written informed consent. Participants were included in this analysis if RNA-Seq data and FACIT-F data were complete at both time points. At pre- and post-intervention, participants completed the FACIT-F, which assesses quality of life and fatigue (Yellen et al., 1997). The FACIT-F is a 40-item questionnaire that asks how true each statement is during the previous week; it includes questions such as “I am able to enjoy my life” and “I feel fatigued” with five response options anchored from “not at all” to “very much” (Cella et al., 1993). The 13-item fatigue subscale is itself a valid and reliable measure of fatigue among patients with cancer (Yellen et al., 1997). Pre- and post-intervention, peripheral whole blood was collected in PaxGene Blood RNA tubes, and RNA was isolated using the PaxGene RNA isolation kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA); for full methods see (Peppone et al., 2019). [RESULTS] Seventy breast cancer survivors provided blood samples and FACIT-F responses at pre- and post-intervention. Two samples had <75% alignment from the MultiQC analysis (Dobin et al., 2013), resulting in 68 participants with evaluable data (Table 1). Participants were 61.2±9.4 years old; 84% had stage 1 or 2 cancer; 97% had undergone surgery; 79% received radiation therapy, approximately half received chemotherapy, and 75% were receiving hormone therapy. FACIT-F fatigue subscale scores improved from 26.0±8.4 points at baseline to 32.5±10.3 points post-intervention (p<0.001; Table 2). Notably, scores improved 6.5±9.9 points, and an improvement of 3–4 marks a minimally clinically important difference (Webster et al., 2003). The effect of supplementation regimen did not reach statistical significance, so data from all three treatment groups were used in further correlational analyses. Individual genes from mtDNA had relative expression levels of 377±294 (MT-ND6) to 95,698±35,991 counts (MT-ND4) (Table 3). No significant differences were observed between treatment groups at pre- or post-intervention. On average, the expression of all mitochondrial genes decreased over the course of the intervention with no effect of group (Supplemental Digital Content [SDC] 1); when a group×time interaction term was added to the model, no effect of group×time was found (p>0.10). This means that, on average, participants had fewer circulating mRNA transcripts of mtDNA protein-expressing genes post-intervention compared to pre-intervention and the three supplement doses had no differential effects on changes in gene expression. No statistically meaningful positive associations were observed between baseline mtDNA gene expression and baseline fatigue, as measured using the FACIT-F fatigue subscale
[DISCUSSION] In this exploratory study among 68 breast cancer survivors, we observed clinically meaningful improvements in CRF over a six-week RCT as assessed using the FACIT-F and a concurrent reduction in mtDNA gene expression in whole blood. Neither measure was dependent on treatment group. Notably, we showed that cancer survivors with lower mtDNA gene expression at baseline had greater improvements in CRF. These data, from novel mtDNA gene expression techniques, support the growing body of literature (Saligan et al., 2015; Tomas et al., 2017; Wawrzyniak et al., 2016) that mitochondrial dysfunction may play a role in the pathology of CRF. These results corroborate recent literature investigating mitochondrial function and CRF. For example, Chae et al. observed that reduced mtDNA content correlated with worsening CRF in a prospective study among 108 early-stage patients with breast cancer (Chae et al., 2018).
