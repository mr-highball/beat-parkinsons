Primary Abstract:
BACKGROUND: Sunflower seeds are a popular allergen-free peanut alternative. OBJECTIVE: To describe sunflower seed allergy incidence and characteristics. METHODS: We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients with sunflower seed allergy from 1995 to 2021 in a pediatric allergy clinic. We described demographic characteristics, testing results, atopic comorbidities, and reaction histories of patients with sunflower seed allergy and calculated the annual cumulative incidence of the allergy. Logistic regression was used to estimate the increase in odds of sunflower seed allergy diagnosis for each year from 1995 to 2021. RESULTS: From 1995 to 2021, we identified 235 patients with sunflower seed allergy. Among patients with sunflower seed allergy, the median age at diagnosis was 3.9 years. More than three-quarters of patients had another atopic condition. Half of the reactions consisted of mild urticaria or rash, and a quarter met criteria for anaphylaxis. The cumulative incidence ranged from 0% (1995-1999, 2001-2004, and 2006) to 0.38% (2021). From 1995 to 2021, the odds of sunflower seed allergy diagnosis increased annually by 21% (odds ratio, 1.21; 95% CI, 1.17-1.25). CONCLUSIONS: In our single-center cohort of children with sunflower seed allergy, most children were diagnosed in early childhood, had high rates of comorbid atopic conditions, and had high rates of cutaneous reactions to sunflower seed products. Moreover, in our cohort, incidence of sunflower seed allergy increased.

[METHODS] We used electronic health record (EHR) data (1995–2021) from Boston Children’s Hospital (BCH) to describe sunflower seed allergy incidence and characteristics in a retrospective cohort study. We also described mustard seed allergy incidence in a retrospective cohort study over the same time period in the same clinic population to help address the incidence of another rare food allergy over the same period of time. If the incidence estimates of both seed allergies were to go up at the same rate in the same time period, our study would be capturing an overall increase in the rate of food allergy over time, and not a trend specific to sunflower seed allergy. Such a finding would weaken the hypothesis that increases in sunflower seed exposure were the primary driver behind an increase in the sunflower seed allergy incidence. Mustard seed was used as a comparison food because the rate of exposure in this time period had likely not changed as drastically as sunflower seed exposure. We followed the The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines. We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients with sunflower seed allergy at the Allergy Program at BCH. The study was approved by the BCH Institutional Review Board. “Sunflower seed” is not a preselected allergy in our EHR, so we were unable to identify patients with sunflower seed allergy through the EHR’s allergy list. [RESULTS] Our retrospective chart review identified 3470 patients with potential sunflower seed allergy through the EHR, as illustrated in Figure 2. Of these, 235 patients were identified as having sunflower seed allergy on the basis of prespecified criteria. We used quality control measures to make sure we screened all possible patients with sunflower seed allergy. We had 33 patients screened because of provider recollection. All these 33 patients were captured by Hound Dog, and 3 (9%) of them were also captured by i2b2. We had 44 patients identified by SPT to sunflower seed butter over several months. All these 44 patients were captured by Hound Dog, and 5 (11%) of them were also identified by i2b2. Our retrospective chart review identified 1000 patients with potential mustard seed allergy through the EHR. Of these, 121 patients were identified as having mustard seed allergy on the basis of prespecified criteria. Our cohort consisted of 60.4% male patients, and median age at diagnosis was 3.9 years (IQR, 2.0–8.4 years; range, 0.7–19.8 years). There was documentation that 9 patients outgrew their sunflower seed allergy, with a median age of 6.2 years when outgrown (IQR, 4.3–7.8 years; range, 0.8–13.8 years). Among the 9 patients with documentation of outgrowing the allergy, the median time from diagnosis to be outgrown was 3.0 years (IQR, 2.0–3.6 years; range, 0.3–6.1 years). [DISCUSSION] To our knowledge, this study documents the largest cohort of patients with sunflower seed allergy. Our study identifies important characteristics of children with this emerging food allergy. We found that children were diagnosed with sunflower seed allergy in early childhood. More had ssIgE testing compared with SPT at diagnosis, which may indicate differences in the availability of testing. Testing results at diagnosis were variable, indicating that there may be benefits in obtaining both types of testing. Further studies are needed to better understand the diagnostic accuracy of sunflower seed ssIgE testing, and we need a standardized sunflower seed extract. More than half of the reactions were to sunflower seed butter, reflecting that it is a popular food. Although half of the reactions were mild,43 nearly a quarter of the reactions were classified as anaphylaxis.44 Finally, our study is the first to show an increase in the incidence of sunflower seed allergy at a more rapid pace than another food allergy (mustard seed allergy used as a control).
