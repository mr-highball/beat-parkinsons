Title: 2016 Celery root extract, venlafaxine, and St John's wort

Khalid et al., 2016, Postgraduate Medicine 128:682–683, DOI 10.1080/00325481.2016.1218263, PMID 27467225. The publisher's accessible abstract reports a woman with depression stabilized on venlafaxine and St John's wort developed a manic episode with elevated venlafaxine after starting celery root extract for menopause. The authors propose a pharmacokinetic hypothesis; this uncontrolled report cannot establish causality. Publisher abstract accessed 2026-11-15; original full case text was not accessible in this pass, so hallucination type, dose, duration and dechallenge are not verified from the original.

Le et al., 2022, DOI 10.2174/1570159X19666210809100357, PMID 34370637, PMCID PMC9881059, section 3.1 and Table 1, describe the same case as oral celery root capsules, with mania and hallucinations within 48 hours; their narrative says the symptoms resolved after celery discontinuation. This secondary narrative does not identify hallucination modality or quantify frequency. The root extract, concurrently administered agents, psychiatric population and unverified original case details prevent extrapolation to a dietary dried-leaf effect on visual hallucinations in Parkinson disease.

Phillips et al., 2018, DOI 10.1002/mds.27390, PMID 30098269, full article adverse effects: in the ketogenic arm, one participant's visual hallucinations resolved after pramipexole reduction. The menus list celery stalk and seed (sometimes optional substitutions), not confirmed dried leaves; no participant-specific celery intake or isolated food effect was reported. This event has no reported hallucination-frequency change attributable to celery leaf. The mixed diet and medication change preclude a directional claim about leaf.
