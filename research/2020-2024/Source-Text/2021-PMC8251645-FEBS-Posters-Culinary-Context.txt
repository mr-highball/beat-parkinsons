Archive scope: Paraphrased member findings from the complete 405-page primary poster supplement. Member IDs and printed pages identify distinct studies. The publisher describes the supplement as unreviewed conference abstracts and excludes late-breaking abstracts.
Reviewed: 2026-11-20

P-02.2-07, p. 160. Leri and colleagues compared oleuropein aglycone and hydroxytyrosol individually with a mixture in an Alzheimer-related cellular assay. They report more autophagy activation with the mixture, less reactive oxygen species and better cell viability after amyloid challenge. The same team published the detailed cell study in July, DOI 10.3390/ijms22137225. Treat the poster and paper as one evidence family where their experiments overlap. The poster's olive oil mention identifies the compounds' dietary origin. It has no oral oil comparison, Parkinson symptoms, bowel outcome or anxiety rating.

P-03.2-20, p. 219. Mickus and colleagues tested nutmeg essential oil and isolated sabinene, alpha-pinene and alpha-copaene in cell pairs of the Novikoff rat hepatoma line. The tested oil and terpenes inhibited Cx43 gap-junction conductance reversibly in the short term. The poster also reports reduced Cx43 expression and fewer functional channels at later measurement. Its separate cell survival, growth and colony observations cannot establish a mechanism for a culinary exposure. The same authors and measured systems occur in PMID 33444950. Neither record gives a grated or ground nutmeg comparison for depression, fatigue or constipation in Parkinson disease.

P-05.2-64, p. 277. Ulusoy and colleagues prepared a crosslinked basil seed mucilage and alginate hydrogel for potential drug delivery. Reported test outcomes were swelling across buffers and microscopy of the material. A drug-release experiment was proposed as a future step. The processed seed-derived material differs from fresh or dried culinary basil leaves. The study gives no ingestion, bowel transit, stool, fatigue, rigidity or falls measurement.

P-04.3-06, pp. 242–243. Shumakova and colleagues give 5 g per 100 g sunflower oil as part of a reference casein diet for laboratory animals. The proposed experimental contrast substitutes a microbial protein concentrate for casein while retaining the macronutrient and energy profile. The poster does not authenticate refined or cold-pressed oil, isolate the effect of the oil, or provide measured anxiety, depression, fatigue or rigidity outcomes.

P-07.1-14, p. 365. Ekremoglu and colleagues used corn oil in the control group of an acute malathion study with 24 female Wistar rats across four groups. The reported outcomes are liver interleukin-6 and insulin receptor substrate-1 after 24 hours. No comparison isolates refined corn oil from the pesticide exposure, and no Parkinson outcome was assessed. P-08.2-02, p. 413, used olive oil in a subcutaneous carrier for a mammary tumor inducer. The measured outcomes concern tumors and the effects of arginase and nitric-oxide synthase inhibition, with no oral olive-oil comparison.

P-07.2-08, p. 373. Savinova and colleagues compared bacterial isolates obtained from kefir, amasi and mahewu. Two isolates described as Lactobacillus paracasei from kefir had substantially different antimicrobial susceptibility profiles. The authors reported no transmissible resistance genes in the tested strains. The poster does not give strain identifiers. The same team's subsequent work names two kefir grain isolates, KF1 and ABK, and separately characterizes commercial drinks. These are microbiological and beverage composition comparisons, without plain kefir administered to people or measurements of falls and fatigue.

P-07.4-03, p. 384. The plant called black cumin was identified as Nigella sativa. Its seed thionins were tested against microbes. This is a different plant and preparation from Cuminum cyminum used in the frozen cumin claims. P-06.2-51, p. 316, separately refers to traditional constipation use of Pergularia tomentosa in the introduction to a liver-cell cardenolide experiment. It reports no measured bowel outcome and involves none of the queued constipation-claim preparations.

Other matched terms include onion ancestry (P-01.3-06, p. 131), egg material in genetic or biomaterial studies, a food-source list for isolated dihydro-alpha-lipoic acid in a fibrinogen assay (P-02.5-14, p. 182), tomato genetics and plant pathogens, leek plastid cold-stress transcription (P-07.4-06, p. 385), millet and barley in a plant-breeding starch context (P-07.4-24, p. 391), mango as background to rye stress tolerance (P-07.4-31, p. 393), trout metal burdens (P-07.1-25, p. 368) and farmed-trout infection markers (P-08.3-60, p. 461). Cherry is a tomato cultivar label in the seed-treatment poster on p. 290. Plant, animal, compound and symptom terms on different posters do not form a shared comparison.
