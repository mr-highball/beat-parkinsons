Ginger Tea on Dysmenorrhoea Among Nursing Students
Sheetal Crasta, Philomena Fernandes and Shynee Paul
Journal of Health and Allied Sciences NU 9(2), 64–75, issue dated 2019. First verified public online publication February 10, 2020. DOI 10.1055/s-0039-1700705.
Original full article: https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/pdf/10.1055/s-0039-1700705.pdf
Coverage: Methods and original Tables 1–2.

Fifty students with moderate or severe menstrual pain entered a quasi-experimental comparison, 25 per group. The intervention was 120 mL ginger tea after breakfast and dinner during the first two menstrual days. Pain was assessed before and two hours after each dose. The paper does not establish a fresh or grated root preparation. The groups were not randomized or masked, and there was no matched control beverage.

Table 2 between-group post-dose intensity tests give Z = −3.101 (p = 0.002) on day-one morning and Z = −4.244, −3.838 and −4.677 (each p < 0.001) at the other occasions. The report describes lower pain ratings after intervention. Table 2 does not provide a clear between-group change estimate. Table 1 gives identical printed group means for pain duration (2.68) alongside a claimed difference of 0.40. The 2013 Awed study is cited and is a separate sample. The exposure and menstrual-pain population do not establish a result for grated ginger and Parkinson pain.
