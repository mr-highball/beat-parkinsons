Primary Abstract:
The high-altitude, low-pressure, and hypoxia environment poses a significant threat to human health, particularly causing intestinal damage and disrupting gut microbiota. This study investigates the protective effects of Brassica rapa L. crude polysaccharides (BRP) on intestinal damage in mice exposed to hypobaric hypoxic conditions. Results showed that oxidative stress and inflammation levels were elevated in the hypoxia group, while BRP intervention significantly increased antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, GSH-Px, T-AOC) and reduced inflammatory markers (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α). BRP also restored intestinal barrier function by enhancing claudin-1, occludin, and ZO-1 expression. Notably Chromatographic and metagenomic analyses revealed that BRP enriched butyrate levels, promoted beneficial bacteria like Akkermansia muciniphila and Leuconostoc lactis, and upregulated L-arginine biosynthesis II and L-methionine biosynthesis III pathways to enhance antioxidant activity. Fecal microbiota transfer experiments confirmed the role of gut microbiota in mediating BRP's protective effects, providing valuable insights into prebiotic-based therapeutic strategies for hypobaric hypoxia-induced intestinal damage.

[Discussion] An increasing number of research suggests that plant extracts can effectively modulate intestinal disorders and ameliorate oxidative stress-related diseases29–31. which are both key fator influence high altitude disease especially the intestinal injury caused by hypobaric and hypoxia conditions29. We used a hypobaric oxygen chamber to simulate high-altitude conditions to evaluate the protective effects of BRP on high-altitude intestinal injury and further explore the response mechanism of gut microbiota through metagenomic analysis. In lines with our study, pervious study also reported a notable decrease in food and water intake, accompanied by a rapid decline in body weight in mice, resembling human responses to high-altitude environments32. Additionally, acute hypoxia leads to the generation of ROS within the body, triggering oxidative stress in tissues and organs, resulting in damage2. Vital antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px play indispensable roles in safeguarding against ROS-induced harm7. Studies have found that in high-altitude environments, when the activity of antioxidant enzymes in the mitochondria of lung tissue cannot remove free radicals, it causes damage to rat lung tissue33. Research has shown that polysaccharides improve oxidative damage in organisms by enhancing SOD activity and reducing MDA levels34,35. Research on FMT has found that FMT intervention reduced the content of MDA in DSS-induced colitis in mice and increased the activity of the antioxidant enzymes SOD and CAT36. Our study demonstrates that BRP can increase the activity of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, GSH-Px and T-AOC), alleviating oxidative stress induced by hypoxia, and FMT intervention also improves antioxidant activity to a certain extent.
