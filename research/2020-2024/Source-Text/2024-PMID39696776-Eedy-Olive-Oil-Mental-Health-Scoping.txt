The review included 49 studies across human experimental, observational and animal designs, including studies that used olive oil as a comparator. The reported observational directions varied: higher rates of mental illness in five reports, lower rates in eight, and no association in five. The aggregate favorable experimental description covers anxiety or depression, so separate primary endpoints, study families, and each oil exposure need verification. Its indexed references include the human acute typing-stress imaging study PMID 33841804.

Original body and study-family follow-through, November 26, 2026

Table 1 groups four human experimental studies in which olive oil was the intervention. Foshati studied depressive symptoms and biological outcomes in major depression. Rus compared extra-virgin and refined olive oil for a mental-health composite in fibromyalgia, with no separate anxiety measure. Canheta reported HADS anxiety in severe obesity under three active study arms. The trial is already mapped in the experiment, and within-arm declines do not identify a standalone oil effect. Mitsukura studied an imaging measure during a mental task. Thus the four-study category contains one trial that reports anxiety and supplies no new direct Parkinson anxiety contrast.

In Table 2, Daley’s adverse anxiety association is with estimated oleic acid across foods. The original publication reports an association with self-reported diagnosed anxiety (OR 1.02 per gram, CI 1.00–1.05) and no association for the non-diagnostic anxiety symptom measure. The exposure is neither grade-specific olive oil nor a controlled diet. The review’s 13 studies using oil as a comparator address several distinct domains. Similar within-arm changes and comparative results cannot all be treated as oil benefit. The print issue appeared March 28, 2026, after the first online publication in December 2024.
