# Chervil and lovage leaf outcomes

## 2020 Parkinson diet appendix and individual symptom coverage

Hegelmaier and colleagues list chervil and lovage among the seasonings in Appendix A. The vegetarian dietary intervention included 16 Parkinson participants, six on diet alone and ten on diet with enemas. The article reports total UPDRS III improvement in both groups. Figure 4 reports total scores and Figure S2 reports individual participants' total score changes during the intervention. The paper does not specify actual individual herb intake, fresh versus dried form, plant part or dose, or report a herb-specific effect on freezing episodes, dystonia or dyskinesia. It does not report herb-specific apathy, mood, attention, sleep, fatigue, hallucination, constipation or pain endpoints. [Existing annotation](Food-Experiment-Umbellifer-Herbs.md#chervil-lovage-and-coriander-in-a-parkinson-diet-intervention) · [Local primary text and Appendix A](Source-Text/PMC7072275.txt) · [Primary publisher and supplement](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4409/9/2/376)

## 2020 Feasibility and adverse events in the six-week lovage soup trial

Cong and colleagues presented BLEND2 at FENS 2019 and published the proceedings on June 10, 2020. The paper specifies **fresh lovage** in a tomato and red onion soup which was canned and heat-processed. The study enrolled 77 adults and reported four reflux or heartburn events and three gastrointestinal distress events, with three dropouts associated with those adverse issues. The abstract does not attribute events to an individual herb or give a group-specific breakdown. Neither soup arm showed changes in weight or blood lipids versus habitual diet. This is a mixed cooked-food tolerability report in adults without a Parkinson cohort. The frozen lovage symptom outcomes were outside the reported scope. [Primary abstract](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0029665120005790) · [Food processor report](https://www.campdenbri.co.uk/news/may2019.pdf) · [2019 registration](../2015-2019/Food-Experiment-Chervil-Lovage-Outcomes.md#2019-registration-for-a-six-week-lovage-soup-trial)

## 2020 Fresh lovage leaf phenolics after simulated digestion

Złotek and colleagues compared phenolic fractions from fresh lovage leaves grown with or without treatment. They measured lipoxygenase and cyclooxygenase-2 inhibition before and after simulated digestion. Some treated-plant fractions had stronger enzyme effects than controls, and results depended on the extraction and assay. This lab comparison did not measure pain severity. [Local primary text, Figure 1](Source-Text/PMC7346211.txt) · [Prior phenolic comparison](../2015-2019/Food-Experiment-Chervil-Lovage-Outcomes.md#2019-lovage-leaf-phenolics-and-inflammatory-enzyme-activity)

## 2020 Lovage extract in a rat spatial learning study

Amraie and colleagues administered *Levisticum officinale* extract in a lipopolysaccharide neuroinflammation model. The published abstract reports improved spatial learning and memory and changes in hippocampal markers. The accessible abstract does not identify the botanical organ or extraction solvent. Spatial learning in a rodent inflammatory model cannot identify a change in human Parkinson attention after fresh leaf consumption. [Primary abstract, published July 10](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlelanding/2020/fo/d0fo01030h/unauth)

## 2021 Dried lovage leaf extracts and inflammatory enzyme activity

Złotek and colleagues studied conventionally, microwave-, freeze- and traditionally dried leaves, including plants grown without elicitation. Traditional drying yielded lower antioxidant potential in the experiments, and convective drying lowered the lipoxygenase inhibition capacity of samples after simulated digestion. These extraction results provide a preparation-specific laboratory contrast, without an animal nociception or clinical pain result. [Local primary text, Results](Source-Text/PMC8146002.txt)

## 2021 Aconite leaves mistaken for lovage in a meal

Michel and colleagues documented two people who ate garden herbs including monkshood leaves mistaken for lovage. Paresthesias, hypotension and arrhythmias followed, and both serum specimens contained aconitine. This food adverse-event report establishes a separate exposure and does not identify effects on fatigue, sleep, dyskinesia or other symptoms from authenticated lovage leaves. [Primary abstract in German and English](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34251482/)

## 2022 Garden chervil leaf and stem chemistry

Slimestad and colleagues analyzed fresh-cut garden chervil leaves and stems. Methanolic extracts contained apiin, other flavone glycosides and a bicyclic lactone. Radical scavenging was measured in a chemical assay, and an isolated compound showed moderate cytotoxic activity in leukemia and normal cell lines. These measurements do not resolve effects on clinical cognition, attention, pain or fatigue after eating the herb. [Local primary text, Methods and Table 1](Source-Text/PMC9098402.txt)

## 2022 Lovage extract and treadmill arms in a rat memory study

Amraie and colleagues compared treadmill running and *Levisticum officinale* extract in the same type of lipopolysaccharide rat model. The public abstract attributes improvements in spatial learning and memory to treadmill exercise alone and in combination with extract. The abstract does not give an extract-only behavioral effect size or verify the plant organ. The study was first posted December 8, 2022, and the journal issue was dated 2023. It does not measure clinical attention or fatigue after culinary lovage. [Primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36478529/) · [2020 extract study](#2020-lovage-extract-in-a-rat-spatial-learning-study)

## 2024 Chervil extract and neurodegeneration-related enzymes

Stojković and colleagues studied a garden chervil methanolic extract using enzyme assays, cell experiments and docking. The public abstract reports enzyme activity and predicted binding of identified constituents to acetylcholinesterase, among other targets. Its anti-inflammatory experiment used keratinocytes. These outcomes are distinct from patient attention, executive function and pain. The abstract gives no comparison of fresh and dried culinary herb. [Primary abstract, first published July 6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38977255/)
