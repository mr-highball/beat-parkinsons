# Anti-inflammatory diet, oil and tea in a multiple-sclerosis fatigue trial

## Mixed-diet fatigue effects and endpoint extraction

Mousavi-Shirazi-Fard and colleagues first published the trial on April 16, 2020. In the accessible author abstract, the personalized anti-inflammatory diet favored the diet arm over healthy-diet recommendations on the Modified Fatigue Impact Scale at 12 weeks (p = .001). The abstract describes 100 participants. NICE's independent study extraction records 104 randomized and 100 analyzed, with 50 analyzed per arm. It lists canola and olive oil for cooking or salad dressing and allows green or white tea within the overall dietary prescription. The extraction lists spices generally and does not name cinnamon or verify individual food consumption or preparation. [Publisher author abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32249637/) · [NICE intervention and outcome tables, printed pp. 524–528](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK585277/bin/niceng220er3-app-et1.pdf#page=524)

NICE reports mean MFIS totals of 47.96 to 47.22 for the diet arm and 47.84 to 47.92 for controls. Its unadjusted endpoint difference is −0.70 points (95% CI −5.34 to 3.94). This is a different analysis from the favorable p value reported in the article's abstract. The original outcome table and model specification remain unverified. Neither source isolates refined canola oil or brewed green tea, and the population has multiple sclerosis. The trial registration lists the planned fatigue outcome and the two diet groups.
