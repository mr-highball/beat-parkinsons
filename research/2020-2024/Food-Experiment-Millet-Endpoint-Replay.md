# Millet pain evidence

## Millet porridge in a labor analgesia comparison

Bu, Wu and Cheng first posted their 70-patient study on 30 October 2024 and published it on 7 March 2025. All participants ate approximately 300 g millet porridge, species and cooking details unreported. Thirty-five chose epidural analgesia and 35 did not. Pain was recorded before and at 60, 90 and 120 minutes after the common meal; the final report says scores were lower in the epidural group at the three postmeal measurements. This is a self-selected analgesia comparison without any millet-free group, not evidence that millet caused a pain change. The population was laboring women, not Parkinson patients. [2024 preprint record](https://sciety.org/articles/activity/10.21203/rs.3.rs-5338203/v1) · [2025 original, Methods and Results](https://www.ejog.org/article/S0301-2115%2825%2900135-6/pdf)

An earlier [2022 preprint](https://assets-eu.researchsquare.com/files/rs-2293383/v1/479d02a8-815e-499a-bfbf-7873b259fd36.pdf?c=1681973073) shares two authors, institutions and a very similar meal/protocol but describes 86 participants (66 vs 20), not 70 (35 vs 35). Cohort overlap is unresolved; do not count independent cohorts without clarification.

## Pearl millet cultivar and measured daily physical activity

First published June 3, 2021. The analysis comes from a randomized six-month Indian adolescent feeding study comparing iron-biofortified pearl millet against conventional pearl millet. Accelerometry data were collected from 130 children over six-day periods at baseline and follow-up. Adjusted light activity was 22.3 minutes per day higher with iron-biofortified millet (95% CI 1.8–42.8, p = .034). This measures general activity. The paper does not make a dexterity comparison, and the two arms both received pearl millet.
