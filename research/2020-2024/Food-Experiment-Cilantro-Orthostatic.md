# Cilantro, blood pressure and orthostatic symptoms

## 2020 — Coriander listed in a Parkinson dietary intervention

Hegelmaier and colleagues' Appendix A lists coriander among seasonings in the vegetarian diet used for Parkinson participants. The table groups seasonings without naming an individual exposure, plant part or preparation. The intervention paper reports motor examinations and does not give levels of standing lightheadedness or blood pressure by ingredient. [Existing local primary assessment](Food-Experiment-Umbellifer-Herbs.md#chervil-lovage-and-coriander-in-a-parkinson-diet-intervention) · [Local report and Appendix A](Source-Text/PMC7072275.txt)

## 2020 — Acute mixed-spice meal pilot measured endothelial dilation

Two reports associated with NCT03064958 describe postprandial measurements after adding 0, 2 or 6 g of a spice blend to a high-saturated-fat, high-carbohydrate meal in men at cardiovascular risk. The cytokine report lists 0.13 and 0.40 g of coriander in the two spice conditions (Table 2) without specifying its plant part. The primary vascular report found a smaller decrease in flow-mediated dilation after the 6 g meal than after the no-spice meal. Blood pressure measurements were baseline characteristics. Neither paper tests standing symptoms or describes Parkinson participants. [Primary vascular report](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2020/fo/c9fo02438g) · [Local cytokine report, Methods and Tables 2–3](Source-Text/PMC7269750.txt) · [Official registry](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03064958)

## 2021 — Freeze-dried leaf extract inhibited ACE in vitro

The Thai vegetable experiment used authenticated *Coriandrum sativum* leaves. The researchers freeze-dried and ground them before extraction in aqueous ethanol. Table 4 reports 70.66 ± 2.34% ACE inhibition at 0.2 mg/mL extract across triplicate assays. The Results and table footnote specify 0.2 mg/mL, while the Discussion gives 1 mg/mL. The result concerns an enzyme assay with a solvent extract. No blood pressure was measured. [Local licensed source, Methods and Table 4](Source-Text/PMC8400534.txt)

## 2021 — Four-week mixed seasonings and ambulatory blood pressure

Petersen and colleagues' NCT03064932 crossover trial assigned three dose levels of mixed culinary herbs and spices. Table 2 lists coriander at 0.069, 0.417 and 0.833 g/day per 2100 kcal, with 23 other seasonings changing concurrently. The high-spice diet lowered mean 24-hour systolic pressure versus the moderate-spice diet by 1.9 mm Hg (95% CI −3.6 to −0.2, p = .02). The high versus low comparison was −1.6 mm Hg (95% CI −3.3 to 0.04, p = .058). Participants were at increased cardiometabolic risk. The reported 24-hour means are not paired active-standing results. [Primary report, design and Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8634565/) · [Primary abstract and effect estimates](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34510174/) · [Registered protocol](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03064932)

## 2022 — Animal blood pressure studies of coriander extracts

Rana and colleagues used an aqueous-methanol extract of dried coriander seeds in rodents with arsenic-induced hypertension. The Methods identify the seed preparation and the tail-cuff pressure measurements. The authors report that the seed-extract intervention attenuated the systolic, diastolic and mean pressure elevations caused by arsenic. They also studied lemon peel and combined extracts in separate groups. This experiment concerns a chemically induced hypertension model with no paired standing response or intact leaf consumption. [Local licensed source, Sections 2.2–2.3, Methods and Results](Source-Text/PMC9695286.txt)

Wang and colleagues report that oral coriander at 1 or 2 g/kg attenuated high-fructose/high-salt diet-induced elevations of systolic, diastolic and mean arterial pressure in rats. The accessible abstract does not specify the plant part or confirm the material as a culinary preparation. The publisher's full Methods were inaccessible. Supplemental Table S1 was recovered from ACS Figshare and lists chemical identifications without a plant-part or preparation description. The record supports an animal blood-pressure lead without establishing a leaf effect. [Primary abstract, online March 22, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35315647/) · [Primary supporting Table S1](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jafc.2c00267.s001)

## 2023 — Ten- and twenty-minute coriander oil inhalation

Yarosh and colleagues report several separate essential-oil arms in a total sample of 849 women with hypertension. Systolic pressure after ten minutes of relaxation with coriander vapor fell from 156.04 to 141.46 mm Hg in 26 participants. Relaxation alone changed from 155.26 to 152.00 in 42 controls (between-group change p = .0006 as tabulated). Table 4 reports a within-arm fall in the 20-minute series with no significant difference versus relaxation (p = .13). The plant part used for the oil is not identified in the report. The investigators and intervention overlap with the 2018 reports, but the sample sizes and means differ and shared participants are unquantified. There was no orthostatic challenge, oral coriander or Parkinson group. [Primary Russian report, Tables 1 and 4](https://www.mediasphera.ru/issues/voprosy-kurortologii-fizioterapii-i-lechebnoj-fizicheskoj-kultury/2023/2/1004287872023021022) · [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37141519/)
