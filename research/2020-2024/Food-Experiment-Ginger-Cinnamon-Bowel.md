# Ginger and cinnamon bowel outcomes, 2020–2024

## Standardized ginger tablets in relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis

Foshati and colleagues randomized 52 adults with relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis to ginger tablets standardized for gingerols or corn placebo for 12 weeks. Forty-nine completed the study. The intention-to-treat tables include all 52. On 100 mm visual analogue scales, constipation frequency changed by −23.63 (SE 5.36) versus +14.81 (SE 2.78), and severity by −24.15 (SE 5.10) versus +11.39 (SE 3.23), both baseline-adjusted comparisons p<.001. Baseline severity was higher in the ginger arm (40.77 versus 18.65), and the study assessed several gastrointestinal symptoms. The tablet preparation and disease differ from fresh grated ginger and Parkinson disease. The authors report registration IRCT20180818040827N3. The earlier neurological outcomes article cites the same trial and contributes no independent constipation analysis. [Original report, 27 October 2023, Tables 3–4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10605938/) · [Licensed article text](Source-Text/PMC10605938.txt)

## Pulverized ginger rhizome and hypothyroidism symptoms

Ashraf and colleagues randomized 60 adults with treated primary hypothyroidism to pulverized ginger rhizome or starch capsules for 30 days. The end-point symptom analysis included 27 and 26, respectively. The constipation item decreased from 1.06 to 0.38 in the ginger group and increased from 0.98 to 1.04 with placebo. The end-point between-group comparison gave p<.001. Thyroid disease and the 30-day capsule intervention limit transfer to Parkinson constipation and a fresh preparation. [Original 2022 report, Table 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8794674/) · [Licensed article text](Source-Text/PMC8794674.txt)

## Standardized ginger extract and occasional constipation

Eraiah and colleagues randomized 60 adults to standardized ginger extract or cellulose placebo for 56 days. Fifty-five completed. The report gives favorable symptom scores at weeks four and eight and higher stool frequency at the measured follow-ups. Stool consistency was inconclusive between groups at every visit. The abstract and methods describe the administered extract and gingerol mass inconsistently. The manufacturer funded the study. The authors give registry ID CTRI/2022/01/039843. This adds a symptom-specific estimate for a processed ginger preparation in a separate population. [Original report, 29 August 2023](https://annexpublishers.co/full-text/JNH/10104/Efficacy-of-Standard.php) · [Original methods and results summary](Source-Text/2023-Eraiah-Ginger-Constipation.txt)

## Cassia water extract and transit in adults with diarrhea

Park and colleagues randomized 70 adults with diarrhea to cassia aqueous extract or placebo for eight weeks. Colonic transit changed from 19.2 to 21.7 hours with extract and from 25.6 to 15.6 hours in the comparison arm, with a treatment-by-time interaction p=.019. Much of the relative difference reflects faster transit in the placebo arm. The report supplies a transit measure in diarrhea, with no estimate of constipation symptoms after ground cinnamon. [Original 2023 report, Table 2](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlepdf/2023/fo/d2fo01835g) · [Original methods and results summary](Source-Text/2023-Park-Cassia-Transit.txt)

## Cinnamon spice crossover and daily stool counts

Eighteen adults with obesity and prediabetes received 4 g of encapsulated Cinnamomum burmannii powder daily or matched maltodextrin placebo in two four-week periods separated by a two-week washout. There was no clear between-treatment-by-time difference for daily stool counts in the original main results. The supplement's arm-level stool-count estimates remain open. The study gives an indirect measure in a population unselected for constipation. [Original 2024 report](https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165%2824%2900008-X/fulltext) · [Original methods and results summary](Source-Text/2024-Zelicha-Cinnamon-Stool.txt)

## Cassia extract and stool output in a rat diarrhea model

Yu and colleagues prepared a cassia bark water extract and administered it to maternally separated rats. The middle and high doses reduced the model's elevated pellet frequency over two hours, with no improvement in fecal consistency. The Results and Discussion are inconsistent on the model's fecal water content. The direction on pellet output differs from the loperamide constipation model. It does not provide a human ground-cinnamon constipation comparison.
