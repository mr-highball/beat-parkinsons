# Hazelnut and walnut oil outcome follow-up

## Oral hazelnut oil and timed urine collection

Abraham and colleagues first published October 20, 2020. In two exposure weeks, the same 12 volunteers ate 12 g of Culinaria hazelnut oil containing abundant MCPD esters or 50 g of palm fat rich in glycidyl esters with bread. The study selected a commercially sampled contaminant-rich lot and does not confirm the roasting or pressing status. On exposure days, staff asked participants to drink enough to void on an hourly schedule. The report gives 0.73–5.87 L/day (mean 2.63 L, SD 1.15) pooled across 96 participant-days. It provides no oil-condition-specific total urine-volume comparison or symptom-scale measure. Measured changes were urinary excretion of contaminants. A related 2021 paper used the same exposure to measure alternative urinary metabolites. The findings identify a human oral hazelnut-oil protocol for 0397, while the scheduled voids and pooled volume do not estimate urgency. [First primary report, §§2.2–2.3 and 3.1](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mnfr.202000735) · [Companion paper](https://doi.org/10.1002/mnfr.202000736) · [2017 registration](../2015-2019/Food-Experiment-Hazelnut-Walnut-Gap-Followup.md#hazelnut-oil-in-a-registered-urinary-biomarker-study)

## Whole walnuts and post-snack saliva flow

Simões and colleagues published April 21, 2021. Twelve adults completed four counterbalanced test sessions with 15.7 g whole walnuts, bread, apple and yogurt as energy-matched snacks. Unstimulated saliva was collected before and immediately, five minutes and 30 minutes after each snack. Section 2.1 and Figure 1A show increased flow after yogurt or apple and no clear increase after whole walnuts or bread. A salivary band that the authors tentatively identify as a proline-rich protein increased after walnuts, without evidence of an overall walnut flow increase. This related null finding addresses saliva output under short-term whole-nut exposure for 0813. Drooling in Parkinson’s and cold-pressed walnut oil remain distinct. [Primary article, Figure 1A and Sections 2.1 and 4.1–4.2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8122275/) · [Local article text](Source-Text/PMC8122275.txt)

## Whole walnuts with or without environmental enrichment in a rotarod test

An aged NMRI mouse study first published December 26, 2020 compared standard housing and a sunflower-oil control diet with a 6% whole-walnut diet, alone or with separate housing enrichment. The 6% walnut diet alone did not clearly change rotarod latency to fall (p = .9999). Combining walnuts with enrichment was associated with better rotarod performance. The study lacked an enrichment-without-walnuts arm. It supplies a related null motor result for 0859 and leaves effects on clinical fall incidence unresolved. [Primary study, Figure 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7929966/) · [Local article text](Source-Text/PMC7929966.txt)

## Whole walnuts with concurrent training and measured postural sway

Kamoun and colleagues first published February 29, 2024. Twenty of 28 randomized physically active men aged 65 or older completed six weeks of matched endurance and resistance training, with or without 15 g/day whole walnuts. Center-of-pressure excursions improved in the walnut-plus-training arm on selected dual-task conditions. Table 4 gives training-by-group interactions for eyes-open mediolateral excursion (F(1,18) = 9.4, p < .01) and eyes-closed mediolateral excursion (F(1,18) = 4.6, p < .05). The article reports an eyes-closed anteroposterior interaction of F(1,18) = 47, p < .001 in prose, while Table 4 lists the same comparison as nonsignificant. The eyes-open anteroposterior interaction is p < .05 in Table 4 and p < .01 in prose despite the same F(1,18) = 5.4. The published anteroposterior significance reports are inconsistent. Baseline-only six-minute walking test and the posturography do not measure actual fall frequency. Sleep quality was measured by the six-question Spiegel questionnaire and improved more with walnuts (interaction p = .03). It did not use a daytime sleepiness scale. This is related to 0859 through measured balance, with different preparation and population. [Primary paper, Methods, Tables 1 and 4 and Figure 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10904559/) · [Local article text](Source-Text/PMC10904559.txt)

## Registration for whole walnut and sleep measurement

NCT04799821 was first posted March 16, 2021 for a crossover of daily walnuts and a nut-free control period. The native registry search returned a completed record with no results posted. The current registry lists 100 as actual enrollment and does not enumerate the Epworth Sleepiness Scale among its outcomes. Izquierdo-Pulido and colleagues presented this same registration at the November 2023 European Nutrition Conference. Its proceedings were published February 28, 2024 despite the 2023 issue label. The proceedings report 80 young adults and lower Epworth daytime sleepiness after walnuts (p = .004). The 2025 full paper accounts for four post-inspection exclusions and analyzes 76 participants. These are reports from one trial of whole walnuts in healthy adults. ## Sunflower oil comparator and open-field stress proxies

The aged control feed in Esselun and colleagues contained sunflower oil as the fat basis. The comparison diets used 6% whole walnuts and one group also had enriched housing. Supplementary Table 9 records 3.0 fecal boli on average in the aged sunflower control, 3.2 with walnuts and 2.7 with walnuts plus enrichment (overall p=.8856). The deposited table numbering differs from the parent text's pointer for the droppings data. The authors also report no clear group contrasts in open-field distance, speed, turning or stop fraction. The study gives a feed comparator and animal proxy results. The oil grade and a clinical anxiety effect remain unresolved.
