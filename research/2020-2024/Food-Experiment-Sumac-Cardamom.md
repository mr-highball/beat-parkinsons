# Food experiment evidence for sumac and cardamom

## 2020 Sumac powder and Beck depression scores

Hariri and colleagues randomized 62 overweight or obese women with mild or moderate depression to 3 g/day *Rhus coriaria* fruit powder capsules or placebo. Both arms followed a calorie-restricted diet for 12 weeks. Table 4 reports BDI-II from 25.38 to 14.96 with sumac and 26.38 to 16.64 with placebo. Mean changes were −10.41 ± 11.08 and −9.74 ± 8.50, respectively (between-group p = .78). The trial tested a preparation close to the ground spice in 0682 and the named symptom with a validated scale. The population did not have Parkinson’s, and the improvement from baseline in the sumac arm cannot isolate the intervention’s effect. The study cites registration IRCT20131228015968N6. [Original article](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ptr.6737) · [Full author copy, Results](https://www.academia.edu/45075643/The_beneficial_effects_of_sumac_Rhus_coriaria_L_supplementation_along_with_restricted_calorie_diet_on_anthropometric_indices_oxidative_stress_and_inflammation_in_overweight_or_obese_women_with_depression_A_randomized_clinical_trial) · [Iranian registry](https://en.irct.ir/trial/36888)

## 2020 Cardamom listed in a Parkinson diet intervention

Appendix A of Hegelmaier and colleagues’ Parkinson pilot lists cardamom among foods and spices in a 14-day ovo-lacto vegetarian program. Six participants had the diet alone and ten also received enemas. Total UPDRS III scores improved in both subgroups (p = .033 and .005). The scale includes a speech item, rigidity, postural stability and rest tremor. The article gives a composite score, with no reported cardamom intake per patient, cardamom-specific comparator or separate scores for those items. It therefore informs 0455, 0527, 0602, 0706 and 0884 as a documented dietary context. The two speech claims remain distinct because the paper does not report separate volume and intelligibility measures. [Primary report, Appendix A and Figure 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7072275/) · [Existing local full text](Source-Text/PMC7072275.txt)

## 2020 and 2022 Green cardamom powder and blood pressure in type 2 diabetes

Ghazi Zahedi and colleagues’ protocol proposed 3 g/day whole green cardamom powder in capsules or rusk placebo for ten weeks in 80 adults with type 2 diabetes and cites IRCT2016042717254N5. The subsequent results report lower systolic blood pressure versus placebo. This adds a controlled blood-pressure lead for 0453, alongside the 2017 trial’s null result in prediabetes. The protocol and results do not measure Parkinson’s or orthostatic lightheadedness. [Protocol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7440108/) · [Local protocol text](Source-Text/PMC7440108.txt) · [Primary results](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hermed.2022.100555)

## 2021 Green cardamom fruit extract and mouse learning

Saini and colleagues tested an ethanolic *Elettaria cardamomum* fruit extract with scopolamine and other induced amnesia models in mice. They reported changes in maze and avoidance performance. This provides a different preparation and cognitive task for comparison with 0574. The study did not measure sustained attention after ground spice ingestion in Parkinson’s. [Primary article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40816-021-00300-5)

## 2021 Aqueous tulsi and cardamom mixture and depression scores

Suresh and colleagues’ abstract describes 40 elderly people with depression, with one group receiving aqueous extracts of tulsi and *Elettaria cardamomum* and another receiving green tea extract for eight weeks. It reports a statistically significant pre-post change in the blend arm and no significant change in the green-tea arm. The available abstract provides no between-arm estimate. The two-herb extract and non-Parkinson population provide a mood-related lead for 0777. [Article DOI](https://doi.org/10.18311/jnr/2021/27128) · [Original abstract indexed by OpenAlex](https://openalex.org/works/W3181911683)

## 2022 Cardamom as an odor test with a null case-control contrast

Baert and colleagues report that their cardamom odor identification test found no significant difference between participants with Parkinson’s and healthy controls. The test used natural spice odors, with no consumption or pre-post outcome. This gives a Parkinson diagnostic comparison for 0550 alongside [the 2021 Jacob test](Food-Experiment-HAL-Followup.md#2021-cardamom-and-ginger-odors-in-a-parkinson-smell-test) and [the 2026 Nagaraj test](../2025-2029/Food-Experiment-Herb-Spice-Followup.md#cardamom-odor-as-an-assessment-stimulus), which found differences under other methods and samples. [Primary report, Figure 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9072051/) · [Existing local full text](Source-Text/PMC9072051.txt)

## 2023 Sumac fruit extract and tail suspension in mice

Parizad and Abbasi Maleki administered an ethanolic *Rhus coriaria* extract by intraperitoneal injection in mice. Extract doses of 50–200 mg/kg reduced immobility in the tail suspension test. The open-field locomotor measure did not significantly change. The result is a mood-related preclinical lead for 0682. The study used an injected extract and a behavioral proxy, with no Parkinson model or clinical depression score. [Primary article, Figure 1](https://brieflands.com/journals/jjnpp/articles/139330)

## 2023 Cardamom oil and reserpine-induced depressive behavior in rats

Abdel-Rasoul and colleagues administered oral cardamom oil to reserpine-injected male rats. They reported reduced depression-like behavior in forced swimming and changes in open-field activity. The experiment used reserpine to model depressive behavior. The oil, animal endpoint and experimental population limit its relevance to the ground cardamom and depressive symptoms claim 0777. [Primary study, PMID 36781058](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36781058/)

## 2023 Cardamom in allergic reactions with dysphagia and presyncope

Bernal-Rubio and colleagues described a 38-year-old woman who developed dysphagia and angioedema five minutes after a supervised challenge with 0.8 g of Sicilian mortadella. The recorded blood pressure was 120/84 mmHg. Cardamom was the only additive with a positive prick-by-prick result. The patient’s serum also reacted to proteins in the cardamom extract, including a candidate α-L-fucosidase. Abdominal pain occurred after the original sandwich. The challenge involved the mixed food and the paper does not identify the spice particle size. This is an acute allergic dysphagia lead for 0748 and abdominal pain context for 0363. Published online November 8, 2023 and in the June 2024 issue under the same DOI. [Primary report, pages 205–206](https://www.jiaci.org/revistas/vol34issue3_5-8.pdf)

Leck and colleagues reported presyncope after a cinnamon-cardamom bun in a 69-year-old woman and hypotension after challenge with the same bun in the 2023 British Society for Allergy and Clinical Immunology abstracts. The patient tolerated the other listed bun ingredients, and tryptase rose during the challenge. The report describes an allergic reaction. It provides no standing test or Parkinson-related orthostatic symptom measurement for 0453. [Primary abstract A021](https://www.bsaci.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Clin-Experimental-Allergy-2023-_.pdf)

## 2023 Sumac and anise reflux trial registration

IRCT20231008059650N1 registered a double-blind trial on October 31, 2023. The intervention field describes 1.5 g brown sumac and 1.5 g anise per day in capsules for four weeks. The trial planned to measure heartburn, regurgitation and complications. Recruitment is marked complete and the completion-date field is blank. The registration gives no outcome values and does not identify a Parkinson sample. [Official registration](https://irct.ir/trial/73127)

## Appetite companion of the ground sumac depression trial

Hariri and colleagues' February 2023 appetite report gives the same registry ID and ethics approval as their 2020 depression report. The later report lists 60 enrolled and 57 analyzed, whereas the earlier paper reports 62 allocated. The cohort is counted once pending participant reconciliation. Both tested 3 g/day of sumac versus placebo with calorie restriction for 12 weeks. The 2023 paper reports an appetite contrast (p = .02) and metabolic markers. It gives no independent depression estimate. ## Cardamom in an uncontrolled Jawarish Shahi bowel study

The 2022 report identifies green cardamom fruit or seed in Table 1 as one component of the oral Unani preparation. The mean composite irritable bowel severity score declined from 229.50 to 123.76 by day 45 in the single-arm study. The scale includes dissatisfaction with bowel habits and other symptoms. A bowel-habit or symptom discussion does not supply a controlled constipation severity estimate. Individual cardamom attribution and a Parkinson cohort are absent. [Primary report and tables](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9618389/) · [Local factual summary](Source-Text/2022-Jawarish-Shahi-IBS-Cardamom.txt)

## Cardamom in a controlled multi-ingredient study with bowel comments

Wightman and colleagues analyzed 66 supplement and 62 placebo participants. The 90-day formula lists cardamom without species, plant part or grind. The supplement presents 13 active and ten placebo day-90 bowel comments. Two active comments mention constipation or difficulty, and six placebo comments mention constipation. A placebo comment separately reports improvement. The table is a selected set of responses, without randomized-arm prevalence or validated severity. A separate adverse-event table lists zero versus two constipation reports. The registry gives a different, enrolled, denominator and records no posted results. Timing of recruitment differed markedly by group before and after the pandemic. The study does not isolate any botanical. [Original body](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10598389/) · [Licensed text](Source-Text/PMC10598389.txt) · [Supplement result summary](Source-Text/2023-Wightman-Cardamom-Supplement-Bowels.txt) · [Official registry](Source-Text/Trials/NCT05504668.txt)

## Recognition of cardamom essence in a Parkinson smell test

Mukhtar and colleagues used ten odorants in a Pakistani case-control smell identification test. Original Table 4 reports correct identification of a commercial cardamom essence in 36 of 87 Parkinson cases and 49 of 58 controls (p < .001). The article PDF lists publication December 31, 2021 and the journal record lists posting January 19, 2022. A different 2022 natural cardamom smell test reports an inconclusive case-control difference. The tests measured recognition of presented odors with distinct procedures. They did not study the effect of eating ground cardamom. [original Methods and Table 4](https://nja.pastic.gov.pk/PJCIS/index.php/PJNS/article/download/3641/3584) · [Local text](Source-Text/2021-Mukhtar-Cardamom-Odor-Table-Extract.txt)

### Original tulsi–cardamom outcome tables

The full randomized report gives post-treatment Geriatric Depression Scale mean ranks 11.98 for the aqueous tulsi–cardamom blend and 29.03 for green tea, 20 older adults per arm. U=29.5 and Z=−4.634, reported p<.001. The product contained both plants, and the report estimates a post-treatment rank contrast rather than a between-group change. It supplies no isolated ground cardamom or Parkinson depression estimate.
