# Coffee and tea outcomes in Parkinson’s and task-fatigue trials

## Prediagnostic coffee and clinical features in an Italian multicentre cohort

Belvisi et al. examined 694 people with Parkinson’s in a report first published April 28, 2022. Coffee drinking in the year before motor symptom onset was recorded by interview. The model associated coffee history with a lower total motor severity score (coefficient −1.383, 95% CI −2.646 to −0.121). Methods identify MDS-UPDRS Part III for motor severity, while the Discussion refers to Part II when interpreting that coefficient. Coffee did not emerge as an association with total NMSS, motor complications or tremor-dominant subtype. The public accepted manuscript does not give a coffee comparison for individual fatigue or a brewing method. [Primary accepted manuscript, Methods and Models 2–6](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10151816/) · [Journal report, PMID 35526494](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35526494/)

## Caffeinated coffee and symptom items in Fox Insight

Gabbert et al.’s June 12, 2023 cross-sectional report drew on 35,959 Fox Insight participants with self-reported Parkinson’s. The regressions used smaller subsets with usable data, including 4,889 to 4,917 in the binary coffee models and 3,848 to 3,868 in the dose models. A coffee drinker reported caffeinated coffee at least weekly for at least six months. The analysis used coffee intake before age at onset, with separate models for any qualifying intake, cups per week, and years of use. The questionnaire did not distinguish brewing methods. Models adjusted for age at examination, sex, and disease duration. The published p-values are exploratory and uncorrected for multiple comparisons. [Primary report, Tables 1–2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-31531-w) · [Local CC BY source text](Source-Text/PMC10260917.txt)

In Table 1, qualifying coffee use was associated with fewer chewing and swallowing problems (β −0.1435, SE 0.0731, p = .0497). The exposure-dose and duration results for that item were inconclusive. None of the three exposure models gave p < .05 for tremor, speech, saliva and drooling, walking and balance, freezing, or difficulty getting up. In Table 2, cups per week was associated with more reports of unexplained pain (β 0.0083, SE 0.0035, p = .0168). The other models for unexplained pain were inconclusive, as were all three exposure models for constipation, memory problems, sadness, anxiety, changed interest in sex, and light-headedness. The supplement confirms that the swallowing item includes pills, meals and texture modification, and that the nonmotor analyses do not measure fatigue. The extra Geriatric Depression Scale item analyses apply to smoking. [Primary supplement, Tables S2–S5 and S12–S15](https://pmc-oa-opendata.s3.amazonaws.com/PMC10260917.1/41598_2023_31531_MOESM1_ESM.pdf)

## Matcha suspension and exercise-related fatigue

Shigeta et al. reported two randomized placebo-controlled trials on July 5, 2023. The first trial randomized 17 healthy young men to a drink containing 1.5 g of suspended matcha powder twice daily or placebo alongside eight weeks of resistance training. Figure 2C reports lower subjective fatigue in the matcha group at week 1 (p < .05), with no significant between-group marker at week 8. The Methods define the 100-mm fatigue rating as taken before exercise at rest. The abstract describes it as fatigue after exercise. The second trial reports stress and training outcomes without another fatigue comparison. The observed week-1 direction is indirect context against `0948`, with a powdered suspension, a training program, and participants without Parkinson’s. [Primary report, Methods and Figure 2C](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12937-023-00859-4) · [Local CC BY source text](Source-Text/PMC10320999.txt)

## Green tea and roasted green tea during mental tasks

Kurosaka et al. reported an acute two-day experiment in 20 healthy men on April 13, 2024. On each day participants drank hot water first and either commercial green tea or roasted green tea (hojicha) second. The drinks were provided in four 50 mL portions at 55 °C. Preparation at brewing is unspecified. Figure 2 and the Results show total standardized Subjective Fatigue Feelings scores rising within both hot-water sessions (p < .001 and p = .001) and during the green-tea session (p = .036). The increase in the roasted-tea session was inconclusive. Supplementary Table 2 covers task performance, workload, flow, and duration judgment. It gives no between-beverage difference in the fatigue change. The within-session rise following both water and unroasted green tea, fixed order, and brief task prevent attribution of the rise to green tea. The May 16 correction reverses two original beverage labels in Figure 3, which covers physiological outcomes. Figure 2 contains the fatigue outcome. [Primary report, Methods, Results, and Figure 2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-59383-y) · [Supplementary tables](https://pmc-oa-opendata.s3.amazonaws.com/PMC11016062.1/41598_2024_59383_MOESM1_ESM.docx) · [Author correction](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-61716-w) · [Local CC BY source text](Source-Text/PMC11016062.txt)

The 2024 article refers to an earlier Japanese article by Tagata et al. for additional analysis of task performance and subjective evaluation. JST dates it September 20, 2023, and the journal verifies pages 1377–1388. The study relationship is likely given the shared team, beverages, outcomes, and explicit citation. The earlier public metadata supplies no full methods or data to verify cohort overlap or fatigue results. A distinct 2025 report used a single day and a fixed sequence of no drink, water, and green tea. [Japanese journal issue](https://www.lifescience.co.jp/yk/yk23/yke2309.html) · [JST bibliographic record](https://jglobal.jst.go.jp/en/detail?JGLOBAL_ID=202302212238051453) · [Later protocol and results](../2025-2029/Food-Experiment-Tea-Followup.md#commercial-green-tea-and-acute-task-fatigue-in-healthy-men)

## Coffee intake and nonmotor outcomes in the Finnish imaging cohort

Saarinen et al. published a Finnish imaging cohort May 20, 2024. The cross-sectional comparison in Table 1 includes 115 unmedicated people with Parkinson’s. Median total NMSS was 34.5 with 0–3 self-reported cups of coffee per day and 43.0 with more than 3 cups per day (p = .50). Median Beck Depression Inventory scores were 6.0 in both groups (p = .45). The paper does not specify the coffee brewing method. In the longitudinal component, total caffeine intake from a 31-product diary showed little association with annual change in total NMSS among participants initially unmedicated (n = 29, Spearman r = −.07, p = .72). The reported sleep and fatigue data are limited to the aggregate nonmotor scale, with no separate fatigue result for `0121`. The public university copy lists CC BY-NC-ND. [Primary report, Tables 1 and 3 and evaluation of caffeine consumption](https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.26957) · [University of Helsinki version and license](https://researchportal.helsinki.fi/en/publications/dietary-caffeine-and-brain-dopaminergic-function-in-parkinson-dis/)

## Longitudinal fatigue in ParkWest

Ongre et al.’s nine-year ParkWest follow-up appeared online on September 13, 2020. It reports Fatigue Severity Scale assessments in 191 people with newly diagnosed Parkinson’s and 170 controls at baseline and then every other year through year nine. The accessible abstract lists clinical correlates and gives no estimate by baseline coffee intake or preparation. The Norwegian National Research Archive record has no attached file. The publisher’s data statement and study access profile direct researchers to request participant data. The full model tables and overlap between the participants with coffee and fatigue data remain unverified. [Publisher abstract and data statement](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ene.14520) · [Norwegian National Research Archive](https://nva.sikt.no/registration/0198cc959ed9-15f2d15d-9304-45d4-99dc-77ffc22e90cf) · [Dataset access profile](https://atlaslongitudinaldatasets.ac.uk/datasets/norwegian-parkwest-study) · [Earlier coffee and fatigue reports](../2010-2014/Food-Experiment-Coffee-Followup.md#coffee-history-and-fatigue-measures-in-parkwest) · [One-year follow-up](../2015-2019/Food-Experiment-Beverage-Fatigue-Followup.md#first-year-fatigue-measures-in-parkwest)

Eidem et al.’s January 20, 2024 report used cerebrospinal fluid from ParkWest participants with low and high FSS. The main text describes 10 people with low fatigue and nine with high fatigue in its proteomic analysis. Wiley lists selected patient characteristics in Supporting Table S1 with a 20-person caption. The available report contains no coffee or caffeine variable, and the supplement link returned HTTP 403. The repository carries the main article under CC BY-NC-ND. ## Caffeinated coffee and fatigue fields in PPMI Online

PPMI Online’s December 15, 2022 version 5.0 questionnaire asks separately about regular caffeinated coffee in the past and present, age at starting or stopping, and cups per week. It provides no options for coffee preparation. The Part Ib fatigue item rates the prior week from 0 to 4 by effect on activities and social participation. Its wording distinguishes fatigue from sleepiness and sadness. PPMI describes an application process for downloading participant data. The public questionnaire establishes the variables for a possible analysis, with the joint sample and result still unknown. [Official questionnaire, printed pp. 10–11 and 56](https://www.ppmi-info.org/sites/default/files/docs/v5.0_AS%20IMPLEMENTED_PPMI%20Online%20PROs_15Dec2022.pdf) · [PPMI download and access](https://www.ppmi-info.org/access-data-specimens/download-data)

## Coffee and first symptom in the Australian Parkinson cohort pilot

Bivol et al.’s February 25, 2022 report analyzes the first 1,532 participants in the Australian Parkinson’s Genetics Study. Table 4 records 1,120 people drinking caffeinated coffee (73.1%). Figure 2 classifies 43 first symptoms in a combined pain and fatigue category. The paper does not cross-tabulate these measures, identify the coffee brewing method or report fatigue severity. The authors list a controlled research request route for participant data. The journal’s license is CC BY-NC. ## Canned coffee and striatal receptor occupancy in Parkinson’s

Ishibashi and colleagues first published this five-person study on January 9, 2022. Each participant had a baseline PET scan after restricting caffeine and a second scan about 45 minutes after drinking commercially canned Suntory Premium Boss coffee. Three participants chose one 185 g can (129.5 mg caffeine) and two chose two cans (259 mg). Mean striatal adenosine A2A receptor occupancy was 54.2% and 65.1%, respectively. The study measures receptor occupancy and blood caffeine after drinking canned coffee. It does not report fatigue, sleepiness or clinical symptom change, and the methods give no information on the product’s brewing process. [Primary report, Methods and Results](https://doi.org/10.1002/mds.28897) · [Local CC BY article text](Source-Text/PMC9306703.txt)

The 2023 Japanese Society of Neurology poster Pj-035-5 reports the same five patients, dose split, serum values and receptor occupancy estimates. It adds no fatigue result. [Society proceedings, printed p. S269](https://www.neurology-jp.org/Journal/public_pdf/63_supplement_04.pdf) · [Meeting dates](https://www.neurology-jp.org/neuro2023/en/general/about.html)

## 2023 registrations for instant coffee reintroduction and withdrawal

The interventional registry entry NCT05927038 and the companion observational entry NCT05927103 first posted July 3, 2023. ## Caffeinated and decaffeinated coffee use around Parkinson diagnosis in UK Biobank

Bouhadoun et al.’s 2024 Movement Disorders congress abstract 301 identifies 3,979 Parkinson cases in UK Biobank and compares their reported coffee intake with matched controls. The analysis groups participants by time relative to diagnosis, from 13 years before to six years after. The authors report a declining trend in coffee-derived caffeine in cases (p = .04), no clear decline in overall daily coffee cups, and a shift toward decaffeinated coffee (p = .02). The analysis groups distinct participants by time relative to diagnosis. It gives no within-person switch estimate, brewing method or fatigue measurement. [Primary congress report and figures](https://www.mdsabstracts.org/abstract/shifting-tides-of-caffeine-evaluating-consumption-patterns-in-parkinsons-disease-progression/)

## Oral green coffee extract in rodent motor assays

Molska and colleagues' November 1, 2021 report tested an orally administered extract of unroasted Coffea arabica seeds in rodent catalepsy and rotation assays. A higher acute dose increased ipsilateral rotations in rats with a medial forebrain bundle lesion versus vehicle (p = .0145). The authors found no between-group rotation difference across four drug-challenge timepoints after repeated post-lesion administration. In the separate striatal-lesion experiment, a side-to-side rotation difference within the vehicle group did not establish a difference between treatment groups. The article uses extract and animal motor outcomes. It gives no brewed-coffee or clinical fatigue estimate for `0121`. The earlier UNIFESP thesis record `W2888399484` has matching author, exposure and assays, while the thesis PDF and experiment-level overlap remain unverified. [Primary article, Methods and Results](https://www.scielo.br/j/aabc/a/3XBk4jnwCzqBwLpXwm3rpMx/) · [Local CC BY article text](Source-Text/2021-Molska-Green-Coffee-Extract.txt) · [Earlier thesis record](https://repositorio.unifesp.br/handle/11600/10623)

## Injected caffeine and grip fatigue in a mouse model

Alves and Aguiar's March 15, 2023 paper tested injected caffeine and haloperidol in a reserpine mouse model. Caffeine alone lengthened grip time versus reserpine, while the significant improvement in the strength-over-time measure occurred in the combination arm and the levodopa arm. The experiment measured physical performance in mice and used isolated caffeine by intraperitoneal injection. The exposure and outcome differ from brewed coffee and fatigue in people with Parkinson disease. [Primary report, Methods and Figure 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10828253/)

## Plant diet quality and separately reported fatigue

Sotos-Prieto and colleagues published this prospective Nurses' Health Study analysis on September 30, 2022, in women aged 60 years and older. Table 3 reports 34,493 fatigue cases. The adjusted fatigue hazard ratio comparing the highest with lowest quintile of the healthful plant-based diet index was 0.79 (95% CI 0.77–0.82). The unhealthful index estimate was 1.16 (1.12–1.20). Both trend p values were less than .001. Supplement Table S1 groups caffeinated or decaffeinated coffee with tea. Table S4 evaluates broad plant or animal food groups against composite frailty. The paper gives fatigue as its own measure for diet patterns and supplies neither a separate coffee–fatigue estimate nor a Parkinson-specific comparison. ## Instant coffee vehicle and attentional tasks in Parkinson disease

Sharma and colleagues’ January 10, 2022 crossover tested 24 Parkinson completers and 42 healthy completers after a seven-day caffeine washout. Both conditions contained instant decaffeinated coffee, with a 100 mg caffeine tablet dissolved in one condition. The published mixed-cohort results include better Stroop accuracy and mixed visual temporal attention effects. The paper and six supplementary tables measure task accuracy and latency. None reports a fatigue severity outcome. This exposure isolates added caffeine in a common instant-coffee vehicle. [Primary article and supplements](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00213-021-06054-9#Sec29) · [Local CC BY original](Source-Text/PMC8799544.txt)
