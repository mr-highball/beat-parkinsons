# Food experiment evidence for celery leaf and spearmint

## 2020 Celery and mint leaves in a Parkinson diet intervention

Appendix A of Hegelmaier and colleagues’ 14-day vegetarian diet lists celery among vegetables and mint leaves among herbs. The menu does not state the celery part or mint species. Six participants received the diet alone and ten also received enemas. The aggregate UPDRS III score improved in both arms (p = .033 and .005). The report supplies no separate speech, bradykinesia, gait, stride, or hand-movement values, and no food-specific exposure measure or comparator. The menu supports an ingredient exposure lead for motor outcomes and flags which identification details are missing. [Primary report, Appendix A and Figure 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7072275/) · [Existing local full text](Source-Text/PMC7072275.txt)

## 2021 Spearmint and green tea extract blend and sleep symptoms

Tubbs and colleagues randomized adults with subclinical sleep complaints to a proprietary water-extracted spearmint and green tea blend or placebo for 30 days. Sleep onset latency and activity-tracker REM sleep, the two primary outcomes, showed no significant group-by-time effects. Table 3 gives a group-by-time coefficient of −1.0 ISI point (95% CI −2.0 to −0.03, p = .044), with the active-arm mean falling from 6.07 to 5.12. Table 2 gives a morning-sleepiness coefficient of −0.06 (95% CI −0.22 to 0.09). Depression and fatigue comparisons in Table 3 also span zero. This gives related directions for 0140, 0373, 0518 and 0923. The intervention combines two extracts and the population did not have Parkinson’s.

The ClinicalTrials.gov results posted July 18, 2024 give corresponding ISI changes of −0.95 for blend and +0.96 for placebo. Its displayed analyses are within-arm paired tests. The 2021 paper’s adjusted mixed-effects comparison uses a different statistical contrast. The 2018 first-posted registry, 2021 publication and 2024 posted results are one trial family. [Primary report, Tables 2–3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8000032/) · [Local article](Source-Text/PMC8000032.txt) · [Official registry results](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03567343?tab=results) · [Local registry results](Source-Text/Trials/NCT03567343-Results.txt)

## 2023 Celery stem and leaf powder and seated blood pressure

Mohsenpour and colleagues used oven-dried celery stems and leaves in 750 mg/day capsules in a double-blind placebo-controlled trial with 36 adults with overweight or obesity and type 2 diabetes. Pressure was measured after seated rest. Between-arm change was not statistically significant for systolic (p = .334) or diastolic pressure (p = .751). No side effects were reported. This gives a controlled comparison for 0578, with mixed plant parts and no standing symptom assessment. [Primary report, Table 5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10494649/) · [Local full text](Source-Text/PMC10494649.txt)

## 2023 Registered Mentha spicata oil in a tuberculosis trial

NCT06157034 lists encapsulated *Mentha spicata* oil and was first posted December 5, 2023, after the listed 2021–2022 study dates. The population had pulmonary tuberculosis and the registry describes gastrointestinal symptoms from treatment. The record contains no posted results. The 2025 publication uses conflicting botanical identifications. ## 2024 Celery decoction and blood pressure in hypertension

Febriza and colleagues examined celery decoction in adults with hypertension and report lower blood pressure after the intervention. The available original abstract does not establish a posture-linked symptom or a Parkinson cohort. This offers a directionally different pressure lead alongside the seated null result from the 2023 celery powder trial. [Primary proceedings article](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0218057)

## 2020 Spearmint extract and alpha-synuclein fibrils in vitro

Ogawa and colleagues compared a water-soluble spearmint extract standardized to 12% rosmarinic acid, isolated rosmarinic acid and untreated purified proteins. Figures 2–4 show reduced alpha-synuclein fibrillation with both preparations in fluorescence and electron microscopy assays. Figure 5 evaluates disassembly of existing fibrils using isolated rosmarinic acid. The study did not administer spearmint to people or animals and measured no motor or nonmotor symptom. It extends the 2016 oil assay with a different extract and an isolated-compound comparison. [Primary report, Methods and Figures 2–5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7696425/) · [Local full text](Source-Text/PMC7696425.txt)

## Celery leaf decoction and reported complaints in hypertension

Badrujamaludin and colleagues' June 2020 nonrandomized comparison tested celery leaf and Indonesian bay leaf decoctions in 11 participants each, all receiving amlodipine. The paper reports no serious complaints related to either decoction and gives dizziness as an example. It gives no event count, severity or relationship to standing. The report prints a final mean diastolic pressure for celery of 84.82 in Table 1 and 84.53 in Table 2. [Original Methods, Tables 1–2 and Discussion](https://ejurnalmalahayati.ac.id/index.php/holistik/article/download/2541/pdf) · [Local factual text](Source-Text/2020-Badrujamaludin-Celery-Decoction-Extract.txt)

## Celery seed extract and the Beck anxiety dizziness item

Shayani Rad and colleagues published online November 5, 2022. The primary abstract describes a randomized crossover in 50 adults with hypertension, using 1.34 g/day of seed extract or placebo during four-week periods alongside usual medications. It reports a favorable change for a dizziness item within the Beck anxiety inventory (p < .001). The accessible report gives no item-level between-arm estimate or link to standing. The preparation was a seed extract. The same trial registration is cited by other blood-pressure reports. [Primary publisher abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10787-022-01083-y) · [Local factual text](Source-Text/2022-Shayani-Rad-Celery-Seed-Dizziness-Extract.txt) · [Earlier official record](../2015-2019/Food-Experiment-Celery-Spearmint.md#registered-celery-seed-extract-and-blood-pressure)

## Spearmint odor recognition in comparative parkinsonism

First online October 6, 2023. Kwak and colleagues enrolled 78 people with Parkinson disease and 31 with drug-induced parkinsonism. The disease groups differed in age. Supplementary Table 4 reports correct spearmint identification of 53.8% in the Parkinson group and 76.9% in the drug-induced group (chi-square p = .038, Holm-adjusted p = .721). The table gives group headers of 78 and 27, but 76.9% cannot be obtained from a whole-number numerator out of 27. It gives no item-level counts or explanation and does not specify age adjustment for the item. Spearmint was a scent in the identification test. The study provides no brewed intake, no oil or leaf preparation and no therapeutic change after exposure. [Original source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10846967/) · [Official Supplementary Table 4](https://pmc-oa-opendata.s3.amazonaws.com/PMC10846967.1/jmd-23105-Supplementary-Table-4.pdf) · [Local text](Source-Text/2023-Kwak-Spearmint-Odor-Parkinson-Comparison.txt)

## 2023 Celery leaf decoction and pain in gout case reports

Hanafi and colleagues published September 1, 2023, an uncontrolled report of two gout patients in Central Java. Table 2 gives pain scores of 5 before and 2 after in each. The preparation used celery leaves and stalks boiled and strained; the report also describes nursing care. Its methods propose seven days, but stated collection dates span June 29–July 1, leaving the post-treatment timing unclear. The patients were not reported to have Parkinson disease and did not eat fresh leaf as the intervention.
