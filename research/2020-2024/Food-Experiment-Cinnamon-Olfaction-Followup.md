# Cinnamon and olfactory testing, 2020–2024

## Cinnamon odor recognition in newly diagnosed Parkinson’s at Tanta

Rashed and colleagues published on July 10, 2020. Table 3 reports correct identification of the cinnamon Sniffin’ Sticks stimulus in 1 of 32 recently diagnosed Parkinson participants (3.1%) and 17 of 24 matched healthy controls (70.8%, p < .001). The test was given in a cross-sectional case-control study, with no cinnamon consumption measure or intervention. [Primary report, Table 3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41983-020-00205-z) · [Local CC BY article text](Source-Text/2020-Rashed-Patterns-Olfactory-Dysfunction.txt#L279)

## Dried cinnamon and a synthetic cinnamon odor in the same study

Baert publicly defended the thesis on May 26, 2021, released the smell-test workbook on August 23, 2021, and published the journal article on April 28, 2022. Chapter 6 and the paper report the same 80 Parkinson and 105 healthy-control Sniffin’ Sticks participants and the same 34 and 53 participant dried-spice subset. The workbook gives 24/80 Parkinson versus 45/105 controls for the synthetic cinnamon pen, which the article reports as nonsignificant. It gives 4/34 versus 26/53 for the dried cinnamon stimulus (Figure 2, p < .001). The investigators put dried spices in covered sample containers and asked participants to smell and name them. The released sheets contain identification, familiarity and pleasantness ratings, clinical variables and nutrition screening. They give no cinnamon intake or effect of eating it on olfactory impairment. ## Cinnamon item in the original six-odor PREDICT-PD model

Bestwick and colleagues published on September 24, 2021. The cross-sectional olfaction model compared 932 Tracking Parkinson’s cases with 887 PREDICT-PD controls. Correct cinnamon odor identification was associated with lower odds of being a Parkinson case in the 16-odor model (OR 0.31, 95% CI 0.21–0.45) and the six strongest odor model (OR 0.21, 95% CI 0.15–0.30). These are model coefficients for a scratch-and-sniff response. The design does not compare ground cinnamon intake or changes in smell. The later PREDICT-PD reports use the short kit in other collection periods, rather than providing independent estimates of cinnamon intake. [Primary report, Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8463675/) · [Local CC BY article text](Source-Text/PMC8463675.txt#L107) · [Later PREDICT-PD study family](../2025-2029/Food-Experiment-Food-Followup.md#cinnamon-as-a-diagnostic-odor-in-predict-pd)

## Taichung odor valence cohort linked to the later item analysis

Chiu and colleagues published on April 2, 2024. They examined 176 Parkinson participants in the October 2016 to April 2022 Taichung database and grouped 35 UPSIT-TC test odors by pleasantness. Grouping included banana in the pleasant set, peach and coconut in the neutral set, and onion in the unpleasant set. Cinnamon was absent from the regional test version. Nineteen participants were excluded from the group comparison. Table 2 reports omnibus p = .003 for rigidity by predominant odor-impairment group. The grouping does not establish an individual odor association or measure eating the named foods. The 2026 individual-item paper explicitly identifies overlapping participants with this 2024 analysis. Both cite approval CE23443C and analyze 176 people, with 96 versus 110 men reported respectively, so identical cohorts cannot be assumed. The 2023 and 2025 repeat-visit analyses cite CE22189B and draw from the same center and dates. Participant-level overlap with those analyses remains unresolved. ## Sage and marjoram in the dried-spice identification task

The original Kruidengeuren worksheet gives correct sage identifications in 2 of 34 people with Parkinson disease and 6 of 53 controls, and correct marjoram identifications in 1 of 34 and 5 of 53, respectively. The journal article reports no clear group difference for either stimulus. This is the same cohort as the dried cinnamon comparison. Participants smelled dried spices and named the stimulus. The study gives no clinical olfactory change after eating ground sage or dried marjoram. [Primary journal article, Figure 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9072051/) · [Primary workbook](https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.16415667) · [Native workbook counts](../../experiments/1000_see_what_sticks_food/followup/coverage-bias-2026-11-15/workbook-recognition.tsv)

## Cinnamon within six-odor training in a diabetes registry

ClinicalTrials.gov first posted NCT06327633 on 25 March 2024. The protocol assigns 60 adults with type 2 diabetes and mild cognitive impairment to training that includes cinnamon and five other inhaled odors, or control. Registered olfactory outcomes use OLFACT threshold, identification and memory measures. The November 2026 query found the study completed without registry results. A 2025 publication closely matches intervention, population, center and sample size, with no verified explicit identifier in its accessible abstract. [Official registration](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06327633) · [Local registry text](Source-Text/Trials/NCT06327633.txt)

## Cinnamon odor in the original simplified diagnostic preprint

The August 2024 Li and colleagues preprint used cinnamon as a scratch-and-sniff or identification stimulus in a seven-odor diagnostic panel. Table 4 reports a pooled AUC of 0.87 (95% CI 0.85–0.90) for Parkinson or dementia with Lewy bodies versus controls across the combined cohorts. The result uses all seven odors and has no cinnamon intake or pre/post treatment comparison. The later peer-reviewed report belongs to the same study family. [Original preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.09.24311696v1) · [Licensed publisher JATS text](Source-Text/2024-Li-Cinnamon-Smell-JATS.txt) · [Original panel and boundary extract](Source-Text/2024-Li-Cinnamon-Smell-Preprint.txt) · [Later report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40268961/)

## Cinnamon and orange odor cues in the Veranopolis protocol

First published December 15, 2021. The Veranópolis community Parkinson protocol specifies 16 odor cues in a standardized smell identification battery, including cinnamon and orange. The cues are smelled during assessment. Other instruments in the schedule address falls and sleep symptoms separately. The protocol provides no consumed cinnamon or orange intervention and no odor-item results. The PROBE-PD registry names the same site and an extended observation period, with precise overlap with this protocol not established. [Related registered cohort](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05677529). ## Veranopolis conference prevalence report and missing odor results

The February 9, 2024 supplement contains abstract 138 describing 3,470 people screened in Veranópolis and an estimated Parkinson prevalence of 1.64% (95% CI 1.25–2.12). The reported age cutoff differs from that in the 2021 protocol. The abstract gives no cinnamon or orange identification result and no food-exposure comparison. Supplement DOI and PMCID identify the collection. Abstract number 138 identifies this presentation. ## Oral ground cinnamon and ratings of meal smell

Moreira and colleagues published this randomized crossover on October 9, 2024. Nineteen men with type 2 diabetes completed meal sessions with ground Cinnamomum cassia in water or capsules and a control meal. After eating, participants rated breakfast-smell pleasantness. Table 3 reports medians of 7.8 for control, 7.3 and 6.9 for the two powder sessions, and 7.3 and 7.9 for the capsule sessions. The authors report all palatability comparisons as p at least .80. The endpoint measures response to the meal and provides no detection threshold, clinical olfactory impairment score or Parkinson comparison. The journal cites ReBEC RBR-98tx28b. Its current registration details were not verified separately in this packet. [Original source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11463819/) · [Local factual text](Source-Text/2024-Moreira-Oral-Cinnamon-Meal-Smell.txt)

## Cinnamon and orange identification in a Turkish Parkinson cohort

The September 2020 report compared 31 people with idiopathic Parkinson disease and 31 controls. The 12-item smell identification method includes cinnamon and orange among the odor cues. Parkinson participants scored lower on the screening test (p<.001). The article reports anosmia in 90% versus 54.8% of controls (p=.005). The study contrasts existing olfaction and examines cognition. Cinnamon and orange were presented as test scents; the design does not assign or measure eating either food.
