Title: Algerian Gastrointestinal Patient Survey: Self-Reported Herbal Infusion Use of Spearmint (Mentha spicata) and Thyme (Thymus vulgaris) for Bloating and Constipation
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10878339/
Source kind: Cross-sectional patient utilization survey
Publication date: 2024
Date basis: Peer-reviewed publication year (Tazi et al. 2024, PMID:38369815, PMC10878339).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Patients frequently self-selected spearmint and thyme infusions for bloating (57%) and constipation (30%) expecting digestive relief, though many reported only temporary relief and lack of physician guidance; uncontrolled survey without objective bowel-frequency measurement.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed open-access patient survey (Tazi et al. 2024, PMID:38369815, PMC10878339; Saida, Algeria, n=160 adult GI patients) inspected verbatim via OA XML during Round 7 review.
Efficacy evidence: Uncontrolled cross-sectional utilization survey in non-PD GI patients; documents real-world patient behavior and symptom targets without establishing causal efficacy.

Survey Context & Verified Quantitative Findings (PMC10878339):
In a 2024 cross-sectional survey of 160 adult patients presenting with functional gastrointestinal symptoms in Saida, Algeria (Tazi et al. 2024, PMID:38369815), medicinal plant infusions were widely self-administered alongside or instead of conventional therapy. The primary GI complaints targeted by respondents were abdominal bloating (57%) and constipation (30%). Among all botanical species cited by patients, Mentha spicata (spearmint) ranked highest with 70 citations (12.2% of botanical mentions), closely followed by Thymus vulgaris (thyme) with 65 citations (11.3% of mentions), typically prepared as hot leaf infusions/decoctions.

Balanced Limitations & Safety Notes:
Respondents frequently relied on family or herbalist recommendations without medical supervision, often described symptomatic relief as temporary or variable, and did not represent a diagnosed Parkinson disease cohort. No blinded comparator, standardized dosing, or validated constipation severity score (such as GSRS or Rome IV stool diaries) was collected.
