Title: Iranian, Mediterranean, and Moroccan traditional medicinal and culinary practices for watercress (Nasturtium officinale) alongside fascioliasis awareness
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8123986/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2021
Date basis: Peer-reviewed open-access ethnopharmacology review publication year (Panahi Kokhdan et al., eCAM 2021, PMC8123986), cross-referencing Klimek-Szczykutowicz et al. 2018 (PMID:29852261), Guarrera et al. 2005 (PMID:15878246), and Geraci et al. 2018 (PMC5813353).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional favorable use for respiratory complaints (bronchitis, asthma), diuresis/renal colic, blood-sugar and blood-pressure support, arthritis, and scurvy, balanced against major foodborne liver-fluke (Fasciola hepatica) hazard from raw wild aquatic watercress; zero traditional Parkinson disease efficacy claim.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed ethnobotanical field surveys and ethnopharmacology reviews inspected during Round 7 review (PMC8123986; PMID:29852261; PMID:15878246; PMC5813353; PMC2870496).
Efficacy evidence: Traditional folk-medicine and food-safety context only; does not establish clinical efficacy for Parkinson motor or non-motor symptoms.

Across Iran, Azerbaijan, Morocco, Sicily, and Southern Italy (Basilicata), fresh and cooked aerial parts of watercress (Nasturtium officinale R. Br., locally known as Alaf-e-Cheshmeh / Bakla in Persian regions and crescione in Italy) are longstanding wild-foraged and cultivated culinary greens used in folk phytotherapy (Panahi Kokhdan et al., 2021, PMC8123986; Klimek-Szczykutowicz et al., 2018, PMID:29852261; Guarrera et al., 2005, PMID:15878246; Geraci et al., 2018, PMC5813353). Documented traditional applications include soothing bronchitis and asthma, promoting diuresis and easing renal colic, addressing scurvy, supporting blood glucose and blood pressure control, and relieving inflammatory joint or rheumatic discomfort (used orally or topically). Critically, epidemiological records from Iran, Turkey, France, Spain, and South America document that eating raw wild or inadequately protected aquatic watercress is the leading global foodborne route for human Fasciola hepatica liver-fluke infection (fascioliasis), causing fever, eosinophilia, abdominal pain, and hepatobiliary injury (Mailles et al., 2006, PMC2870496; Mas-Coma et al., 2018, PMID:29991363; Cicek et al., 2023, PMID:38039011). No traditional Middle Eastern or Mediterranean text documents controlled efficacy of raw or cooked watercress for Parkinson disease.
