Title: Global traditional uses of Syzygium aromaticum (Ayurveda, TCM, Unani, Jamu) and concentrated clove oil toxicity cautions
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32019140/
Source kind: cultural
Publication date: 2020-01-30
Date basis: Peer-reviewed review publication date (Batiha et al., Biomolecules, PMC7072209, January 2020); historical materia medica traditions span centuries across South, Southeast, and East Asia.
Reviewed: 2026-11-25 UTC
Reported direction: mixed (traditional warming/digestive/dental-analgesic uses alongside documented hepatotoxic, coagulopathic, and mucosal-injury hazards of concentrated clove oil)
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed ethnopharmacology and toxicology synthesis.
Efficacy evidence: Traditional materia medica and toxicological case literature; culinary spice tradition does not establish PD clinical efficacy, and topical dental eugenol does not transfer to oral culinary ground clove.

Across Ayurveda (Lavanga), Traditional Chinese Medicine (Ding Xiang), Unani medicine (Qaranfal), and Indonesian Jamu, dried flower buds of Syzygium aromaticum (clove) have historically been used in small culinary or decoction doses as warming digestive carminatives, anti-emetics, breath fresheners, and—specifically as topical clove essential oil—for localized toothache anesthesia. Batiha et al. (2020, Biomolecules, PMC7072209) and clinical toxicity reports (including AlBaazi & Hassoun 2022 documenting transient dysarthria and dysphagia after repeated concentrated clove oil ingestion) sharply separate trace dietary spice consumption from concentrated clove essential oil or pure eugenol, which carry risks of mucosal irritation, hepatotoxicity, central nervous system depression, and antiplatelet bleeding interactions. Traditional uses do not validate safety or efficacy for Parkinson's motor or non-motor symptoms.
