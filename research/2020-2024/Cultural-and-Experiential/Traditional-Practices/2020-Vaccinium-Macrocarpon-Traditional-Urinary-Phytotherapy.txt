Reported direction: Longstanding traditional urinary tract prophylaxis use; commercial supplement potency gaps and warfarin caution noted; no Parkinson clinical efficacy claim
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed pharmacognosy and analytical chemistry literature documenting historical Vaccinium macrocarpon culinary/medicinal traditions and modern dietary-supplement quality discrepancies
Efficacy evidence: Traditional urinary-prophylaxis background only; controlled OAB/LUTS/UTI RCTs are evaluated separately, and neurogenic bladder UTI trials remain null

Community/context: American cranberry (Vaccinium macrocarpon Ait., native to North American peat bogs) has a long indigenous and folk-medicine history as a culinary food and urinary-health remedy, later entering widespread European and North American phytotherapy for preventing recurrent urinary tract infections (Gonzalez de Llano et al., 2020, PMID:32752183; Martau et al., 2023, PMID:36838522).
Preparation, standardization, and safety limits: Culinary cooked cranberry sauces and sweetened dried cranberries differ substantially in A-type proanthocyanidin (PAC) concentration from standardized unsweetened dried cranberry powders (250-500 mg/day) used in urology RCTs. Analytical UHPLC-HRMS testing of 10 commercial U.S. cranberry dietary supplements (Wang et al., 2020, PMID:32863574) found only 4% to 11% of claimed fresh-cranberry phenolic equivalents and 1 adulterated product. Additionally, Parkinson's communities frequently caution about warfarin-cranberry interactions (reflected in a 2009 Parkinson's UK post), although controlled clinical reviews (Talasaz et al., 2025, PMID:39288907) find moderate intake generally safe with warfarin.
Related research: Cross-linked with Cho 2021 (PMID:32945735), Vidlar 2016 (PMID:26049866), Stonehouse 2025 (PMID:39863114), and Cochrane 2023 (PMID:37068952).
