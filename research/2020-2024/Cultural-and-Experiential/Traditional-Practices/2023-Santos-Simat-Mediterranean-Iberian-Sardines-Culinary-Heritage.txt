Across Portugal, Spain, Southern Italy, Greece, Morocco, and the Adriatic coast, the European sardine (Sardina pilchardus) is an iconic small pelagic staple of the UNESCO-recognized Mediterranean Diet and Iberian Atlantic coastal heritage—eaten fresh over charcoal (Portuguese Festas dos Santos Populares sardinhas and Andalusian espetos), oven-baked with olive oil and herbs, or preserved through centuries-old artisanal tinning (conservas) (Santos et al., 2023, PMC10153001; Simat et al., 2020, PMC7404705). Peer-reviewed matrix analyses emphasize that whole sardines deliver highly digestible protein (19-25 g/100 g), EPA/DHA, taurine, arginine, vitamin D, selenium, and bioavailable bone-in calcium with substantially lower methylmercury than large predatory fish (Smid et al., 2026, PMC13512054). Traditional and food-safety literature equally stresses key handling boundaries: rapid cold-chain chilling to prevent scombroid histamine accumulation (Tunc et al., 2025, PMC12249119), avoiding improperly home-marinated jars associated with botulism (Courtot-Melciolle et al., 2023, PMC10571493), recognizing persistent parvalbumin fish allergenicity in canned sardines (Taki et al., 2023, PMC10952748), and accounting for purines and canning sodium. Cultural staple status does not establish clinical PD symptom efficacy.
