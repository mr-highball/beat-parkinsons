Title: Traditional culinary-medicinal uses of Coriandrum sativum leaves versus seeds and OR6A2 sensory aversion
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35279837/
Source kind: cultural
Publication date: 2022-03-01
Date basis: Peer-reviewed ethnobotanical review publication date (Sobhani et al., PMC9314633, March 2022), complemented by 2026 structural olfactory-genetics decoding of OR6A2 (Wang et al., PMID:41570822); claimed historical culinary-medicinal traditions span centuries across Persian, Ayurvedic, Mediterranean, and Mexican foodways.
Reviewed: 2026-11-25 UTC
Reported direction: mixed (digestive/cooling leaf traditions alongside genetically mediated unpleasant soapy taste/smell aversion)
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed ethnobotanical synthesis and human sensory-receptor study.
Efficacy evidence: Traditional and sensory-genetic documentation only; does not establish clinical efficacy or harm for any Parkinson's disease symptom.

Across Persian traditional medicine (Geshniz), Ayurveda (Dhanyaka green herb), and Mexican/Latin American culinary traditions, fresh Coriandrum sativum leaves (cilantro) are treated separately from dried coriander seeds/fruits. Traditional accounts attribute cooling, carminative, appetite-settling, and mild calming uses to fresh herb preparations, while noting starkly different aroma chemistry (medium-chain leaf aldehydes such as (E)-2-decenal and (E)-2-dodecenal rather than seed linalool). Preserving unfavorable and no-benefit perspectives, structural decoding of human olfactory receptor OR6A2 (Wang et al., 2026, Cell, PMID:41570822) explains why a substantial subset of consumers experience fresh cilantro leaves as intensely soapy, pungent, or aversive—limiting palatability regardless of culinary intent. Historical culinary-medicinal traditions and sensory genetics do not constitute clinical evidence for Parkinson's motor or non-motor symptoms.
