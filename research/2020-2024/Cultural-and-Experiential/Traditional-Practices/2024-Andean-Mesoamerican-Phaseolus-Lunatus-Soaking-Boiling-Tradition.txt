Reported direction: Customary food-safety culinary practice: traditional Andean and Mesoamerican preparation of lima beans (pallares / Phaseolus lunatus) mandates prolonged soaking, discarding soak water, and thorough boiling to neutralize cyanogenic glycoside (linamarin/HCN) and lectin toxicity in raw seeds, with no traditional Parkinson symptom efficacy claim.
Authenticity: Traceable peer-reviewed ethnobotanical and food-chemistry literature (Choque-Delgado et al. 2024, PMID:39251475; Popoola et al. 2023, PMC10141332; Kato & Terada 2014, PMID:24990764; Adeparusi 2001, PMID:11379294) inspected during Round 5 review.
Efficacy evidence: Ethnobotanical and essential food-safety context only; documents why raw Phaseolus lunatus is toxic and must be soaked and thoroughly cooked or commercially canned, without supplying clinical efficacy evidence for Parkinson symptoms.

Lima bean (Phaseolus lunatus, known as pallares in coastal Peru and butter bean in Anglophone traditions) was independently domesticated in the Andean region and Mesoamerica and later adopted across parts of West Africa and Asia (Choque-Delgado et al. 2024, PMID:39251475; Popoola et al. 2023, PMC10141332). Because wild and landrace Phaseolus lunatus seeds accumulate the cyanogenic glycoside linamarin (up to 4,950-5,590 ug/g in some butter-bean samples; Kato & Terada 2014, PMID:24990764) alongside lectins and trypsin inhibitors, traditional culinary foodways across Peru, Mesoamerica, and Nigeria universally prescribe prolonged soaking, discarding the soak water, and vigorous open-pot boiling (often with water changes) to leach and volatilize hydrogen cyanide and denature endogenous linamarase and lectins (Adeparusi 2001, PMID:11379294; Ologhobo 1993, PMID:8358638). These traditional culinary methods establish vital preparation safety rather than Parkinson disease therapeutic efficacy.
