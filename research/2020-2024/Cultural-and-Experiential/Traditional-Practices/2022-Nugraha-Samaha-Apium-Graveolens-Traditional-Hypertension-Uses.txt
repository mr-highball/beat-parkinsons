Title: Indonesian Tenggerese, Lebanese, Chinese, and Italian Traditional Uses of Apium graveolens Aerial Stems and Herb
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9693994/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2022
Date basis: Publication year of primary peer-reviewed ethnobotanical review (PMID:36364351 / PMC9693994), integrating Samaha 2019 (PMID:31330767) and Chiocchio 2024 (PMC10936038).
Reviewed: 2026-11-25 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional antihypertensive, diuretic, and digestive herbal/culinary use of celery herb and petioles; explicitly distinguished from celery seed (Karafs) extracts and synthetic NBP.
Authenticity: Traceable to peer-reviewed ethnobotanical surveys (Molecules 2022; Biomolecules 2019; J Ethnobiol Ethnomed 2024).
Efficacy evidence: Traditional practice context only; does not establish clinical Parkinson motor, blood-pressure, or non-motor efficacy for culinary celery stalk.

Multiple regional ethnobotanical traditions record aerial Apium graveolens (celery stems, leaves, and herb) as a dietary and herbal remedy for elevated blood pressure, water retention, and digestion. In Java, Indonesia, Tenggerese traditional healers list A. graveolens among their five most frequently used medicinal plants for hypertension (Nugraha et al., 2022). In Lebanon, herbalists cite indigenous A. graveolens with a high relative frequency of citation (RFC=0.92) for blood pressure management (Samaha et al., 2019), and rural Italian ethnobotany in Bologna documents culinary celery stems and leaves in digestive and diuretic home traditions (Chiocchio et al., 2024), paralleling Chinese culinary Qin Cai practices. These aerial-herb traditions are distinct from Unani/Ayurvedic celery seed (Karafs) extracts and pharmaceutical dl-3-n-butylphthalide (NBP) and do not establish clinical Parkinson efficacy.
