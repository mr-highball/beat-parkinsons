Descended from Mediterranean coastal sea beet (Beta vulgaris subsp. maritima), Swiss chard (Beta vulgaris L. subsp. vulgaris / var. cicla and var. flavescens; known as pazi in Turkey, bietola in Italy, acelga in Spain, and selek in North Africa) has been cultivated for millennia across the Mediterranean basin (Gamba et al., 2021, PMID:32746613; Ninfali & Angelino, 2013, PMID:23751216; Mzoughi et al., 2019, PMID:30884696). Unlike salad greens often eaten raw, Mediterranean and Turkish cuisines predominantly cook or steam Swiss chard—braising leaves and stalks in olive oil (zeytinyagli pazi), stuffing leaves (pazi dolmasi), or simmering in soups—which softens coarse stalks and reduces astringent soluble oxalate when boiled. In Turkish folk medicine, aqueous leaf decoctions and cooked chard have long been used by patients with diabetes as a traditional blood-sugar-lowering remedy, inspiring multiple Turkish rodent streptozotocin-diabetes studies (Bolkent et al., 2000, PMID:11025163; Sener et al., 2002, PMID:12511111), alongside broader Mediterranean folk descriptions as a cooling, gently laxative, and liver-supportive vegetable. Botanically and biochemically, Swiss chard leaves/stalks differ sharply from beetroot roots (Beta vulgaris var. conditiva; Almeida et al., 2025, PMC11859049), and historical Mediterranean culinary use neither evaluates nor establishes efficacy for Parkinson disease symptoms.
