Title: European culinary tradition of steamed and roasted Brussels sprouts and perceived flatulence/GI tolerability

Reported direction: Contextual dietary fiber and culinary practice; notable public perception of flatulence (23% in UK survey) without evidence of worsening Parkinson constipation.

Efficacy evidence: Culinary tradition and population perception survey only; does not establish clinical Parkinson efficacy.

Brussels sprouts (Brassica oleracea var. gemmifera) have been cultivated in northern Europe since at least the 16th century (associated historically with Brussels, Belgium) and are widely prepared by steaming, boiling, or dry-heat roasting to reduce sulfur-bitter volatile notes.

Both culinary guidance and consumer surveys record a recurring tolerability trade-off: while valued for dietary fiber, vitamin K, vitamin C, and glucosinolates, Brussels sprouts are widely perceived to provoke intestinal gas. In a 2025 UK survey of 516 adults (Thomson et al., Eur J Nutr, PMID:40848137), 23% identified Brussels sprouts as causing flatulence (second only to beans at 34%), though most respondents rated flatulence as non-worrying compared with bloating or pain linked to fatty or dairy foods. Separately, a 2022 Swedish older-adult and Parkinson's focus-group study (Forsberg et al.) found high acceptability (71% liking) for Brussels sprouts as bite-sized vegetables when appropriately softened, highlighting texture considerations for dysphagia and manual eating.
