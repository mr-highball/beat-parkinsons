Title: Mediterranean Culinary Ethnobotany of Traditional Sweet Capsicum annuum vs Pungent Chili Peppers
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7278808/
Source kind: Peer-reviewed regional ethnobotanical and traditional-food studies (PMID:32397242, PMC7278808; PMID:40265785, PMC11946690)
Publication date: 2020-05-08
Date basis: Published May 2020 in Antioxidants (PMID:32397242, PMC7278808), supplemented by 2016-2023 Balearic Islands/Ibiza ethnobotanical survey published March 2025 in Plants (PMID:40265785, PMC11946690).
Reviewed: 2026-11-24 UTC
Reported direction: Neutral / culinary-nutraceutical context (documents traditional Mediterranean cultivation and culinary value of non-pungent sweet Capsicum annuum while clearly separating its phytochemical profile from capsaicin-rich hot peppers).
Authenticity: Verified via indexed PubMed/PMC open-access records (Della Valle et al. 2020; Gonzalez et al. 2025).
Efficacy evidence: Low (regional traditional culinary and laboratory phytochemical comparison only; does not establish clinical Parkinson symptom efficacy).

Della Valle et al. (Antioxidants, 2020; PMID:32397242; PMC7278808) examine the traditional Italian Altino sweet pepper (Capsicum annuum L., Abruzzo region) alongside pungent hot peppers grown in the same locality, illustrating that traditional regional foodways deliberately maintain non-pungent sweet cultivars for culinary consumption and mild antioxidant/nutraceutical use rather than the pungent capsaicin-mediated medicinal applications of hot chili peppers. Similarly, a 2016-2023 ethnobotanical field survey of 101 informants in Ibiza/Balearic Islands (Gonzalez et al., Plants 2025; PMID:40265785; PMC11946690) identifies Solanaceae including Capsicum annuum among the core traditional culinary ethnoflora. Neither source documents traditional use of roasted, grilled, or raw sweet bell pepper specifically for Parkinson motor or non-motor symptoms.
