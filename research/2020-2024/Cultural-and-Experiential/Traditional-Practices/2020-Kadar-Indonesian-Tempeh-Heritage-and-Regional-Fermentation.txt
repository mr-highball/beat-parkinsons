Title: Traditional Indonesian tempeh fermentation heritage, regional microbial terroir, and culinary preparations
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7569771/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2020-09-09
Date basis: Peer-reviewed journal electronic publication date (Metabolites, 2020-09-09; PMID:32932879; PMC7569771).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Contextual heritage and food-science record documenting Indonesian soy tempeh as a traditional daily staple since at least the 16th century with major regional microbial and metabolite diversity; no controlled Parkinson symptom claim.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed open-access study (Kadar et al., 2020, PMC7569771, corroborated by Wicaksono et al., 2024, PMC11533015) inspected during Round 7 review.
Efficacy evidence: Traditional food-culture and compositional context only; high regional variability in Rhizopus and lactic-acid bacterial consortia underscores why clinical findings from one artisanal or fortified tempeh batch cannot be assumed universally across cooked tempeh.

Kadar et al. (2020, PMC7569771) document tempe (tempeh) as a traditional Indonesian fermented soybean staple embedded in Javanese culinary culture since at least the 16th century, prepared by soaking, dehulling, boiling, and solid-state fermentation of soybeans with Rhizopus spp. mycelium into a compact cake that is traditionally steamed, simmered, braised, or fried before eating. Companion regional metagenomic research (Wicaksono et al., 2024, PMC11533015) demonstrates strong regional terroir in Indonesian tempeh bacterial communities (10^3 to 10^9 copies/g and 40 to 175 ASVs across producers), while Sjamsuridzal et al. (2021, PMC8671487) document shifts in Rhizopus diversity with commercial starter adoption since the 2000s. This cultural and microbiological diversity explains why bioactive profiles (such as GABA, folate, vitamin B12, and isoflavone aglycones) vary substantially across preparations and highlights that culinary heritage does not substitute for controlled clinical trials in Parkinson's disease.
