Title: Ayurvedic (Jatiphala), Moluccan, and European traditional uses of nutmeg alongside longstanding intoxication cautions
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33987899/
Source kind: Traditional practice
Publication date: 2021-07-01
Date basis: Publication year (2021) of peer-reviewed ethnomedicinal review Barman et al. (PMID:33987899), cross-checked with historical poison-case compilation Cushny (1908) and Bleisch et al. (2026, PMC13206746); publication dates distinguished from centuries-old traditional use.
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional culinary/herbal pinch use for sleep, diarrhea, digestion, and joint aches contrasted with well-documented historical warnings of delirium, anxiety, stupor, and visual hallucinations at excessive doses.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed ethnomedicinal synthesis (Barman et al. 2021, Phytotherapy Research) corroborated by historical clinical pharmacology literature (Cushny 1908) and modern poison-center surveillance.
Efficacy evidence: Traditional pinch-level uses for calming sleep or digestive comfort have not been validated in controlled human PD trials, whereas the traditional and clinical warning against large doses (>=5-15 g) is corroborated by modern multi-center poison-control cohorts.

Peer-reviewed ethnomedicinal reviews (Barman et al. 2021, PMID:33987899) document the centuries-long culinary and traditional medicinal use of true nutmeg seed (Myristica fragrans Houtt., native to the Banda/Moluccan islands; Jatiphala in Ayurveda) across South Asia, Southeast Asia, the Middle East, Africa, and Europe. Traditional preparations employed small pinches of freshly grated or powdered seed for digestive distress, diarrhea, sleep disturbances, and topical or oral remedies for joint and rheumatic pain. At the same time, both folk knowledge and medical records dating back over a century (Cushny 1908; Barman et al. 2021; Bleisch et al. 2026, PMC13206746) consistently recognize nutmeg as a potent psychotomimetic intoxicant when ingested in large quantities, causing agitation, feelings of impending doom, visual hallucinations, dry mouth, and stupor. Distinguishing culinary pinches (<0.5-1 g) from supradietary amounts (>=5-15 g) is essential; traditional calming claims lack controlled human trial validation.
