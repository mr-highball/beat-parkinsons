Title: East Asian traditional culinary daikon radish root versus Traditional Chinese Medicine Raphani Semen (Lai Fu Zi) seed remedies for digestion and constipation
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35580770/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2022
Date basis: Peer-reviewed Journal of Ethnopharmacology publication year (Gao et al. 2022, PMID:35580770), cross-referenced with Sham et al. 2013 (PMC3723324) and Japanese takuan-zuke daikon processing studies (PMID:34730849, 2021).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional East Asian culinary and TCM association of Raphanus sativus with digestion, relieving food stagnation, phlegm-cough, and constipation—crucially distinguishing dried radish seeds (Raphani Semen / Lai Fu Zi) from raw, stewed, or pickled culinary radish taproot (daikon / luobo).
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed ethnopharmacology review (Gao et al. 2022, J Ethnopharmacol, PMID:35580770) inspected via PubMed alongside Sham et al. 2013 (PMC3723324) and Kumakura et al. 2021 (PMID:34730849) during Round 6 review.
Efficacy evidence: Traditional East Asian food-medicine and TCM herbal context only; explicitly warns against conflating multi-herb TCM radish-seed prescriptions (Lai Fu Zi) with raw or roasted culinary radish root in Parkinson disease.

In East Asian food and medical traditions, white winter radish (daikon / luobo, Raphanus sativus L.) is eaten raw grated (daikon-oroshi), simmered, or salt-dried and fermented into traditional Japanese takuan-zuke (PMID:34730849) as an everyday digestive accompaniment to rich or starchy foods. By contrast, Traditional Chinese Medicine materia medica reviews (Gao et al. 2022, PMID:35580770; Sham et al. 2013, PMC3723324) emphasize that formal TCM prescriptions for abdominal distension, food retention, constipation, and phlegm-cough rely specifically on Raphani Semen (Lai Fu Zi)—the dried ripe seeds of R. sativus, often combined in multi-herb formulas such as Atractylodes-Raphanus or Liqi Tongbian rather than culinary raw or roasted radish root. Preserving this plant-organ boundary prevents attributing seed-based TCM herb claims to everyday dietary radish roots.
