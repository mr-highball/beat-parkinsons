Title: East Asian culinary traditions of soybean curd (tofu) vs fermented tofu preparations
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8001704/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2021-03-15
Date basis: Peer-reviewed journal electronic publication date (Molecules, 2021-03-15; PMID:33803982; PMC8001704).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Contextual staple-food tradition across China, Japan, and Okinawa distinguishing ordinary coagulated tofu from fermented tofu varieties (such as Okinawan tofuyo and Huizhou mao-tofu); no controlled Parkinson symptom claim.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed open-access review (Fukami et al., 2021, PMC8001704, cross-referenced with Liu et al. 2004 PMID:15228989 and Wang et al. 2026 PMID:42933304) inspected during Round 7 review.
Efficacy evidence: Cultural and food-processing context only; clarifies culinary preparation heterogeneity (coagulant type, steaming, grilling, baking vs fermentation) without establishing Parkinson clinical efficacy.

East Asian culinary foodways have prepared soybean curd (Chinese doufu, Japanese tofu) for centuries by grinding soaked soybeans into soymilk and coagulating the protein curd with mineral salts such as nigari (magnesium chloride) or gypsum (calcium sulfate), then serving it steamed, simmered, grilled, or baked as a daily dietary protein staple (documented in rural Chinese dietary surveys such as Liu et al. 2004, PMID:15228989, where 95.9% of women consumed soy foods led by tofu, and contemporary structural reviews such as Wang et al. 2026, PMID:42933304). Fukami et al. (2021, PMC8001704) further distinguish ordinary unfermented coagulated tofu from regionally fermented tofu specialties such as Okinawan tofuyo (fermented with Monascus red yeast rice since the 18th century) and Huizhou mao-tofu, underscoring that unfermented tofu differs chemically from fermented soy foods in isoflavone aglycone, peptide, and microbial composition. In modern public-health discussions, traditional culinary tofu has also been contrasted with informal-market food-adulteration concerns (such as formaldehyde preservatives discussed in Indonesian observational memory surveys), reinforcing why culinary context and preparation must be distinguished from controlled clinical outcomes.
