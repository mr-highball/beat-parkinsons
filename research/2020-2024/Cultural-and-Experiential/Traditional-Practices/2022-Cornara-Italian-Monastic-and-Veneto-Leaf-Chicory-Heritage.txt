Title: Italian monastic mixed-salad and Veneto winter-forced radicchio (Cichorium intybus var. foliosum) bitter culinary heritage
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8838144/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2022
Date basis: Peer-reviewed Plants publication date (2022-01-24; PMID:35161282, PMC8838144), referencing 17th-century Italian monastic mixed-salad recipes and Veneto leaf-chicory landrace literature (Ghedina et al. 2015, PMC4696096).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional culinary consumption of raw, blanched, and winter-forced bitter Cichorium intybus leaves in Italian regional diets for bitter flavor balance and general dietary health; no specific neurological or Parkinson symptom claim.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed open-access ethnobotanical and phytochemical study (Cornara et al. 2022, Plants, PMC8838144), corroborated with Veneto Radicchio di Chioggia and Treviso landrace genomic/agronomic documentation (Ghedina et al. 2015, PMC4696096; Lucarini et al. 2012, PMID:22190077).
Efficacy evidence: Historical culinary and ethnobotanical heritage only; supplies no controlled clinical efficacy evidence for raw or grilled radicchio in Parkinson disease.

Cornara et al. (2022, Plants, PMC8838144) document traditional Italian recipes for the insalata dei frati (mixed salad of the Capuchin monks), recorded in Italy since the 17th century, in which bitter Cichorium intybus L. leaves were combined raw or briefly blanched with Plantago coronopus, Rumex acetosa, and Artemisia dracunculus for bitter culinary character and traditional nutritional value. In neighboring Veneto (Treviso, Chioggia, Castelfranco, Verona), farmer-selected landraces of cultivated red-leaf chicory (Cichorium intybus subsp. intybus var. foliosum; Ghedina et al. 2015, PMC4696096) developed specialized winter forcing and blanching methods that moderate leaf bitterness and concentrate red anthocyanin pigments for raw winter salads and grilled dishes. Both sources explicitly distinguish leafy culinary radicchio from industrial root chicory grown for inulin extraction or roasted coffee substitutes. No controlled human Parkinson outcomes are documented.
