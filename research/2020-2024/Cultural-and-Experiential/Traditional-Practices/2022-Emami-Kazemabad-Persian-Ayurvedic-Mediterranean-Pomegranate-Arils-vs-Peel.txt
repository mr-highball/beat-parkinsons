Across classical Persian medicine, Ayurveda, and Mediterranean folk foodways, Punica granatum L. (pomegranate) is widely documented as both a staple culinary fruit and a traditional botanical remedy (Emami Kazemabad et al., 2022, PMC9476556; Zarfeshany et al., 2014, PMC4007340). Crucially, traditional pharmacopeias strictly differentiate plant parts and taste profiles: fresh sweet pomegranate arils (the juicy, anthocyanin-rich pulp enclosing edible fibrous seeds) are traditionally consumed raw as a cooling restorative food and gentle stomachic/cardiac tonic, whereas dried bitter pomegranate peel (pericarp/rind, heavily enriched in hydrolyzable punicalagin tannins) is specifically decocted as a strong astringent for diarrhea, dysentery, abdominal pain, and bleeding. This traditional plant-part distinction directly informs modern Parkinson evidence appraisal: peel-extract capsules or astringent rind powders cannot be equated with eating raw culinary arils (which supply intact seed fiber alongside far lower punicalagin concentrations than industrial peel extracts). Historical references to pomegranate as a general brain or heart tonic in Persian and Ayurvedic texts do not identify modern Parkinson disease diagnoses or provide controlled efficacy evidence.
