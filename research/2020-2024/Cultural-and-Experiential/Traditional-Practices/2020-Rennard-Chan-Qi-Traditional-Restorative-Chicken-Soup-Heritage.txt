Title: Cross-cultural restorative chicken soup, postpartum poultry broth, and East Asian chicken essence traditions

Reported direction: Traditional restorative, anti-fatigue, and convalescent culinary use; does not test Parkinson's disease clinical symptoms.

Efficacy evidence: Traditional and cultural dietary heritage only; does not establish clinical efficacy for roasted, baked, or poached chicken in Parkinson's disease.

Across Mediterranean, Jewish, European, and East Asian foodways, slow-simmered or poached chicken broths and stewed poultry dishes have long been prepared as restorative convalescent foods during illness, respiratory recovery, and postpartum recuperation. Rennard, Kalil, and Casaburi (Chest 2020; PMID:32387517; PMC7201233) trace the medical-culinary lineage of chicken soup back to twelfth-century physician Moses Maimonides and 'Jewish penicillin' folk medicine, noting its enduring role as a comforting, easily digested restorative meal rather than a proven disease-modifying therapy.

In East Asian dietary heritage, Chan et al. (Eur J Clin Nutr 2000; PMID:11083489) documented widespread postpartum consumption of simmered chicken soups and ginger-vinegar poultry preparations among Hong Kong Chinese women to restore physical vigor and mood, while Qi et al. (J Food Sci 2017; PMID:28732107) characterized the traditional Chinese stewed yellow-feather chicken soup matrix from which modern commercial Essence of Chicken evolved. In Round 3 evidence synthesis, this cross-cultural tradition explains why aqueous chicken extracts have been studied for mood, fatigue, and working memory, while underscoring that historical culinary use differs from roasted or baked solid chicken meat and does not establish clinical Parkinson's disease efficacy.
