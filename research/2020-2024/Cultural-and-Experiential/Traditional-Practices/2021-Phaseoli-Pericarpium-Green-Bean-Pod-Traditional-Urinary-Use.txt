Title: European pharmacopeial and folk tradition of Phaseoli pericarpium (green bean pods) for urinary and metabolic support
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33746003/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2021
Date basis: Peer-reviewed ethnopharmacology publication year documenting European pharmacopeial history since 1608.
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional herbal teas of dried or boiled Phaseolus vulgaris bean pods have been used since 1608 as mild diuretics and folk kidney-stone or glycemic infusions; culinary green bean pods carry no controlled Parkinson symptom claim.
Authenticity: Traceable peer-reviewed ethnopharmacology and historical review records (Popowski et al. 2021, J Ethnopharmacol, PMID:33746003; Helmstadter 2010, J Med Food, PMID:20132042; Noorulla et al. 2024, Heliyon, PMC10943400).
Efficacy evidence: Traditional herbal and culinary context only; does not establish clinical efficacy or safety for Parkinson urinary urgency or motor symptoms.

European herbal and pharmacopeial textbooks record Phaseoli pericarpium (common bean pods, Phaseolus vulgaris) as a traditional mild diuretic tea since 1608 and as a folk antidiabetic infusion throughout the 19th and 20th centuries (Popowski et al. 2021, PMID:33746003; Helmstadter 2010, PMID:20132042). Parallel regional traditions in South Asia, East Africa, and the Middle East also describe boiled fresh green bean pod infusions as folk remedies for kidney stones (Noorulla et al. 2024, PMC10943400; Jalal et al. 2020, PMC7692137), and Indonesian rural foodways include fresh green beans in traditional dietary management of diabetes (Tjokroprawiro et al. 1983, PMID:6393443). Distinction: Traditional pharmacopeial uses involve aqueous decoctions or dried pod teas targeting renal stone flushing or blood sugar rather than steamed culinary green beans for Parkinson neurogenic bladder urgency or neurological symptoms.
