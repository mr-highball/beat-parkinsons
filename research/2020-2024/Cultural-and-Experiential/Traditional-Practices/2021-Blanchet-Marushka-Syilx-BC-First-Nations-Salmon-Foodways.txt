Title: Syilx Okanagan Nation and British Columbia First Nations Pacific salmon traditional foodways and well-being
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8283975/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2021
Date basis: Peer-reviewed publication years (Blanchet et al., BMC Public Health 2021, PMID:34271895, PMC8283975; Marushka et al., Public Health Nutr 2018, PMID:30215341, PMC10260770); Indigenous salmon foodways span thousands of years.
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Favorable cultural, nutritional, and community well-being association for traditional Pacific salmon harvesting and consumption; no traditional clinical Parkinson's disease symptom claim.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed community-partnered public health surveys inspected during Round 6 review (PMID:34271895, PMC8283975; PMID:30215341, PMC10260770).
Efficacy evidence: Indigenous cultural, nutritional, and food-sovereignty evidence only; distinguishes holistic cultural well-being from controlled clinical Parkinson's disease symptom trials.

For the Syilx Okanagan Nation and First Nations communities across British Columbia and the Pacific Northwest, Pacific salmon (Oncorhynchus spp.) is a foundational traditional food carrying deep nutritional, ceremonial, ecological, and spiritual significance (Blanchet et al., 2021, PMC8283975; Marushka et al., 2018, PMC10260770). In the First Nations Food, Nutrition and Environment Study (n=1,103 across 21 BC communities), salmon was the leading traditional seafood source of dietary EPA, DHA, vitamin D, and high-quality protein while methylmercury exposures remained generally low compared with top marine predators. A 2021 Syilx-led survey further reported that engagement with Sockeye salmon restoration and harvesting initiatives was positively associated with cultural connectedness and overall well-being. Similarly, across Nordic and North Atlantic coastal cuisines, poached, baked, and grilled Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) is a long-standing seasonal staple. Traditional food-sovereignty and wellness value does not constitute controlled clinical evidence for Parkinson's disease motor or non-motor symptoms.
