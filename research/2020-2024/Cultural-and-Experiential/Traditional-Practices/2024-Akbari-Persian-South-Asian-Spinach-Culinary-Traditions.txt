Title: Traditional Persian (Esfenaj) and South Asian (Palak) culinary-medicinal traditions of cooked spinach and calcium-paired oxalate mitigation

Date basis: Peer-reviewed review publication year (Akbari et al., Curr Drug Discov Technol 2024, PMID:38549540), cross-referencing Chai & Liebman 2005 (PMID:15826055), Brogren & Savage 2003 (PMID:12810415), and Yalcin Celiktas et al. 2026 (PMC13585253).

Reported direction: Favorable traditional humoral description as cooling, nutrient-dense, and mildly laxative (folk uses for constipation, back/joint pain, insomnia, inflammation, urinary discomfort), paired with culinary cooking/dairy practices that lower oxalate absorption; no controlled Parkinson disease claim.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed ethnomedicine and food-chemistry publications inspected during Round 7 review (PMID:38549540; PMID:15826055; PMID:12810415; PMC13585253).
Efficacy evidence: Traditional dietary and humoral classification only; does not establish clinical efficacy in Parkinson disease and highlights preparation-dependent oxalate differences.

Spinach (Spinacia oleracea L.), native to ancient Persia (Esfenaj), holds a prominent place in Traditional Persian Medicine and South Asian cuisine (Palak) as a cooked leafy staple (Akbari et al., 2024, PMID:38549540). Classical Persian medical texts characterize spinach as cold-moist, rapidly digested, and gently laxative, citing traditional applications for constipation, sore throat, inflammatory swelling, back and joint pain, insomnia, and urinary discomfort, though modern authors emphasize that controlled human trials for these symptom claims remain absent. Traditional culinary pairings across Persian (borani-ye esfenaj with yogurt) and Indian (palak paneer with fresh cheese) kitchens align closely with modern oxalate food chemistry: boiling spinach and discarding the cooking water removes 30-87% of soluble oxalate compared with 5-53% during steaming (Chai & Liebman, 2005, PMID:15826055), while co-ingesting calcium-rich dairy products binds intestinal oxalate and significantly lowers urinary oxalate bioavailability in humans (Brogren & Savage, 2003, PMID:12810415). Conversely, modern raw or blended spinach smoothies concentrate soluble oxalate without calcium binding and have been linked to kidney stones and acute oxalate nephropathy (Samra & Gupta, 2024, PMC11414766). Traditional culinary esteem does not establish Parkinson disease motor or non-motor efficacy.
