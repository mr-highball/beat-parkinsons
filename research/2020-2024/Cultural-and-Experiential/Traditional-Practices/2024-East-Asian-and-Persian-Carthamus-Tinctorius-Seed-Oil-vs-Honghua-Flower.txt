Title: East Asian and Persian traditional differentiation between Carthamus tinctorius florets (Honghua) and safflower seed oil
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11682395/
Source kind: Ethnopharmacological and phytochemical boundary record
Publication date: 2024
Date basis: Publication year of Jaradat et al. 2024 (Scientific Reports, PMID:39732883, PMC11682395), cross-referenced with Ruyvaran et al. 2022 (PMID:34487844) and Syrian ethnomedicinal survey (PMC8231352).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional Chinese, Korean, Japanese, Arabian, and Persian medicine distinguishes water-soluble flower remedies (Honghua for blood stasis and pain) from lipophilic seed oil (culinary, metabolic, topical/rheumatic folk uses); no verified clinical Parkinson symptom claim for seed oil.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed ethnopharmacological and analytical publication (Jaradat et al., Sci Rep 2024; PMID:39732883, PMC11682395) cross-referenced with Ruyvaran et al. 2022 (J Ethnopharmacol, PMID:34487844) and local Syrian record (PMC8231352).
Efficacy evidence: Traditional herbal and plant-part context only; prevents erroneous transfer of water-soluble floret flavonoid/HSYA findings (e.g., PMID:35940347, PMID:38822547) to culinary safflower seed oil.

Title and source: Traditional medical distinction between Carthamus tinctorius L. dried florets (TCM Honghua) and expressed safflower seed oil across Chinese, Korean, Japanese, Arabian, and Persian traditions, inspected via Jaradat et al. 2024 (Sci Rep 14:31206; PMID:39732883, PMC11682395) and Ruyvaran et al. 2022 (PMID:34487844).
Community/context: Across East Asian TCM and Perso-Arabic/Unani pharmacopoeias, Carthamus tinctorius florets (rich in water-soluble quinochalcone pigments such as hydroxysafflor yellow A [HSYA] and flavonoid glycosides) are used internally in decoctions to invigorate blood circulation and relieve blood-stasis pain, whereas the expressed seed oil (~80% linoleic acid and ~11% oleic acid in linoleic varieties, with fat-soluble tocopherols and phytosterols) is used as a culinary oil, metabolic/cardiovascular folk remedy, and topical or internal preparation for joint/rheumatic complaints (consistent with central Syrian Table 2 records in PMC8231352).
Exposure and reported symptom: Clearly separates culinary cold-pressed or refined seed oil from flower pigment extracts (HSYA/safflower flavonoids) that have been studied in rodent Parkinson models.
Evidence and limits: Documents historical regional practice and essential phytochemical plant-part boundaries without establishing human clinical efficacy for Parkinson motor or non-motor symptoms.
