Title: South American and tropical culinary-medicinal traditions of Ananas comosus pineapple fruit and enzymatic distinction from stem bromelain
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11243481/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2024
Date basis: Peer-reviewed open-access review publication year (Kansakar et al., Nutrients 2024, PMC11243481; Ramli et al., Food Chem 2018, PMID:30381175); indigenous South American traditions predate European contact.
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional digestive aid (post-meal fresh fruit/juice for heavy digestion), appetite support, and topical wound/swelling poultice; no traditional Parkinson clinical efficacy claim, and thermal grilling denatures proteolytic fruit bromelain.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed open-access biomedical and food-chemistry literature inspected during Round 6 review (PMC11243481; PMID:30381175; PMC10974198).
Efficacy evidence: Traditional culinary-medicinal context only; underscores matrix separation between raw culinary fruit, heat-denatured grilled pineapple, and concentrated industrial stem bromelain.

Native to South America (Brazil, Paraguay, and northern Argentina), pineapple (Ananas comosus L., Bromeliaceae) was traditionally employed by indigenous South American and later tropical Asian communities as a fresh digestive aid after heavy meals, an appetite tonic, a mild diuretic beverage, and a topical fruit-pulp poultice for minor skin wounds and localized swelling (Kansakar et al., 2024, PMC11243481). Modern food biochemistry attributes proteolytic digestive and tenderizing activity in raw pineapple to cysteine proteases, while demonstrating two major evidence boundaries: (1) commercial pharmaceutical bromelain tablets tested in clinical trials are predominantly purified stem bromelain from inedible pineapple stems, structurally distinct from culinary fruit bromelain (Ramli et al., 2018, PMID:30381175), and (2) proteolytic bromelain undergoes irreversible thermal inactivation at cooking/pasteurization temperatures (Colletti et al., 2024, PMC10974198), meaning grilled or cooked pineapple lacks active bromelain. Traditional usage supplies no controlled clinical efficacy evidence for Parkinson disease.
