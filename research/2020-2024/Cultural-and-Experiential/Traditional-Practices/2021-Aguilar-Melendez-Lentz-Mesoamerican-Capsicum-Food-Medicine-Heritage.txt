Title: Mesoamerican Capsicum spp. food-medicine continuum and Maya ceremonial chili pepper heritage
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8535541/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2021-10-19
Date basis: Peer-reviewed ethnobotanical publication date (Aguilar-Melendez et al., Foods 2021, PMID:34681551, PMC8535541), corroborated by 2,000-year Maya ceremonial archaeobotany (Lentz et al. 2024, PMID:38669253, PMC11051596) and Capsicum pharmacognosy review (Mandal et al. 2023, PMID:36255140).
Reviewed: 2026-11-25 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional culinary, ceremonial, digestive, warming, and analgesic food-medicine practices; does not establish clinical efficacy in Parkinson's disease.
Authenticity: Traceable peer-reviewed ethnobotanical and archaeobotanical records (PMID:34681551 / PMC8535541; PMID:38669253 / PMC11051596; PMID:36255140).
Efficacy evidence: Ethnobotanical and historical heritage only; distinguishes claimed millennia-scale Mesoamerican use from modern clinical evidence and does not establish Parkinson's disease efficacy.

Pungent Capsicum peppers (C. annuum, C. frutescens, and allied domesticated taxa) occupy a central food-medicine continuum across Mesoamerica and global spicy cuisines. Aguilar-Melendez et al. (Foods 2021; PMID:34681551; PMC8535541) documented traditional culinary and ethnomedicinal uses of chili peppers across 67 Indigenous and Afro-descendant cultural groups in Mexico, including warming digestive tonics, appetite stimulation, respiratory clearing, and topical or internal pain remedies.

Archaeobotanical environmental DNA from a 2,000-year-old Maya ritual deposit at Yaxnohcah (Lentz et al., PLoS One 2024; PMID:38669253; PMC11051596) further identified Capsicum alongside ceremonial botanicals, while Mandal et al. (Phytother Res 2023; PMID:36255140) reviewed traditional South Asian and global medicinal uses of pungent Capsicum fruits. Distinguishing publication date (2021-2024) from ancient archaeological age (~2,000 years BP), these traditions provide rich cultural context for oral ground cayenne without establishing clinical efficacy or safety for Parkinson's disease motor or non-motor symptoms.
