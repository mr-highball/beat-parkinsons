Reported direction: Traditional culinary and folk uses for aches and cooking, strictly distinguished from the 1981 aniline-adulterated industrial rapeseed oil poisoning epidemic.

Efficacy evidence: Ethnobotanical and historical toxicological context only; does not establish clinical Parkinson efficacy or harm of food-grade canola oil.

Brassica napus (rapeseed; low-erucic-acid cultivars bred as canola oil since the 1970s) has traditional culinary and ethnomedicinal uses across South and Central Asia and Europe. Rahman et al. (2020, PMC7508056) document folk uses of B. napus seeds, leaves, and oil for minor aches, coughs, and digestive complaints, while Sabbah et al. (2024, PMC11479033) document traditional Khowar culinary oil heritage in northern Pakistan.

In public memory and occasional online discussions, rapeseed/canola oil is sometimes conflated with the 1981 Spanish Toxic Oil Syndrome (TOS) outbreak, which caused severe chronic neuromuscular, cognitive, and depressive sequelae (Polentinos-Castro et al., 2021, PMID:33820907; Benito-Leon, 2025, PMID:41040954). Historical epidemiology established that TOS resulted from criminal resale of industrial rapeseed oil denatured with 2% aniline (forming toxic fatty-acid anilides), NOT from food-grade culinary canola oil.
