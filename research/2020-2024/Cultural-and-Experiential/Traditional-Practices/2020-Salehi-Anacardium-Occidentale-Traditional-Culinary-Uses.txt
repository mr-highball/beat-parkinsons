Title: Traditional culinary and medicinal distinctions across Anacardium occidentale (cashew) plant parts
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7303264/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2020
Date basis: Peer-reviewed ethnopharmacological review publication year (Frontiers in Endocrinology 2020; PMID:32595597, PMC7303264), cross-referencing Behravan et al. (2012; PMID:22186317).
Reviewed: 2026-11-25 UTC
Reported direction: Customary culinary nourishment for roasted cashew kernels and traditional Iranian analgesic attribution for cashew nut core; distinct non-kernel uses of caustic shell liquid, leaves, bark, and cashew apple.
Authenticity: Traceable peer-reviewed open-access ethnopharmacological review (Salehi et al. 2020, PMC7303264) and Iranian traditional-medicine pharmacology report (Behravan et al. 2012, PMID:22186317).
Efficacy evidence: Traditional and culinary context only; does not establish clinical Parkinson efficacy for roasted cashew nuts.

Ethnobotanical reviews of Anacardium occidentale document centuries of culinary and traditional medicinal use across tropical South America (especially northeastern Brazil), West Africa, India, and Iran. Crucially, traditional sources separate the edible cashew kernel—which must be heat-treated/roasted and shelled to remove caustic cashew nut shell liquid (CNSL) rich in anacardic acids and cardol—from non-kernel tissues. Roasted kernels serve widely as a nutrient-dense snack, paste, and culinary thickener, while traditional Iranian medicine also cites the cashew fruit core (nut) for pain management (Behravan et al., 2012, PMID:22186317). By contrast, traditional antidiarrheal, astringent, anti-inflammatory, and hypotensive decoctions predominantly use stem bark, leaves, or cashew apple pseudofruit rather than roasted kernels. None of these traditional accounts assess diagnosed Parkinson's disease symptoms.
