# Ground sumac and symptom outcomes

## 2022 Volatiles in Sicilian sumac samples

Arena and colleagues profiled six *Rhus coriaria* preparations and identified 263 volatile compounds. Their comparisons cover fruit ripeness, storage and processing. The later Parkinson fibroblast paper uses this report for prior chemical characterization. The assays do not establish olfactory impairment or improvement in people after eating sumac. [Local licensed primary text](Source-Text/PMC8912007.txt) · [Primary article](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27051727)

## 2022 Sumac fruit extract in fibroblasts from Parkinson patients

Isgrò and colleagues applied a phenolic sumac extract to cultured skin fibroblasts from three people with early-onset Parkinson disease and PARK2 variants. Hydrogen peroxide assay results fell after extract exposure in the patient cell cultures. ATP responses differed across donors, and total ATP decreased under one treatment condition in donor 3. Figure 1 reports lower MTT viability in cells from two donors at the higher concentrations, with no significant difference for the third donor or healthy control. Table 2 records donor-level disease severity for characterization. The participants did not eat the extract and the study did not measure treatment changes in their facial expression, gait, dystonia, OFF duration or any other symptom. [Local licensed primary article, Figures 1, 3 and 7 and Table 2](Source-Text/PMC9655332.txt) · [Publisher](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms232112774)

## 2022 Sumac extract and isolates from urinary infections

Marouf and colleagues tested hydroalcoholic *Rhus coriaria* extract against bacterial isolates from urinary infections. The investigators reported reduced growth and adhesion in laboratory assays. The study treated isolated organisms and did not give sumac to the patients from whom the isolates came. It supplies no urgency event counts or urinary result in Parkinson disease. [Primary report](https://doi.org/10.7324/JAPS.2022.120522) · [Publisher full text](https://japsonline.com/admin/php/uploads/3612_pdf.pdf)

## 2022 Sumac and pomegranate in a COVID-19 symptom trial

Forouzanfar and colleagues randomized 182 COVID-19 outpatients to standard treatment with or without commercial sumac and pomegranate juice. The report says 178 completed the trial. Table 4 combines smell and taste disorders into one binary item. In men, 38 of 68 reported the item before and 17 of 68 after the combined intervention. The comparison group went from 25 of 52 to 21 of 51. The study found no significant post-treatment difference between groups for the combined item in either sex. That analysis differs from the abstract's description of a significant comparative reduction. The study cannot isolate the effect of sumac or establish a change in olfactory impairment in Parkinson disease. [Local licensed primary article, Results and Table 4](Source-Text/PMC9729036.txt) · [Primary article](https://doi.org/10.1155/2022/6850342)

## 2023 Culinary brown sumac and aroma liking

Soleymani Majd and colleagues compared butternut squash soup with no sumac or 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75% or 1% commercial Iranian brown *Rhus coriaria* added before serving. Forty healthy older and younger adults took part in the sensory crossover. The authors did not detect a difference in aroma liking attributable to adding sumac. Figure 2 and Section 3.3 report a difference in perceived brown color among younger participants. The study excluded conditions or medication affecting sensory perception and rated liking of the soup without a clinical measure of odor threshold, identification or change in olfactory impairment. The authors identify NCT05534152 as the retrospective registration for the separate food-intake phase. The lead author's doctoral thesis is dated June 2021 and covers the sensory and appetite project. [Local licensed primary article, Sections 2.3 and 3.3](Source-Text/PMC10345699.txt) · [Primary article](https://doi.org/10.1002/fsn3.3369) · [Original doctoral thesis](https://radar.brookes.ac.uk/radar/file/fd1f75aa-5ce5-4d0d-8ad6-c66fef279fea/1/SoleymaniMajd2021SumacAppetite.pdf)

## 2023 Sumac extract and mouse open-field locomotion

Parizad and Abbasi Maleki reported less immobility on a tail-suspension test after injected sumac extract. Table 1 and the Results subsection report no significant change in open-field crossings (p = .168) or rearings (p = .197). These results do not measure stride length, gait speed, dystonia or Parkinson medication-state duration. [Existing local study annotation](Food-Experiment-Sumac-Cardamom.md#2023-sumac-fruit-extract-and-tail-suspension-in-mice) · [Primary report, Section 4.2 and Table 1](https://brieflands.com/journals/jjnpp/articles/139330)

## 2024 Sumac fruit infusion in a voice-remedy survey

Jarrar, Alatrash and Amer surveyed 100 herbal medicine practitioners in the West Bank. Table 2 of the first public version identifies *Rhus coriaria* fruit, voucher Pharm-PCT-2037. Fifty-six respondents cited an infusion in the voice-disorder category. The study measures reported use by practitioners and documents its preparation. It supplies no patient response, speech intelligibility score, direction of effect, or ground-spice Parkinson comparison. The preprint posted February 5 and the journal article describes the same survey. [Licensed preprint, Table 2](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-3906517/v1) · [Final primary article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eujim.2024.102374)

## Sumac in an olfactory test familiarity questionnaire

2021-11-17. Kamrava and colleagues surveyed familiarity with candidate odor descriptors for the Iranian Sniffin’ Sticks identification test. Table 1 gives sumac a 57% familiarity rating in the 200-person survey, below the study’s selection threshold. It was excluded from the final identification odors. No participant consumed sumac as a test exposure, and the result does not measure a change in smell. [Primary report and Table 1](https://doi.org/10.47176/mjiri.35.153)

## Generic dizziness in the combined sumac and pomegranate trial

Table 4 reports nonspecific dizziness in acute-illness outpatients. Among men, the combination arm changed from 55/68 to 1/68, and standard care from 33/52 to 3/51. The table prints 8.5% beside 17 of 19 women and a post-treatment control denominator of three despite 39 at baseline. It gives no posture trigger, separate sumac-only contrast, or Parkinson comparison. Recovery and baseline differences complicate the trends. [Local article, Table 4](Source-Text/PMC9729036.txt) · [Published original](https://doi.org/10.1155/2022/6850342)

### Primary-table provenance

Table 4 gives mean BDI-II change −10.41 (SD 11.08) with sumac fruit powder and −9.74 (SD 8.50) with placebo, between-group p=.78. Both groups followed calorie restriction. The trial does not establish clinical equivalence or estimate Parkinson-specific depression.
