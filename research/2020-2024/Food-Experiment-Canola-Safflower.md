# Canola and safflower oil evidence

## Cold-pressed safflower seed oil and blood pressure

Ruyvaran and colleagues first published their randomized double-blind trial on September 4, 2021. They compared cold-pressed safflower seed oil softgels with placebo in 67 participants with metabolic syndrome. Mean systolic changes were −8.80 and −2.26 mmHg (p = .021). Mean diastolic changes were −3.53 and −0.70 mmHg (p = .041). The primary preview identifies the cold-press process. Neither an orthostatic symptom rating nor a Parkinson cohort is reported. [Primary abstract and preparation methods](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378874121008199)

## Canola oil and pain in osteoarthritis

Davis and colleagues’ 2021 feasibility trial randomized 30 adults with knee or hand osteoarthritis to liquid canola oil, light olive oil or fish oil capsules. Nine were assigned canola oil. The abstract reports no clear group difference in secondary outcomes, which included visual analog pain scores, at 12 weeks. The canola refining grade and numerical pain contrast were unreported in the abstract. [Primary congress abstract P49](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/imj.15302)

## Cooking oil exposures in the Tabriz-approved Parkinson reports

Kheirouri, Alizadeh and Keramati’s oil article was first published November 23, 2023. The abstract compares 120 Parkinson participants with 50 healthy controls and reports 20% higher odds of existing Parkinson disease per unit of non-hydrogenated plant-oil use (p = .001). It reports within-Parkinson correlations for animal oil and butter. The abstract does not name canola or safflower as individual analysis variables, specify processing grades or report item-level associations. The publisher full report and exact instrument remain accession leads. OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar mark the article closed and gave no repository copy.

Two accessible 2023 reports show where to trace the instrument. The July micronutrient paper has the same three authors and the same 120/50 counts, an interviewer-administered 147-item food-frequency questionnaire, and a clinician-administered MDS-UPDRS. The November dietary-pattern paper names the same counts, Isfahan recruitment, grant 66548 and ethics approval IR.TBZMED.REC.1400.076. Its scale description includes all four MDS-UPDRS parts. Matching counts, recruitment and methods suggest probable cohort overlap, pending confirmation from the oil paper. The diet-pattern and micronutrient reports give no item-level estimate for canola or safflower oil. [Oil paper primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37997257/) · [Micronutrient full text](Source-Text/PMC10377174.txt) · [Dietary-pattern full text](Source-Text/PMC10867496.txt)

## Canola oil and simulated bread bolus texture

Ikegaya’s October 2023 bench study mixed bread crumbs and artificial saliva, then compared water with emulsions containing canola oil and soy lecithin. Canola-containing preparations decreased hardness, cohesiveness and adhesiveness relative to water alone. No participants swallowed the mixtures. The result concerns the physical properties of a simulated food bolus. [Primary paper, Figure 3](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/cookeryscience/56/5/56_199/_article/-char/en)

## Safflower comparator in a Glut1 movement disorder trial

De Giorgis and colleagues first published their double-blind crossover in May 2024. The randomized maintenance-period median frequency of paroxysmal movement events was 14.3 per four weeks on triheptanoin and 11.8 on the safflower-oil comparator, with estimated difference 1.46 (95% CI −1.12 to 4.36). The endpoint grouped disabling events in Glut1 deficiency syndrome. The official results posted in 2020 record constipation in 1 of 42 during the blinded safflower-oil period and 1 of 43 during the blinded triheptanoin period. The trial gives no untreated oil comparison, Parkinson dystonia or constipation severity result, or oil refining grade.
