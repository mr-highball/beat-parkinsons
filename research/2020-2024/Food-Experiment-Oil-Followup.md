# Edible oil evidence follow-up, 2020–2024

## Registered ketogenic crossover metabolic report and supplement schema

Iacovides and colleagues published a metabolic and thyroid report on June 3, 2022 (PMID 35658056, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0269440). The PACTR identifier, randomized diet sequences and 11-person completion sample link it to the 2019 report on cognition, sleep and mood. The 2022 original provides thyroid and metabolic outcomes. Its separate ethics protocol supplement refers to fatigue in background about an earlier study and names sleep, mood and exercise among the planned daily measures. The raw data archive contains two workbooks. A schema and string check found sleep quality, morning vigilance and mood headings, with no visible named coconut or fatigue field. It did not assess unpublished diaries or establish that a food was consumed. [2022 primary paper](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0269440) · [Ethics protocol supplement](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0269440.s004) · [Supporting data listing](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0269440.s005) · [2019 cognitive outcomes](../2015-2019/Food-Experiment-Coconut-Ketogenic-Crossover.md)

## Registration for the coconut-oil vehicle in the PPI-1011 safety study

NCT05969977 first posted August 1, 2023. It describes a first-in-human, randomized healthy-volunteer drug tolerability study. The record was completed and had no posted results when checked November 17, 2026. The 2025 original publication provides the vehicle and fatigue adverse-event details, with coconut oil used in both assigned groups. [Official registration](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05969977) · [Dated primary results](../2025-2029/Food-Experiment-Oil-Followup.md#coconut-oil-vehicle-and-reported-fatigue-in-healthy-volunteers)

## Oral olive oil alone in a MPTP mouse study

The 2023 study of orally administered fullerene dissolved in olive oil included a separate group of MPTP mice given olive oil alone by gavage. In the coadministration experiment, the olive-oil-only group showed no clear change on the pole or rotarod comparisons with the MPTP model control, whose saline was administered intraperitoneally. The study used ten mice per group and did not specify the oil's processing grade. These motor proxies and unequal administration routes limit its relevance to cold-pressed olive oil and Parkinson’s bradykinesia (`0629`). [Primary report, Figures 4 and S5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10526674/)

## Olive oil items in the MVP diet pattern analysis

Supplementary Table S4 of the 2022 MVP report analyzed 1,205 people reporting Parkinson’s or other parkinsonism. The Mediterranean screener's main culinary fat item was associated with −46.0 total PRO-PD points for olive oil (95% CI −112 to 19.7, reported p = .7). The item for more than four tablespoons daily had an estimate of −7.65 (95% CI −79.3 to 64.0, p = .9). The MIND olive-oil item had an estimate of −52.7 (95% CI −117 to 11.7, p = .6). All three intervals cross zero. Table 2 reports individual symptoms only for overall dietary scores. The olive-oil contrasts do not resolve preparation or any individual frozen endpoint (`0001`, `0072`, `0343`, `0629`, `0897`, `0939`). [Primary report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9739738/) · [NLM supplementary archive, Table S4 on PDF page 3](https://pmc-oa-opendata.s3.amazonaws.com/PMC9739738.1/nutrients-14-05185-s001.zip)

## Coconut oil and composite Parkinson's symptom scores

Farahnik and colleagues analyzed 1,084 participants with self-reported idiopathic Parkinson’s in the MVP 2021 cohort. Self-reported coconut oil supplement use was associated with −153 total PRO-PD points (95% CI −239 to −67) after adjustment for age, sex, income and years since diagnosis. The contrast was observational and cross-sectional. It did not identify oil grade or provide a separate postural-instability estimate for refined coconut oil (`0110`). An earlier MVP analysis associated teaspoon-based coconut oil frequency with −20.2 points (95% CI −31.0 to −9.4), while the earlier study's binary coconut oil supplement estimate crossed zero. The studies are from the same project and use different exposure definitions. [2023 primary analysis, Figure 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9966010/) · [2017 primary analysis, Tables 3 and 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5610862/)

## Coconut oil in both diets of an MPTP mouse study

Zhang and colleagues reported on July 17, 2023 that mice receiving a ketogenic chow performed better than control-diet MPTP mice on grip strength, open-field distance and speed, rotarod, grasping and pole descent. Table 1 lists 20 g of coconut oil in the control formula and 381 g in the ketogenic formula, with 25 g of soybean oil in each. The Methods describe coconut oil as the main fat in both and give macronutrient percentages for the ketogenic diet that differ from Table 1. Both descriptions indicate substantially more fat and less carbohydrate in the ketogenic diet. The motor assays concern the broader diet in a toxin model, without an isolated culinary coconut oil comparison or a dyskinesia or fatigue estimate. [Primary report, Figure 1 and Methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10352270/) · [Local full text, Table 1](Source-Text/PMC10352270.txt#L158)

## Extra-virgin olive oil and olive phenolics in mild cognitive impairment

The 2020 Greek MICOIL pilot compared two extra-virgin olive oils of different phenolic content, each supplied alongside Mediterranean diet instructions, with the instructions alone. The report analyzed 50 participants at 12 months. Both oil groups had more favorable ADAS-Cog changes than the diet-only group in exploratory pairwise tests. The protocol excluded Parkinson’s and severe depression or anxiety. The methods list the Neuropsychiatric Inventory and Geriatric Depression Scale, without a reported separate anxiety or apathy estimate. Subsequent MICOIL biomarker analyses overlap the cohort. [Primary pilot report, Table 4](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/JAD-200405) · [Full primary report, Methods and Tables 3–4](https://cleanthilife.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/jad-78-jad200405.pdf) · [Follow-up biomarker report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34748951/)

The 2024 MeDi-SUPOL registration compares two dietary patterns with or without an oleocanthal-rich olive polyphenol capsule. It explicitly excludes Parkinson’s disease, measures cognition and daily function and includes a secondary depression measure. It listed recruiting with no posted results on November 12, 2026, with primary completion estimated for September 2029. The supplement, diagnosis and outcomes leave the frozen cold-pressed olive oil and anxiety (`0343`) and refined olive oil and apathy (`0939`) claims unresolved. [Registration first posted November 26, 2024, NCT06705738](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06705738) · [Later Greek meeting abstracts](../2025-2029/Food-Experiment-Oil-Followup.md#greek-olive-phenolic-protocols-in-mild-cognitive-impairment)

## Sesame oil carrier in a randomized cannabinoid trial

Liu and colleagues randomized 61 people with Parkinson’s disease to a cannabinoid preparation or placebo. Both solutions contained sesame oil. The placebo used US Pharmacopeia-grade oil with color and strawberry flavor. The reported between-group difference in change on the Neuro-QOL anxiety T-score was 1.35 points (95% CI −0.65 to 3.36). The corresponding pain-intensity difference was −0.26 points (95% CI −1.05 to 0.53). These estimates compare the cannabinoid and placebo formulations. The study gives no independent oil effect or cold-pressed or toasted oil grade for `0039` or `0144`. The skin analysis used the same trial and assessed seborrheic dermatitis. [Primary report, PMID 38487964, Intervention and Table 2](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mds.29768?af=R) · [Related skin report and local text, PMID 38466972](Source-Text/PMC10964137.txt) · [2018 registration](../2015-2019/Source-Text/Trials/NCT03582137.txt)

## Attention in the Colorado cannabinoid trial

Domen and colleagues published cognitive results for NCT03582137 on May 22, 2023. The cognitive tables include 29 participants per arm. The placebo and CBD/THC formulations both contained sesame oil, and the paper gives no processing grade that establishes toasted oil exposure. The unadjusted animal verbal fluency comparison gave p = .109. After adjustment for age and education, the article reports a difference in change of −2.29 words for CBD/THC (p = .040). The publisher HTML displays the upper 95% confidence limit as positive 0.11, which conflicts with the reported p-value. The NMSS item on sustaining concentration during activities worsened with CBD/THC (p = .009), and decreased concentration was reported by 32.3% of 31 participants who took CBD/THC and 13.3% of 30 who took placebo. These concentration findings are related to `0528`. Both groups received the oil, so the trial supplies no independent sesame oil estimate. [Primary report, PMID 37212386, Methods, Table 2 and Results](https://doi.org/10.1002/mds.29447)

## Sesame oil in the earlier Colorado open-label cannabidiol trial

In the earlier NCT02818777 study, Leehey and colleagues administered Epidiolex, a highly purified CBD formulation in an oral sesame oil solution. Their full report first appeared online on March 19, 2020. The study treated 13 people with Parkinson’s disease, with 10 completing the efficacy assessment. Participants took a mean maximum oil volume of 16 mL/day (range 5.5–34.5). Eight participants reported fatigue and three reported abdominal pain as adverse events. These reports concern the combined formulation and provide related evidence for `0404` and `0144`. The pilot had no control group, and the article gives no oil processing grade. The 2017 interim and 2019 conference reports belong to the same pilot. [Full primary report, PMID 33381646, Methods and Tables 2 and 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7759259/) · [2017 interim result and 2019 conference report](../2015-2019/Food-Experiment-Oil-Followup.md#interim-results-of-the-colorado-open-label-cannabidiol-study)

## Corn oil capsules in cannabidiol trials of tremor and sleep

In 2020, de Faria and colleagues reported a crossover of a single cannabidiol dose and placebo in 24 people with Parkinson’s. The publication reports smaller anxiety responses and tremor amplitude during a public-speaking challenge with cannabidiol. An archived review identifies corn oil as the carrier in both arms. This comparison cannot estimate the effect of corn oil, and the tremor outcome describes a provoked response for purposes of `0438`. [Primary report, PMID 31909680](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31909680/) · [Archived review, clinical trial table](Source-Text/PMC11524397.txt)

In 2021, de Almeida and colleagues reported a randomized trial involving 33 people with Parkinson’s and REM sleep behavior disorder. The registration identifies corn oil capsules as the placebo, and the thesis describes their use. The study found no clear difference in its primary REM sleep behavior outcomes and a temporary sleep-satisfaction difference favoring cannabidiol. The published Supplementary Table S5 file was inaccessible. The investigator’s thesis reports mean Epworth Sleepiness Scale scores in Table 9. The cannabidiol and corn-oil placebo groups scored 13.41 and 10.27 at baseline, 11.35 and 8.87 at week 4 (p = .242), 11.06 and 7.94 at week 8 (p = .167), and 10.69 and 9.38 at week 12 (p = .553). The sample contributing scores varied by visit. These are comparisons between the assigned arms at each visit. They give no isolated refined corn oil estimate for `0308`. The REM sleep behavior and sleep-satisfaction outcomes leave the insomnia claim `0726` open. [Primary report and supplements, PMID 33754375](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mds.28577) · [ReBEC registration](https://ensaiosclinicos.gov.br/rg/RBR-5fwhf7) · [Investigator’s thesis, Table 9, printed pp. 65–66](https://teses.usp.br/teses/disponiveis/17/17140/tde-05072021-154535/publico/CARLOSMAURICIOOLIVEIRADEALMEIDAco.pdf)

## Grapeseed oil in a topical Gua Sha lotion

Xu and colleagues randomized 56 people with Parkinson’s disease and pain to Gua Sha with a topical lotion or no added treatment. The lotion contained grapeseed oil, mineral oil, and *Artemisia argyi* oil. After treatment, the adjusted between-group difference on the King’s Parkinson’s Disease Pain Scale was −10.93 points (95% CI −14.56 to −7.29), favoring the combined treatment. The corresponding differences for HADS anxiety and depression were −3.79 and −4.79 points. The trial does not isolate the lotion from the procedure or report grapeseed oil processing. This supplies indirect context for [0338, cold-pressed grapeseed oil and pain](../../experiments/1000_see_what_sticks_food/statements/0338.txt), [0467, refined grapeseed oil and anxiety](../../experiments/1000_see_what_sticks_food/statements/0467.txt), and [0169, refined grapeseed oil and depressive symptoms](../../experiments/1000_see_what_sticks_food/statements/0169.txt). [Primary report, PMID 38962174, Methods and Table 2](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.2147/IJGM.S461958)

## Olive oil vehicle in a cannabinoid trial

The Buriram trial randomized 40 people with Parkinson’s disease to sublingual cannabinoid extract or placebo. Both groups received an olive oil vehicle and 36 participants were analyzed. The report found no clear between-group difference in HADS anxiety, gait velocity, or motor examination scores. The randomized contrast identifies effects of adding cannabinoids to the vehicle. It gives no independent olive oil estimate or processing grade for `0343` or `0570`. [Primary report, PMID 36743777, Methods and Results](https://ruralneuropractice.com/cannabidiol-enriched-cannabis-extraction-product-in-parkinsons-disease-a-randomized-double-blind-and-placebo-controlled-trial-in-buriram-hospital/)

## Coconut oil as a reported OFF-period coping strategy

Mantri and colleagues analyzed responses from 2,110 Fox Insight participants with Parkinson’s disease who reported OFF periods. Coconut oil appears among examples in six responses grouped as supplements in the coping-strategies table. Fatigue was separately coded as a perceived trigger of OFF periods. The article supplies no oil-specific response count, preparation, or comparison between coconut oil use and fatigue severity for `0229`. [Primary report, PMID 34322575, Tables 2–3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8297496/)

## Virgin olive oil as the active comparison

Pantzaris and colleagues randomized 40 people with early Parkinson’s to a fatty-acid and antioxidant formula or pure virgin olive oil over 30 months. The primary report favors the combination formula on overall UPDRS outcomes. The design estimates the difference between the combination and virgin olive oil. There was no untreated or oil-free comparison, and the abstract does not give preparation-specific effects on individual symptoms. The trial documents exposure to an olive oil in a Parkinson’s clinical study and cannot assign the observed group difference to harm from the oil. [Primary report, PMID 34607196](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clineuro.2021.106954)

## Medium-chain triglycerides in a ketogenic feeding study

Choi and colleagues randomized 16 people with Parkinson’s to a ketogenic diet with emulsified medium-chain triglycerides or a standard diet for a week, followed by an open-label extension. Fifteen completed the study. Day-seven Timed Up and Go and the UPDRS comparisons did not clearly differ between groups. The exploratory three-week observations suggested improvement in aggregate nonmotor symptoms, but sleep or fatigue domain scores worsened in at least three participants in each group. The investigators stopped enrollment at their planned interim analysis after the mobility comparison failed their continuation criterion. This intervention changes the entire diet and uses a fractionated triglyceride preparation. It supplies related mobility and fatigue evidence for coconut oil hypotheses. [Primary report, PMID 38561682](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-024-03603-5) · [Local study registration](Source-Text/Trials/NCT04584346.txt)

Table 6 also reports daily dyskinesia and apathy ratings without clear group differences. The authors report higher daily off-time scores in the standard-diet arm. Off time was coded in ordinal bands, and the standard-diet arm already had a higher mean score at the regular-diet day-one assessment, 1.22 versus 0.50. The Results text and the table note give different p-values for the comparison. Both groups followed the ketogenic diet during the open-label extension. The daily log adds symptom-level evidence for the whole-diet comparison and leaves the effect of refined coconut oil unresolved. [Local primary full text, Table 6](Source-Text/PMC10983636.txt#L309)

## Plasma fatty acids and motor and nonmotor symptoms

A 2021 analysis compared 38 people with early Parkinson’s with 33 unaffected controls. In the Parkinson’s group, plasma alpha-linolenic and linoleic acids correlated with lower motor examination scores. Plasma DHA and arachidonic acid correlated with higher nonmotor scores. Dietary estimates did not show a significant relationship with the corresponding plasma values in the Parkinson’s group. The biomarkers, symptom composites, and cross-sectional design leave the effects of consuming specific oils unresolved. [Local full text](Source-Text/PMC8203700.txt) · [Primary report and Table 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8203700/)

Table 4 also reports the Wexner constipation score for the Parkinson’s group. Plasma alpha-linolenic acid showed no clear association with the score after adjustment for age (Spearman ρ = −.101, p = .552) or sex (ρ = −.191, p = .257). Plasma fatty acid is a related biomarker for the cold-pressed flaxseed oil and constipation claim `0470`. The study did not compare flaxseed oil intake. [Local primary full text, Table 4](Source-Text/PMC8203700.txt#L181)

## Reported olive and coconut oil intake and composite symptoms

The MVP investigators’ 2024 report analyzed baseline food frequency and total PRO-PD scores. Its two displayed models each associated higher olive and coconut oil frequency with lower composite symptom scores. In the second model the reported coefficients were −31.4 points for olive oil (95% CI −44.7 to −18.1) and −20.2 for coconut oil (95% CI −31.0 to −9.4) per frequency step. The cohort overlaps the earlier MVP paper. The report does not give oil grade or a preparation-specific result on a named symptom. Correlated foods, self-reported outcomes, and baseline-only comparisons limit attribution. [Investigator’s report](https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/14919326-8cbc-4a3e-88d4-0f609019e178/MVP%20Study%2010-Year%20Summary-c634e33.pdf#page=60) · [Earlier MVP paper text](../2015-2019/Source-Text/PMC5610862.txt)

## Injected extra-virgin olive oil and corn oil in an MPTP mouse model

Farfán-García and colleagues first published this experiment on May 1, 2020. Five groups of eight mice included an unlesioned saline control and four MPTP groups given saline, corn oil, extra-virgin olive oil or levodopa by injection. The olive oil group showed more spontaneous movements and longer rotarod retention than the MPTP-plus-saline group from days two through four. The authors report a longer grasp time on days two and three. The corn oil arm showed no clear improvement in those outcomes relative to saline. The paper mentions acute tremor after MPTP but gives no comparative tremor outcome for either oil. Figures 1–3 give comparisons against saline and no direct olive-versus-corn effect estimate. [Primary paper, Methods and Figures 1–3](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2397847320922939)

Rotarod retention involves balance, coordination and strength. Open-field movement counts cover overall activity. These findings are indirect for postural instability, walking speed and bradykinesia. The paper specifies extra-virgin olive oil but does not report cold-press status or corn oil refining grade. The separate oral olive oil study reported less clear behavioral comparisons and used a different route for its MPTP-only control. [Oral olive oil comparison](#oral-olive-oil-alone-in-a-mptp-mouse-study)

## Olive oil in the Parkinson constipation pilot

Eight participants with Parkinson disease completed five weeks of Mediterranean diet counseling. Olive oil was encouraged as one component alongside dietary changes such as more vegetables, legumes and nuts. The report leaves the oil grade and individual olive oil intake unreported. Mean GSRS constipation syndrome score was 2.25 ± 0.48 at baseline and 1.54 ± 0.31 at week five (paired p = .04). Weekly stool counts and Bristol form comparisons were inconclusive. The intervention had no concurrent constipation control group.

The original Supplementary Table 4 gives mean estimated oleic acid 31.0 → 53.5 g (p = .026) and fiber 24.8 → 36.2 g (p = .027) from dietary recalls. The nutrient table provides no ingredient-specific dose or grade. The registry was first posted February 22, 2019. It lists permeability as primary and overall gastrointestinal symptoms, stool frequency and form among the secondary measures. The later randomized study used this pilot to plan enrollment. Its between-group constipation comparison was inconclusive (p = .60). [Local primary text and Table 2](Source-Text/PMC8733603-Rusch-Parkinson-Mediterranean-Pilot.txt) · [Supplementary Table 4 source extract](Source-Text/2021-Rusch-Mediterranean-Constipation-Supplement-Extract.txt) · [Local 2019 registration](../2015-2019/Source-Text/Trials/NCT03851861.txt) · [Later randomized study](Source-Text/PMC11396875.txt)

## Coconut oil as a reported OFF period coping strategy

Mantri and colleagues analyzed 2,110 Fox Insight respondents with Parkinson medication OFF periods. Coconut oil is one example in a six-response supplement category among 1,416 free-text coping descriptions. No oil-specific respondent count, grade, amount or change in daily OFF time is reported. In the separate Choi randomized diet study, ordinal OFF means of 0.50 versus 1.22 had the same ordering and magnitudes on regular-diet day one and randomized day seven. The reported p values (.0005 in Results and .00005 in Table 6) and ordinal coding (Tables 3 and 6) differ. The intervention used emulsified MCTs across a whole-diet change. The reported ordinal scores provide no refined coconut-oil-specific effect in hours. [Fox Insight Table 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8297496/) · [Local survey text](Source-Text/2021-Mantri-Coconut-Oil-Off-Periods.txt) · [Choi original Tables 3 and 6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10983636/)

## Public KIM protocol and olive oil guidance

The March 6, 2023 author protocol gives the dietary guidance planned for the KIM Parkinson crossover. The ketogenic Mediterranean phase encouraged olive oil alongside multiple plant foods and offered dietitian-prepared meal plans. It gives no olive-oil processing grade, dose or supply commitment. These are plans from the same registered cohort as the later outcomes. Individual olive intake cannot be measured from the plan. ## Coconut and MCT options in a multiple sclerosis diet trial

Lee and colleagues first reported this 12-week randomized pilot in 2020. Fifteen people with multiple sclerosis were assigned to a modified Paleolithic diet (6), an MCT-based ketogenic diet (5), or usual diet (4); one ketogenic participant lacked sufficient data and 14 were analyzed. Ketogenic guidance allowed coconut oil, MCT oil, coconut milk or combinations; the preparation grade and individual coconut-oil dose were not established. The 21-item Modified Fatigue Impact Scale showed a within-group improvement for the Paleolithic group (p = .02), but not for the ketogenic or control groups (p ≥ .10); the paper reports no 12-week between-group differences. This small trial assessed entire diet patterns in multiple sclerosis, with exploratory alpha of .10 and no multiplicity correction. It does not identify an isolated virgin coconut oil effect on Parkinson fatigue. [Original article, Figure 3 and Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11833548/) · [Trial registration](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01915433)
