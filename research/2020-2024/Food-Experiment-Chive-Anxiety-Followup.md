# Chive anxiety evidence in 2020–2024

## Isolated ferulic acid and maize-bran oligosaccharides after LPS

Deng and colleagues published online on 10 September 2022. They used a lipopolysaccharide inflammatory mouse model and reported attenuation of anxiety-like and depressive-like behavior. The publisher preview distinguishes commercial ferulic acid from separately prepared maize-bran feruloylated oligosaccharides. The public abstract and indexed preview give no extractable anxiety arm estimate or exact locomotor comparison. The publisher PDF and full article returned HTTP 403. Two later reviews point to an elevated plus maze and open field, with a 20 mg/kg ferulic acid arm. Dose schedule, route, control, and effects by arm still need confirmation against the full primary paper.

This is evidence about isolated ferulic acid and a distinct cereal-derived mixture in an inflammatory model. Claim 0789 concerns dried chives and anxiety in Parkinson's disease.

## Oral trans-ferulic acid and dark-compartment residence

In the full open-access 7 September 2023 study, Bhuia and colleagues assigned six groups of Swiss albino mice to oral distilled water, diazepam, one of three trans-ferulic acid doses, or trans-ferulic acid with diazepam. Figure 2C reports longer dark residence time at each tested compound dose than with water. The dark residence means were 118.60, 127.20 and 136.00 seconds at 25, 50 and 75 mg/kg, respectively. Figure 2C shows the low bar as vehicle and the high bar as diazepam, clearing up the apparent transposition in the Results sentence. Locomotor measures also declined. 

The authors interpreted increased dark residence as anxiolytic. The original validation of the light-dark box interprets exploration in the white compartment as the anxiety-related measure and reports that known anxiogenic agents increased black-compartment exploration. These directions and the activity change complicate the anxiety inference from the purified compound experiment. The mice were outside a Parkinson model.

## Ferulic acid nanocapsules and the elevated plus maze

Rossato and colleagues published online on 20 September 2024. They allocated 36 male Wistar rats to four arms of nine for an eight-week D-galactose accelerated-aging model. Gavage arms were saline-model control with water, D-galactose with water, and D-galactose with either aqueous ferulic acid or ferulic acid nanoencapsulation at the same dose of 0.5 mg/kg. Both elevated-plus-maze open-arm time and the anxiety index differed across groups (omnibus p=.0026 and p=.001, respectively). The nanoencapsulated arm had greater open-arm time and a lower anxiety index than D-galactose with water in Figure 4. Figure 4 reports comparisons with saline/water and D-galactose/water. It supplies no nanoencapsulated-versus-free pairwise anxiety contrast. The design has no empty nanocapsule group.

This tests a particular delivery formulation in an accelerated-aging model. Both form-specific effects and the presence or absence of an anxiety effect after dried chive ingestion in Parkinson's remain separate questions.

## Injected ferulic acid and open-arm entries in maternal-separation mice

Lorigooini and colleagues published online on 6 May 2020. The primary Methods specify intraperitoneal dosing. Figure 5A reports that 40 mg/kg ferulic acid increased the percentage of elevated-plus-maze open-arm entries relative to saline in maternal-separation mice (p<.05). The authors and model schedule are distinct from the later Simran oral study. This is an independently observed anxiety-like endpoint in stressed mice under a different administration route.

## Common chive exposure and outcome gaps

Hegelmaier and colleagues included chives in a multicomponent diet for people with Parkinson's disease. The reported longitudinal comparison is motor UPDRS III. The published baseline UPDRS I aggregate gives no follow-up anxiety-specific effect. The indexed tissue chemistry study establishes a potential ingredient constituent, ferulic acid, and does not measure what people absorb from dried chives. No comparison of dried common chive leaves with a measured Parkinson anxiety outcome emerged from this follow-up.
