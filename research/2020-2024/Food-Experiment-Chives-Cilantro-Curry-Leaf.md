# Chives, cilantro and curry leaf evidence

## Chives in a vegetarian Parkinson intervention

Appendix A of Hegelmaier and colleagues’ February 2020 pilot lists chives among the vegetables in a 14-day dietary package. Of 16 participants, six received the diet alone and ten also received bowel cleansing. At one year both groups had lower composite UPDRS III scores than their baselines (reported p = .033 and .005, respectively). All participants received the diet, and the report gives no chive-specific intake or preparation. Gait contributes to the composite motor examination, with no separately reported walking speed, stride, or chive contrast. Bowel cleansing and microbiome measures do not provide a constipation severity estimate after eating chives. [Local primary article, Appendix A and Figure 4](Source-Text/PMC7072275.txt) · [Publisher](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4409/9/2/376)

## Curry leaf aqueous extract in paraquat-exposed rats

Reddy, Dhanapal and Lakshmi compared oral aqueous extract from fresh *Murraya koenigii* leaves in a four-week paraquat model, with six rats per group. Across extract groups, the average time to initiate movement in the tail-hold test ranged from 52.50 to 96.75 seconds, versus 142.25 seconds in the paraquat-only group. Bar-test immobility ranged from 32.00 to 55.25 seconds versus 86.50 seconds. The Methods call the rotarod test a rigidity measure. Table 2 reports falls on the apparatus, a motor performance outcome that does not measure muscle tone. The article uses different significance descriptions in the narrative and table legends. These were toxin-model tests with a leaf extract and no clinical cooked or ground leaf comparison. [Primary report, 2020, Tables 2–5](https://journals.innovareacademics.in/index.php/ajpcr/article/download/38421/23719)

## Cilantro leaf fractions and mouse anxiety tests

Puthallath and colleagues reported elevated plus maze and light–dark box results after giving aqueous and oil fractions from a hydroethanolic cilantro leaf extract to Swiss albino mice. The aqueous fraction gave the strongest reported signal among the fractions, and the rotarod tests did not show a motor coordination deficit with the extracts. The study used concentrated fractions in animals without Parkinsonism. [Primary report, June 26, 2020](https://www.ijbcp.com/index.php/ijbcp/article/view/4164) · [Tables 2–5](https://www.ijbcp.com/index.php/ijbcp/article/download/4164/2918)

## Curry leaf fractions prolonged drug-induced catalepsy

Patil and Langade’s 2021 study used a petroleum ether leaf extract and an alkaloid fraction. Both preparations prolonged bar-test catalepsy after haloperidol, and both reduced apomorphine-associated open-field movement. The authors interpret these results as consistent with reduced dopaminergic activity. The longer catalepsy points in the opposite direction to the shorter catalepsy in the 2020 paraquat study and in the 2012 acute haloperidol assay led by Patil. The 2012 preparation was a methanol extract. The studies used different extracts and protocols. Neither tested cooked or ground leaves in people with Parkinson’s. [Primary report, Figures 3–4](https://jmpas.com/admin/assets/article_issue/1638815868JMPAS_MAY-JUNE_2021.pdf#page=3)

## Coriander fruit extract in MPTP-exposed mice

Koppula, Alluri and Kopalli tested *Coriandrum sativum* fruit extract in MPTP-exposed mice. The day-four rotarod comparison showed no clear benefit. At day 20, the extract groups stayed on the rotarod for 37 to 51 seconds versus 27 seconds with MPTP alone. The paper also reports passive avoidance and open-field measures. These experiments use coriander fruit, often called coriander seed in cooking. They leave walking speed and attention after eating cilantro leaves unresolved. [Local full text, Figures 5–6](Source-Text/PMC8222636.txt) · [Primary report, April 28, 2021](https://www.excli.de/excli/article/download/3668/3784)

## Curry leaf extract and climbing in a fly toxin model

Shabir and colleagues fed rotenone-exposed fruit flies a curry leaf extract alongside the toxin. The paper reports a 50.5% reduction in climbing ability with rotenone alone and a 15% reduction with coexposure to the curry leaf extract. The jump assay also favored the extract arm. These outcomes measure toxin-related fly locomotion. They do not measure human walking, freezing or bradykinesia severity after consuming a culinary preparation. [Local full text, Section 3.4 and Figure 9](Source-Text/PMC9405140.txt) · [Primary report, August 21, 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3921/11/8/1623)

## Coriander seed capsules and anxiety in university students

Alqudah and colleagues’ trial registered on August 21, 2023 after the study’s listed 2022 completion date. The registry records actual enrollment of 86 and specifies dried powdered coriander seeds in capsules versus starch. It lists memory as the primary outcome and anxiety and depression as secondary outcomes at 30 days. The January 16, 2024 journal abstract calls anxiety and depression primary and reports an anxiety result favoring the seed capsules (p = .04). The registry gives no posted arm-level results. This is a human anxiety comparison using seeds in students, with no dried leaf or Parkinson arm. [Registration](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05999071) · [Local selected registry fields](Source-Text/Trials/NCT05999071.txt) · [2024 primary report](https://doi.org/10.1108/nfs-08-2023-0193)

## Curry leaf oil in mouse brain slices

Sireesha and Vidyavathi’s August 16, 2023 paper reports biochemical changes when curry leaf essential oil was applied to rotenone-exposed mouse brain slices, including higher measured dopamine and antioxidant measures compared with toxin alone. It also reports docking predictions. There was no live-animal symptom or behavioral test in this experiment. A separate rat report used commercially sourced isolongifolene, an isolated compound discussed in connection with curry leaf, and measured open-field and rotarod performance. These preparations provide mechanistic context without establishing an effect of eating the leaf. [Primary oil report](https://indjst.org/articles/predicting-antioxidant-neuroprotective-and-toxicity-activity-of-murraya-koenigii-essential-oil-by-in-silico-and-ex-vivo-against-rotenone-induced-model) · [Primary isolongifolene rat report](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0891061820301599)
