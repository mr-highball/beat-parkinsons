Title: Pharmacology of lemongrass (Cymbopogon citratus Stapf) III. Assessment of eventual toxic, hypnotic and anxiolytic effects on humans
Publication: Leite and colleagues, July 1986, Journal of Ethnopharmacology 17(1):75–83
PMID: 2429120
DOI: 10.1016/0378-8741(86)90074-7
Source: https://eurekamag.com/research/001/653/001653609.pdf
Coverage: factual extract of the accessible primary source

Fifty healthy volunteers drank two preparations of Cymbopogon citratus dried leaf tea and a placebo on separate nights. The researchers issued 4 g sachets for 200 mL tea, and one tea condition was identified in advance while the other active tea and placebo were coded. Their Table 3 includes dream recall, nightmares, sleep onset, sleep interruptions and morning experience. They reported no differences across the three conditions, including among seven participants with previous sleep difficulties. Dream recall was a questionnaire response without bed-partner, motor or polysomnographic observation. Separate uncontrolled safety sets documented self-reported somnolence (5/11) and polyuria (4/11) after one dose, and one report of somnolence with dizziness among eight volunteers after daily use. These adverse symptom sets do not measure validated daytime sleepiness, sudden urinary urgency, falls or Parkinson outcomes. Table 3 morning item. The answers that indicated no sleepiness were 34/50 after labeled active tea, 37/50 after placebo, and 33/50 after coded active tea. Hence 16/50, 13/50, and 17/50 reported sleepiness. The authors reported no significant paired difference and did not publish individual transition cells. The acute group took tea in the morning. Results date symptom occurrence to one hour after the test drink, and Methods list questionnaire completion later that day.

The labeled and blinded conditions used the same active leaf preparation in the same 50 people. The matched placebo used extracted leaf material with citral added for flavor. Both active conditions had a higher raw count than placebo. The active infusion was stronger than the customary preparation described by the investigators. The single morning item measures occurrence at waking. The uncontrolled safety reports add acute timing context and do not establish an effect size for sustained daytime sleepiness in Parkinson disease.

Paired-data sensitivity from the coded tea and coded placebo margins. The same 50 people provide 17 and 13 positive answers. If x people reported sleepiness under both, then 17−x reported it only after tea and 13−x only after placebo, with possible x from 0 through 13. The active-minus-placebo net count stays four and the possible discordant totals run from 4 to 30 in increments of two. Two-sided exact McNemar p values computed over all possible tables range from 0.1250 to 0.5847. The paired test statistic and individual transitions cannot be reconstructed from the published marginals. The sensitivity calculation addresses the binary waking item in these healthy adults. It does not quantify Parkinson daytime sleepiness severity or a probability of claim truth.
