Title: The concept of plants as teachers among four mestizo shamans of Iquitos, northeastern Peru
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source kind: Traditional practices, primary ethnography
Publication date: 1984
Reviewed: 2026-11-19
Date basis: Journal of Ethnopharmacology 11(2), July 1984. This dates the account without assigning an origin date to the practice.
Source: https://home.iscte-iul.pt/~fgvs/Luna_plants.pdf
PMID: 6492831
DOI: 10.1016/0378-8741(84)90036-9
Coverage: Original article, pp. 135–156. The underwater-world and plant-teacher passages are on PDF pages 5 and 6–8.

Community and context: The field study describes practices and teachings reported by four healers in the Iquitos area. Spirit encounters, teaching, dizziness and dreams are distinct experiences in the participants' descriptions. The healers disagree on whether every plant teacher produces visions. Some plants are said to evoke visions with ayahuasca, and some are associated only with dreams or dizziness.

Exposure and reported symptom: The independent-use list identifies ojé as Ficus insipida. One practitioner's account associates a different named plant, renaco, with journeys to an underwater world. The author identifies renaco only as Ficus sp. and also lists it among possible ayahuasca additions. The published passage neither assigns the renaco report to Ficus insipida nor identifies it as an observed response to that species' latex.

Reported direction: Unspecified for visual episode frequency. The authors record traditional interpretations and individual accounts of visionary experiences.
Evidence and limits: The interviews document the practice and the distinctions among informants. There is no Parkinson cohort, comparison of dried fig intake, standardized episode count or isolated ojé-latex visual outcome.
Authenticity: Original journal article in a university-hosted copy, with bibliographic identity and inspected passages. Common-name identities have the scope assigned by the original.
Efficacy evidence: Qualitative ethnographic documentation. A spiritual encounter and a clinical waking visual hallucination cannot be identified with each other from these observations.
