# Bay leaf foreign bodies

## Bay leaves removed from the pharynx

In December 1980, A. N. Johns described two endoscopically confirmed bay leaf foreign bodies. A 77-year-old man with a longstanding pharyngeal pouch developed fullness and persistent aching after eating Moroccan stew. The pouch failed to empty. Endoscopy removed the leaf two months later and showed mild mucosal inflammation. A 61-year-old man felt something stick after eating pâté. Plain radiography found no radiopaque object, and endoscopy found a bay leaf in the hypopharynx. The two cases identify retained leaves and do not report ground bay leaf exposure, a Parkinson diagnosis, or a graded swallowing or drooling outcome. [Original case reports, p. 1682](https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/281/6256/1682.1.full.pdf) · [PubMed record, PMID 7470211](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7470211/)

## Drooling during a meal-related bay leaf incident

In an August 12, 1983 letter, Brokaw and Wonnell reported a firsthand episode of apparent pharyngeal lodging during a meal, followed by gagging, drooling, coughing and pain initially associated with swallowing. The public JAMA excerpt ends before the case resolution. A contemporary newspaper column attributes removal of a small bay leaf fragment to the same report. The accessible primary excerpt identifies the incident as accidental bay leaf ingestion and describes acute symptoms without a Parkinson diagnosis, ground preparation, baseline drooling or quantitative drooling measure. The original PDF remains gated. [Original JAMA excerpt, p. 729](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/387627) · [PubMed record, PMID 6348312](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6348312/) · [Contemporary secondary account](https://archive.newportbeachlibrary.org/NBPL/0/edoc/1327014/1983-12-12%20-%20Orange%20Coast%20Pilot.pdf)

## Other 1983 bay leaf correspondence

Panzer's July 8, 1983 letter describes a different firsthand incident with an object lodged in the throat after the first bite of chicken cacciatore. The title attributes the episode to a bay leaf. The public excerpt ends before object verification or recovery. Palin and Richardson's February 11 letter concerns two gastrointestinal complications attributed to a stem of *Laurus nobilis*. The publicly visible first case involves a perforated Meckel diverticulum, and Panzer describes the other case as rectal pain. These reports provide no documented Parkinson exposure or standardized swallowing or drooling assessment. [Panzer original excerpt](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/387309) · [Palin and Richardson original excerpt](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/383187)
