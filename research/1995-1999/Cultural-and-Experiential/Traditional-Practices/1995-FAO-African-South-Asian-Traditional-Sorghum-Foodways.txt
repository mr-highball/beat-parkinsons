Community and cultural context:
Across semi-arid regions of sub-Saharan Africa (Sudan, Ethiopia, Nigeria, Mali, Burkina Faso) and South Asia (Maharashtra, Karnataka, and dryland India, where sorghum is known as jowar), Sorghum bicolor whole grain has served for millennia as a drought-resilient dietary staple.

Traditional preparations and processing wisdom:
FAO Food and Nutrition Series No. 27 (1995) documents diverse culinary preparations of sorghum grain: boiled whole or cracked grain dishes, thick and thin porridges (tuwo, ugali, to), unfermented flatbreads (Indian jowar bhakri / roti), fermented flatbreads (Sudanese kisra, Ethiopian injera), and steamed couscous. Traditional household processing—soaking, wood-ash alkali treatment, germination/malting, and lactic acid fermentation—was developed across communities to soften the pericarp, reduce condensed tannins and phytic acid, improve protein/starch digestibility, and enhance B-vitamin and iron availability.

Nutritional caveats and boundary distinctions:
Historical dietary literature notes that unvaried mono-staple diets dominated by high-leucine, low-available-niacin sorghum without alkali/fermentation or legumes were historically associated with pellagra in undernourished populations (cross-referenced with Wang & Kies 1991, PMID:1839061). Culinary whole sorghum grain is strictly distinct from sweet sorghum stalk syrup/molasses, distilled Chinese sorghum liquor (baijiu), and non-grain herbal extracts such as West African sorghum leaf sheath decoctions.
