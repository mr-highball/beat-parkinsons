Kremer et al. Effects of high-dose fish oil on rheumatoid arthritis after stopping nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs. Arthritis Rheum. 1995;38:1107–1114.
DOI: 10.1002/art.1780380813
Primary source: https://5280functionalmed.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Effects-of-High-Dose-Fish-Oil-on-Rheumatoid-Arthritis-After-Stopping-Nonsteroidal-Antiinflammatory-Drugs-1995.pdf
Coverage: factual selected methods and Tables 2–3, visually verified against printed pp. 1110–1111.

Both groups initially took active diclofenac. Fish oil and corn oil were the two assigned supplement groups. Oil refining is unspecified.

Table 2, end of active diclofenac to first visit on diclofenac placebo (fish n=19, corn n=20). Patient-rated pain mean change ± SEM in corn was +0.58 ± 0.19 (p=.007). Physician-rated pain was +0.47 ± 0.23 (p=.06). The corresponding fish-oil changes were +0.21 ± 0.16 (p=.22) and +0.37 ± 0.14 (p=.02). The p=.02 physician result had previously been transcribed into the corn arm in this catalog. The authors report no clear clinical differences between oils in this interval.

The Table 2 interval-to-fatigue changes are −0.76 ± 0.56 hours for corn (p=.19) and −0.19 ± 0.40 hours for fish (p=.64). Table 3, baseline to end of the eight weeks on diclofenac placebo, gives −0.63 ± 0.46 hours for corn (n=14, p=.20) and +0.23 ± 0.67 hours for fish (n=15, p=.74). These are within-arm timing measures in rheumatoid arthritis. Parkinson fatigue severity and isolated corn-oil effects remain unmeasured.
