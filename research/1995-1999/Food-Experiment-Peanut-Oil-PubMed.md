# Peanut oil in dietary motor tests and allergy challenges

## Dietary peanut oil and two different mouse rotarod tests

Francès and colleagues compared a purified diet with peanut oil as its sole 6% lipid source with a diet containing 2.36% peanut oil and 3.64% rapeseed oil. The diets began in dams before mating and continued in offspring. Processing grade was unspecified. Table 3 shows longer retention for the peanut-only diet on the low apparatus (669.2 versus 74.6 seconds, p < .0002). Retention on the elevated apparatus, grasping and swimming endurance did not differ significantly. The authors treated low-apparatus behavior as dependent on learning or motivation to leave and considered the elevated task with the other neuromuscular outcomes. Replacing rapeseed oil changed the alpha-linolenic acid exposure. The animals did not have a Parkinson model and the study did not assess handwriting. [Author-hosted primary article, Tables 1 and 3](https://www.bourre.fr/pdf/publications_scientifiques/210.pdf) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7475943/)

## Labial peanut oil challenge and pooled hoarseness reports

Rancé and Dutau reported 202 labial challenges in 142 children in 1997. Peanut oil accounted for three positive labial tests. Hoarseness appears among pooled systemic reaction types across all food challenges, with no food-specific hoarseness count. Their 1999 report of 132 children with peanut allergy lists hoarseness in 34.8% of overall cases and two labial oil positives among 50 tested. That report also describes peanut-oil sensitivity in a separate oral-test subgroup. The public abstracts provide no processing grade and no table linking the overall hoarseness tally to an individual oil challenge. The authors and centre overlap and the degree of cohort overlap is unknown. Neither report measured loudness or intelligibility. [1997 primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9260218/) · [1999 primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10093132/)

## French oral peanut oil challenges and individual reactions

Moneret-Vautrin and colleagues reported 14 positive oral peanut-oil provocation tests among 62 participants in a wider cohort of 142 people with peanut allergy. Challenge oil was mixed with apple purée and compared with paraffin oil, with negative placebo challenges reported. Table 2 gives individual signs including asthma, facial urticaria or lip swelling in selected participants. The report separately identifies refined oil for the protein extract used in skin tests, but does not explicitly establish the same commercial grade or provenance for the oral oil. It supplies no acoustic or intelligibility assessment. This is a concurrent allergic population from multiple sites, including authors from the Toulouse reports, so overlapping participants are possible. [Original journal record](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1046/j.1365-2222.1998.00370.x) · [Published paper, Methods and Table 2](https://www.academia.edu/109071421/Food_allergy_to_peanuts_in_France_evaluation_of_142_observations) · [Earlier British crude/refined oil challenge](Food-Experiment-Pumpkin-Peanut-Oil.md#crude-and-refined-peanut-oil-challenges)

## Oral cannabinoid carrier in mouse thermal and writhing pain models

Burstein and colleagues gavaged the synthetic cannabinoid CT3 in peanut oil. The primary abstract reports an active-drug response in the thermal plate test, whereas oral CT3 showed no response in a chemically induced writhing test using peanut oil or the separate surfactant vehicle. Its formalin results concern intravenous CT3, so that part is a different exposure. The abstract gives no standalone oral oil efficacy or preparation grade. The later human neuropathic pain trial used hard gelatin capsules with lactose, which breaks the carrier link to clinical analgesia. [Mouse primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9698045/) · [Human primary report, capsule formulation](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/197396)
