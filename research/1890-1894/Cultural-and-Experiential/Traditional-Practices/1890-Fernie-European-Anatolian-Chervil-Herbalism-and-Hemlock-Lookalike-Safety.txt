In The Hospital (20 September 1890, PMC5236521), physician W. T. Fernie documented centuries-old European culinary and herbal uses of cultivated garden chervil (Anthriscus cerefolium)—a classical French fines herbes garnish and Lenten spring-soup herb traditionally regarded as a mild stomachic, diuretic, and 'blood-cleansing' tonic, historically credited by Gerard and Culpeper with warming digestion and easing sluggish circulation, with no specific claim for shaking palsy or tremor. Modern Anatolian ethnobotany (Solhan, Turkey; Polat et al., 2013, PMID:23770029) similarly records culinary and folk-digestive use of cultivated chervil. Critically, botanical safety advisories such as Penn State Extension (https://extension.psu.edu/is-it-poison-hemlock-or-wild-chervil) warn against confusing garden Anthriscus cerefolium with wild chervil/cow parsley (Anthriscus sylvestris), rough chervil (Chaerophyllum temulum), or lethal poison hemlock (Conium maculatum), while clinical case reporting (Van Der Brempt 2020) notes isolated acute allergic uvular/throat swelling and voice change after chervil soup.
