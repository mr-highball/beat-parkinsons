# Food experiment evidence for green beans, pinto beans, tempeh, and garlic

## Canned pinto beans and reported stool changes

Winham and Hutchins reported weekly gastrointestinal responses from two crossovers and a parallel study on November 21, 2011. The pinto arms in the crossover and parallel studies used the same canned *Phaseolus vulgaris* cultivar preparation. In the crossover, 24% of pinto consumers reported a stool change in the first week, and later stool frequency changes were uncommon. In the parallel study, 8–20% of pinto consumers reported a change across weeks versus one or two people per week on the control soup. Although the questionnaire asked about direction and magnitude, the published parallel results combine changes in frequency and consistency. They cannot determine whether constipation worsened or improved. Neither a home-boiled pinto arm nor a Parkinson population was studied. [Primary article text, Methods and Results](Source-Text/PMC3228670.txt#L101) · [First publication and identifiers](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22104320/)

## Green bean seed assays and a later levodopa citation

Mohseni Mehran and Golshani published a chromatography comparison June 1, 2013. It names fresh and dry sprouted fava beans, green peas and green beans. The Results states that only fava beans were confirmed to contain levodopa and carbidopa under the assay. The article gives detailed numbers for fava extracts and no separate green bean detection limits, cultivar or steamed-pod concentration. The 2019 Rijntjes review and a 2026 review give 0.25% levodopa for common bean. The 2019 reference is a 2007 botanical review, not an identified quantitative cooked green bean study. Its other cultivation reference explicitly studies *Vicia faba*. The concentration claim for green beans remains unverified by these sources. [2013 primary paper, Results](https://jcdr.net/articles/PDF/3072/8%20-%205415_PF1%28M%29_E%28C%29_F%28T%29_PF1%28M%29_PF1%28T%29_FA%28T%29_PF1%28PUH%29_u.pdf) · [2019 review citation](../2015-2019/Source-Text/PMC6875167.txt#L93) · [2007 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3330839/)

The full 2007 Ramya and Thaakur review gives no *Phaseolus vulgaris* row or 0.25% value in its Table 1. Its main source for plant content, Ingle’s 2003 review, also gives no 0.25% result for *Phaseolus vulgaris* and explicitly uses *Faba vulgaris* as a synonym for *Vicia faba*. The 2019 review’s cited trail therefore leaves its 0.25% claim unsupported. A separate 2018 primary paper did report 0.25% in one of 33 dried common-bean seed accessions using a UV color assay. That paper is absent from the 2019 citation and provides no cooked green pod data. A later mass-spectrometry study confirmed low concentrations in four different Italian common-bean ecotypes. ## Cooked bean rotation registration and first public protocol

NCT01661543 first posted August 9, 2012. The completed registration describes the original beans and peas cholesterol comparison. It has no submitted public results table. The 2024 journal report documents the study food rotation, including pinto beans, and reports gastrointestinal questionnaires.
