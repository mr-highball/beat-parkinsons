Title: Unani, Islamic Prophetic Medicine (Habbat al-barakah), and classical Persian Medicine traditions for Nigella sativa
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23646296/
Source kind: Traditional practice
Publication date: 2013-05-01
Date basis: Publication date of peer-reviewed ethnobotanical synthesis Ahmad et al. (May 2013, PMC3642442), corroborated by Iranshahy et al. (2022, PMID:35244296) and Al-Attas (2026, PMID:43069870); classical Islamic/Unani/Persian sources cited within span medieval centuries (e.g., Avicenna 980-1037 CE) and are explicitly distinguished from modern publication dates.
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional customary use for general vitality, headache/back pain, digestive complaints, and classical Persian Medicine dietary regimens for brain-based tremor; not an empirical PD efficacy comparison.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed ethnobotanical and historical reviews indexing classical Arabic, Unani, Siddha, Ayurveda, and Persian Medicine pharmacopeias (Rhazes, Avicenna, Jorjani); modern publication dates kept distinct from classical historical provenance.
Efficacy evidence: Traditional and historical accounts establish long-standing culinary and medicinal use of black seed (shooneez / habbat al-barakah) but carry low evidential weight for clinical efficacy in modern diagnosed Parkinson's disease.

Modern peer-reviewed syntheses (Ahmad et al. 2013, PMC3642442; Iranshahy et al. 2022, PMID:35244296; Al-Attas 2026, PMID:43069870) document the prominent place of Nigella sativa L. (black seed, black cumin, shooneez, Habbat al-barakah) across Unani, Tibb-e-Nabwi (Prophetic Medicine), Ayurveda, Siddha, and classical Persian Medicine. Classical medical authors such as Rhazes (854-925 CE), Avicenna (980-1037 CE), and Jorjani (1042-1136 CE) listed black seed among dietary and pharmacological preparations for general vitality, digestive disorders, headache, joint and back pain, and historical syndromes of brain-based tremor. Traditional culinary intake uses small amounts of ground or lightly toasted whole seed on flatbreads, cheeses, and cooked dishes, distinct from concentrated modern cold-pressed oils or standardized thymoquinone capsules. Publication dates of these reviews (2013-2026) are kept separate from the medieval historical age of the cited treatises. Traditional esteem and cultural continuity inform hypothesis discovery but do not substitute for controlled clinical trials in diagnosed Parkinson's disease.
