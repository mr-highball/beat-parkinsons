Title: Caribbean and Central American traditional uses of allspice (Pimenta dioica) for digestive and pain complaints
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3891794/
Source kind: Traditional-practice scholarly review
Publication date: 2012
Date basis: Peer-reviewed journal publication date (Current Drug Targets 2012; PMID 23140298, PMC3891794); summarizes historical Caribbean, Central American, and 1898 British Pharmacopoeia uses.
Reviewed: 2026-11-24 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional accounts describe allspice tea for dyspepsia, colds, and menstrual cramps and topical crushed berries for bruises, sore joints, and muscle ache, plus historical European dyspepsia/purgative use; no traditional Parkinson's disease claim.
Authenticity: Verified via open-access PubMed Central full text (Zhang & Lokeshwar, Curr Drug Targets 2012; PMC3891794).
Efficacy evidence: Customary ethnobotanical documentation only; traditional digestive or topical pain uses do not establish oral clinical efficacy in Parkinson's disease.

Zhang and Lokeshwar (2012, PMC3891794) review folk-medicine uses of Pimenta dioica (L.) Merr. (allspice / Jamaican pepper) across its native Caribbean and Central American range. Jamaicans traditionally drink hot allspice-berry tea for colds, dysmenorrhea (menstrual cramps), and dyspepsia (upset stomach). Costa Rican folk medicine records allspice for dyspepsia and diabetes, Guatemalan practice applies crushed allspice berries topically to bruises, sore joints, and myalgia (muscle ache), and Cuban traditional medicine uses allspice for digestive complaints and toothache. European anecdotal records mention allspice extracts for dyspepsia and as a purgative, and pimenta oil entered the British Pharmacopoeia of 1898. None of these customary practices describe diagnosed Parkinson's disease or controlled oral symptom outcomes.
