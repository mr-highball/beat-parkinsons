Title: West African Yoruba traditional herbal use of Sorghum bicolor leaf sheaths (poporo / Jobelyn lineage) and botanical separation from culinary sorghum grain
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3904510/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2014
Date basis: Peer-reviewed publication date (Ayuba et al., J Altern Complement Med 2014, PMID:24283768 / PMC3904510) documenting traditional West African herbal usage of Sorghum bicolor leaf sheaths.
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional West African herbal tonic use of boiled crimson Sorghum bicolor leaf sheaths for anemia, general weakness, and vitality; strictly separate plant part from culinary cooked sorghum grain and unproven for Parkinson disease.
Authenticity: Traceable peer-reviewed open-access publication (PMC3904510) inspected during Round 7 review.
Efficacy evidence: Traditional herbal and ethnomedical boundary record only; leaf-sheath preparations do not establish clinical efficacy for culinary boiled or cooked sorghum grain in Parkinson disease.

Community and cultural context:
In southwestern Nigeria and parts of West Africa, deeply pigmented crimson leaf sheaths of specialty dye-type Sorghum bicolor cultivars (known locally among Yoruba communities as poporo or oka-baba leaf sheath) have traditionally been boiled as an aqueous decoction and taken as a blood-building tonic for anemia, fatigue, and convalescence, as well as used as a natural food and textile colorant.

Botanical boundary and commercialized extract distinction:
Modern commercial capsules marketed as Jobelyn (and studied as Sorghum bicolor supplement, SbS, in rodent models of rotenone parkinsonism, haloperidol catalepsy, inflammatory pain, and forced-swim immobility, e.g., PMID:40767921, PMID:37347426, PMID:24598834, PMID:24002928, and in Nigerian HIV/sickle-cell cohorts, PMC3904510, NCT01704794) are standardized extracts of dried Sorghum bicolor LEAF SHEATHS containing concentrated 3-deoxyanthocyanidins (apigeninidin, luteolinidin) and dimeric flavones.

Evidence limits:
Recording this ethnomedical lineage prevents category error: leaf-sheath herbal decoctions and commercial Jobelyn capsules represent a completely different botanical organ and phytochemical dose than boiled or cooked culinary sorghum grain eaten as a staple cereal.
