Title: Traditional East Asian and Eurasian folk use of Cannabis sativa seed oil for inflammatory joint pain and skin dryness
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24814038/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2014
Date basis: Peer-reviewed ethnopharmacology publication date (Jeong et al., 2014, Journal of Ethnopharmacology); cross-referenced with Abbasifard et al. (2025) and Farinon et al. (2020).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional folk attribution of anti-inflammatory, joint-soothing, and skin-emollient benefit; no historical Parkinson disease motor claim.
Authenticity: Traceable peer-reviewed ethnopharmacological literature (Jeong et al., 2014, Journal of Ethnopharmacology, PMID:24814038, DOI:10.1016/j.jep.2014.04.041; Abbasifard et al., 2025, PMID:39256070; Farinon et al., 2020, PMC7400098).
Efficacy evidence: Traditional practice context only; centuries-long folk use for skin and joint inflammation does not establish oral clinical efficacy for Parkinson disease symptoms.

Community and practice context: Traditional Korean, Chinese, and Persian/Eurasian folk dietary and topical uses of fixed oil pressed from non-intoxicating Cannabis sativa L. seeds.

Documented traditional scope: Jeong et al. (2014, Journal of Ethnopharmacology, PMID:24814038) record that PUFA-rich hempseed and hempseed oil have been used for centuries in Korean and Chinese folk medicine for atopic dermatitis, inflammatory complaints, and rheumatoid arthritis-like joint inflammation. Abbasifard et al. (2025, Pain Management Nursing, PMID:39256070) similarly note ancient food and traditional analgesic/anti-inflammatory applications of hemp seed oil motivating modern topical knee osteoarthritis testing, and Ma et al. (2023, PMID:37704445) cite Chinese folk food-medicine traditions of hemp seed oil.

Distinction from cannabis resin and PD efficacy: Traditional fixed seed-oil practices target dry skin, joint aching, and general nourishment using non-intoxicating seed lipids rather than psychoactive cannabis resin/flower extracts. Modern human evidence remains limited to atopic dermatitis (Callaway 2005) and topical knee OA pain (Abbasifard 2025), with zero controlled oral PD trials.
