Title: Traditional culinary and medicinal practices of brewed Hibiscus sabdariffa calyces (karkadeh, bissap, agua de jamaica, sorrel)
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3141364/
Source kind: Traditional Practice
Publication date: 2011
Date basis: Publication year of Beni-Sueif ethnobotanical survey (AbouZid & Mohamed 2011, PMC3141364), supplemented by Hopkins et al. 2013 (PMC3593772) and Ojulari/Cureus 2023 (PMC10676230).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Widely consumed hot or cold as a tart daily infusion traditionally attributed antihypertensive, cooling, and diuretic effects; no traditional Parkinson's disease indication.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed ethnobotanical field survey in Upper Egypt (57 informants, voucher CAIM131, PMC3141364) corroborated by cross-cultural ethnobotanical reviews (PMC3593772, PMC10676230).
Efficacy evidence: Ethnobotanical and cultural usage record only; documents traditional antihypertensive and diuretic attribution without establishing Parkinson's disease clinical efficacy or safety.

Across North and West Africa, Mexico, Central America, and the Caribbean, dried calyces of Hibiscus sabdariffa L. (Malvaceae) are steeped or decocted into ruby-red tart infusions known regionally as karkadeh (Egypt and Sudan), bissap or sobolo/zobo (Senegal, Ghana, Nigeria), agua de jamaica (Mexico and Central America), and red sorrel (Caribbean). In a quantitative ethnobotanical survey of 57 urban and rural residents in Beni-Sueif, Upper Egypt (AbouZid & Mohamed 2011, PMC3141364, voucher CAIM131), Hibiscus sabdariffa achieved the highest fidelity level (FI = 97.3%, 36 of 37 use citations) of any plant recorded in the cardiovascular category, prepared as a hot decoction or cold infusion specifically to lower blood pressure. Cross-cultural ethnobotanical reviews (Hopkins et al. 2013, PMC3593772; Cureus 2023, PMC10676230) similarly document traditional Mexican, West African, and Caribbean use of brewed calyces as a thirst-quenching beverage, mild diuretic, and folk antihypertensive. No traditional practice record specifically identifies brewed hibiscus as a treatment for Parkinson's motor or non-motor symptoms; its longstanding antihypertensive and diuretic folk uses align with modern hemodynamic and urine-output clinical findings.
