Title: European and Mexican folk medicine traditions of black radish root juice for bile/gallstones and Bologna ethnobotany of cultivated ravanello
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3471002/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2012
Date basis: Peer-reviewed publication year (Castro-Torres et al. 2012, J Biomed Biotechnol, PMID:23093836, PMC3471002), cross-referenced with Lugasi et al. 2005 (PMID:16161062) and Bologna district ethnobotany (Chiocchio et al. 2024, PMC10936038).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional folk use of squeezed black radish root juice as a cholagogue for bile stimulation, sluggish digestion, and gallstone mitigation (European/Mexican) and cultivated radish infusion for kidney health (Bologna); no neurological or Parkinson symptom claim.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed open-access ethnobotanical/preclinical report (Castro-Torres et al. 2012, PMC3471002) cross-linked with European folk review (Lugasi et al. 2005, PMID:16161062) and Bologna field ethnobotany (Chiocchio et al. 2024, PMC10936038, voucher BOLO0002283).
Efficacy evidence: Traditional digestive and renal folk knowledge only; does not establish clinical constipation, urinary, or Parkinson efficacy, and traditional/clinical sources caution against concentrated black radish choleretics in active gallstones or biliary obstruction.

Across European and Mexican traditional phytotherapy, black radish taproot (Raphanus sativus L. var. niger) has long been used as a pungent digestive bitter and cholagogue—specifically as freshly squeezed root juice to stimulate bile flow, ease heavy-meal digestion, lower lipids, and mitigate cholesterol gallstones (Castro-Torres et al. 2012, PMC3471002; Lugasi et al. 2005, PMID:16161062), alongside Northern/Eastern European folk honey-radish syrups for cough and bronchial mucus. In Northern Italy's Bologna district (Chiocchio et al. 2024, PMC10936038, voucher BOLO0002283), rural informants documented cultivated ravanello (Raphanus raphanistrum subsp. sativus) both as an edible root and in a traditional folk-ritual decoction of seven radishes boiled seven minutes and drunk for seven days for kidney health. Conversely, a human dietary trial in renal-stone and healthy subjects found radish intake increased urinary calcium oxalate crystal excretion (Kumar 2004, PMID:15449653). None of these traditions evaluate Parkinson disease symptoms.
