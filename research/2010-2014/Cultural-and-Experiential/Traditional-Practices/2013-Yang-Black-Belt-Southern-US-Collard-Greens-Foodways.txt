Title: Southeastern US Black Belt and African American culinary foodways of slow-stewed collard greens
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3578153/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2013
Date basis: Publication year of Yang et al. 2013 (Appetite 61:56-62, PMID:23262296, PMC3578153).
Reviewed: 2026-11-25 UTC
Reported direction: Cultural dietary staple and comfort foodway (slow-stewed collard greens); no Parkinson-specific symptom efficacy claim.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed cross-sectional food-preference interview study of 270 older adults in the Alabama Black Belt (Yang et al. 2013, Appetite; PMID:23262296, PMC3578153).
Efficacy evidence: Cultural dietary heritage context only; does not test clinical Parkinson's symptom outcomes.

Peer-reviewed documentation (Yang et al. 2013, Appetite 61:56-62, PMID:23262296 / PMC3578153) interviewed 270 home-health older adults in the Black Belt region of Alabama regarding favorite foods (1,857 favorite foods reported, mean 6.88 per person).

Cultural foodway findings: Collard greens ranked #2 overall among all favorite foods (behind chicken and ahead of cornbread, green beans, fish, and turnip greens). Authors situate slow-stewed collard greens (traditionally braised with seasoned broth / "pot likker") as a defining pillar of African American and Deep South culinary heritage.

Preparation and evidentiary context: Traditional long-simmered preparations differ substantially from raw or lightly steamed greens with respect to water-soluble glucosinolate retention (Song & Thornalley 2007) and vitamin K/lutein bioavailability. Cultural dietary centrality does not constitute clinical evidence for Parkinson's symptoms.
