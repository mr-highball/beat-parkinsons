Traditional Culinary & Folk Medicine Heritage:
Artemisia dracunculus L. (tarragon; Asteraceae) has longstanding culinary and folk-medicine roles across Central Asia, Iran (Tarkhun, eaten fresh in sabzi khordan), the Caucasus/Georgia (Tarkhuna soda and stews), Russia, and classical French haute cuisine (fines herbes, bearnaise sauce) (Obolskiy et al. 2011, PMID:21942448; Majdan et al. 2020, PMC7438555). Historical Persian and European herbals attributed carminative, appetite-stimulating, mild sedative/sleep-aiding, antispasmodic, and local toothache-numbing properties to chewed fresh leaves or infusions.

Species & Chemotype Boundaries + Toxicology Cautions:
Culinary tarragon exists in two distinct chemotypes: vegetatively propagated French tarragon (essential oil containing up to 60-82% estragole) and seed-grown Russian tarragon (higher methyleugenol/elemicin, lower culinary aroma). Artemisia dracunculus must never be conflated with A. annua (sweet wormwood/artemisinin), A. absinthium (thujone-rich wormwood), or A. vulgaris (mugwort). Because estragole and methyleugenol are rodent hepatocarcinogens/genotoxins (Kalantari 2013, PMID:23010670; FEMA GRAS reassessment Davidsen 2023, PMID:36804339), European and food-safety authorities advise keeping concentrated tarragon oil/extract intake as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA), whereas trace culinary seasoning or aqueous estragole-free extracts carry far lower exposure.
