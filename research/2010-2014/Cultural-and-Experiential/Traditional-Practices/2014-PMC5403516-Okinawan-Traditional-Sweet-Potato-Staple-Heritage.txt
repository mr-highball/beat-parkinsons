Title: Traditional Okinawan beni-imo and imo sweet potato staple diet and healthy-aging foodway heritage
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5403516/
Source kind: Ethnographic nutrition and culinary-heritage record
Publication date: 2014
Date basis: Publication year of Willcox et al. 2014 (Mech Ageing Dev, PMID:24462788, PMC5403516), cross-referenced with Le Couteur et al. 2016 (PMID:27130207, PMC4916345); historical 1949-1950 Okinawan dietary intake period noted separately.
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional Okinawan dietary pattern anchored by steamed/boiled sweet potato (~69% of mid-20th-century total calories) is culturally and epidemiologically associated with longevity, low caloric density, high dietary fiber, and plant antioxidant intake; ecological population foodway without isolated Parkinson disease clinical efficacy.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed open-access aging-nutrition scholarship (Willcox et al. 2014, PMC5403516; Le Couteur et al. 2016, PMC4916345) documenting historical Okinawan dietary surveys.
Efficacy evidence: Historical population dietary pattern and culinary heritage only; ecological longevity associations involve entire lifestyle and low-protein/high-carbohydrate patterns rather than isolated sweet potato interventions for Parkinson symptoms.

Title and source: Traditional Okinawan sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas, locally imo / purple-fleshed beni-imo) dietary heritage, documented in Willcox, Scapagnini & Willcox, Mech Ageing Dev 2014 (PMID:24462788, PMC5403516) and Le Couteur et al., Age Ageing 2016 (PMC4916345).
Community/context: Introduced to the Ryukyu Kingdom from China in the early 17th century as a typhoon-resistant famine safeguard, sweet potato became the primary caloric foundation of the traditional Okinawan diet—accounting for roughly two-thirds (~69%) of total daily calories in 1949 dietary surveys, far surpassing rice. Typically steamed or boiled plain alongside green/yellow vegetables, tofu, seaweed, and modest marine protein, the traditional sweet-potato-anchored pattern yielded a high-carbohydrate, fiber-rich, naturally low-protein (~9% energy) diet studied extensively in centenarian aging research.
Relevance and limits: While Okinawan culinary heritage highlights steamed/boiled sweet potato as a daily high-fiber, nutrient-dense, naturally low-protein staple (theoretically compatible with daytime low-protein meal planning around levodopa), ecological longevity patterns reflect multi-factor lifestyle, caloric, and genetic contexts and provide no controlled evidence for roasted, baked, or boiled sweet potato on specific Parkinson motor or non-motor symptoms.
