Title: Southeast Asian and Indian ethnobotanical records of Carica papaya for constipation and digestive disorders
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23183086/
Source kind: Traditional-Practice
Publication date: 2013-01-09
Date basis: Publication date of Abe & Ohtani, Journal of Ethnopharmacology 145(1):189-203 (2013; PMID:23183086), corroborated by Choudhury et al. 2015 (PMID:26387738).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Strong traditional consensus (100% Fidelity Level in Batan Island, Philippines; top-10 Disease Consensus Index in Southern Assam, India) for ripe Carica papaya fruit alleviating constipation and digestive dysfunction.
Authenticity: Quantitative peer-reviewed ethnobotanical surveys interviewing 116 Ivatan informants (Abe & Ohtani 2013) and 29 Bengali informants (Choudhury et al. 2015), alongside traditional Caribbean/Southeast Asian monastic ripening preparations motivating Caricol.
Efficacy evidence: Traceable customary ethnobotanical consensus aligning directionally with the Caricol constipation RCT (Muss 2013) without independently establishing PD clinical efficacy; distinguishes true tropical Carica papaya from TCM 'Mu Gua' (Chaenomeles speciosa flowering quince).

Quantitative ethnobotanical surveys across Southeast Asia and South Asia document strong traditional consensus for Carica papaya in bowel regularity. Among 116 indigenous Ivatan informants on Batan Island, Philippines (Abe & Ohtani 2013, PMID:23183086), Carica papaya achieved the maximum 100% Fidelity Level specifically for treating constipation. In Southern Assam, India (Choudhury et al. 2015, PMID:26387738), Carica papaya ranked among the top ten medicinal plants by Disease Consensus Index for digestive system disorders. Similar Caribbean and tropical culinary traditions value ripe papaya flesh as a gentle digestive fruit while cautioning against unripe papaya latex during pregnancy. Importantly, traditional tropical Carica papaya must be separated from Traditional Chinese Medicine 'Mu Gua' (Chaenomeles speciosa, flowering quince), which is frequently mistranslated as papaya in bioinformatics papers.
