Title: European herbal, Persian, and Unani (Alubukhara) traditional laxative practices for Prunus domestica plums and prunes
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25109788/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2014
Date basis: Peer-reviewed systematic-review publication dates (Lever et al., Aliment Pharmacol Ther 2014, PMID:25109788; Igwe & Charlton, Phytother Res 2016, PMID:26992121); culinary and herbal laxative traditions span centuries across Europe, Persia, and South Asia.
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Favorable traditional laxative and digestive-cooling use of dried plums (prunes), stewed compotes, and infusions for constipation; no historical Parkinson-specific controlled clinical claim.
Authenticity: Traceable peer-reviewed systematic reviews inspected during Round 6 review (PMID:25109788; PMID:26992121) summarizing historical European and Middle Eastern medicinal-food usage.
Efficacy evidence: Traditional culinary-medicinal context only; aligns qualitatively with modern non-PD prune constipation RCTs while highlighting the preparation gap between dried prunes and poached fresh plums.

Across European monastic herbals, British pharmacy confections, medieval Persian medicine, and Greco-Arabic Unani-Tibb (where dried Prunus domestica plums are known as Alubukhara), dried plums (prunes), stewed plum compotes, and prune infusions have been prescribed for centuries as gentle dietary laxatives to soften stools and ease constipation (Lever et al., 2014, PMID:25109788; Igwe & Charlton, 2016, PMID:26992121). Traditional sources classify ripe and dried European plums as cooling and moistening to the digestive tract, attributing laxative effects in modern food chemistry to the combined action of poorly absorbed sorbitol, soluble and insoluble fiber, and phenolic acids (such as neochlorogenic and chlorogenic acids). Traditional documentation strictly distinguishes culinary Prunus domestica and Prunus salicina plums from unrelated botanicals such as Japanese apricot/ume (Prunus mume) or Kakadu plum (Terminalia ferdinandiana). While modern randomized trials confirm bowel-softening and stool-frequency benefits for daily dried prunes and prune juice in non-PD chronic constipation, traditional usage alone does not establish efficacy or optimal dosing for poached fresh plums in Parkinson disease.
