# Shiso and perilla follow-up

## Dried leaf extract and constipation measures in adults

The registry first posted NCT01931930 on August 30, 2013, after the intervention had taken place. It reports 47 participants and no posted numerical results. Buchwald-Werner and colleagues published the randomized, double-blind trial on May 27, 2014. Fifty otherwise healthy adults with gastrointestinal discomfort and reduced bowel movements enrolled and 47 completed according to the protocol. The active capsules contained an aqueous extract of dried *Perilla frutescens* leaves. The official record names maltodextrin for placebo, while the published Methods states tapioca starch. Over four weeks, days with stools increased by 0.83 ± 1.40 per week on extract and 0.80 ± 1.02 on tapioca starch placebo. Neither this comparison, total Patient Assessment of Constipation Symptoms (PAC-SYM), nor constipation quality of life differed significantly between groups. The favorable subgroup contrast reported in women concerned combined bloating and abdominal discomfort. The study gives no estimate for fresh leaves and Parkinson constipation. ## Inhaled perilla aroma and subjective relaxation

Igarashi and colleagues first published this prospective study on June 20, 2014. Nineteen female university students smelled perilla essential oil and control air during near-infrared spectroscopy. Prefrontal oxyhemoglobin decreased during oil exposure. Comfort and relaxation ratings did not differ significantly. The participants were healthy, and the measurements did not assess clinical anxiety, loss of smell or consumption of shiso leaves. [Primary report, PMID 24949882](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24949882/)

## Perilla leaf, cilantro leaf and marjoram in a recombinant MAO-B screen

Deiab, Mazzio and Soliman reported in April 2012 that *Perilla frutescens* leaf, cilantro and marjoram were among extracts showing inhibition of recombinant human monoamine oxidase B (MAO-B). The meeting abstract gives a test concentration range for the assay but provides no individual concentration-response estimates or further specification of the cilantro or marjoram material. [FASEB meeting abstract](https://faseb.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1096/fasebj.26.1_supplement.821.8)

Mazzio, Deiab, Park and Soliman published a screen of 905 natural-product extracts online on August 8, 2012, ahead of the June 2013 issue. The team used ethanol extracts and measured recombinant human MAO-B activity with benzylamine as the substrate. The methods address potential interference from pH and peroxide scavenging. Table 1 places *P. frutescens* leaf (P15, labeled beefsteakplant) at level 2 with IC₅₀ below 0.2 mg/mL, *Coriandrum sativum* leaf (c80) and *Origanum majorana* (M32) at level 4 with IC₅₀ above 0.4 and below 0.7 mg/mL. The table gives no plant part for the marjoram and no perilla cultivar. A separate curry powder blend (C84) contains coriander among other ingredients. The abstract and article share investigators and the core assay, so they are treated as one research stream. The degree of sample overlap is unclear. These measurements concern enzyme inhibition in vitro. The sources do not estimate exposure after eating the herbs or report any Parkinson symptom outcomes. The manuscript was reviewed online and its license falls outside the local full-text archive scope. [Primary article and Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3521852/#T1)
