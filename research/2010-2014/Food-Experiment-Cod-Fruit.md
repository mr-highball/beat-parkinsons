# Food experiment evidence for cod, pineapple, plum, and cantaloupe

## Gliadin-combined melon extract and fatigue

Houghton and colleagues published November 1, 2010, before the April 2011 issue. Thirty-eight women aged 50–65 with self-perceived fatigue entered a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled comparison. Over 12 weeks, general fatigue on the Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory decreased by 1.6% in the melon SOD and gliadin group and 4.1% in placebo, with no clear between- or within-group difference. Physical fatigue, mental fatigue and the other inventory dimensions also had no clear difference. This is a supplemented non-Parkinson cohort with a formulation distinct from Extramel. [Primary report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21044831/) · [Earlier registration](../2005-2009/Food-Experiment-Cod-Fruit.md#glisodin-fatigue-trial-registration)

## Melon concentrate and fatigue in an 84-day trial

Carillon and colleagues randomized 61 healthy adults to a 10 mg Extramel capsule or starch placebo. The concentrate came from a proprietary melon variety and was freeze-dried and palm-oil coated. The June 19, 2014 paper reports Prevost physical fatigue means of 84.4 in the supplement arm and 93.2 in placebo at day 84 (p < .02). The difference at day 28 was inconclusive. Perceived stress was the primary outcome and physical fatigue was secondary. This trial gives no Parkinson fatigue or raw fruit effect. NCT01767922 was first posted January 15, 2013 and lists the same 61-person comparison. Its API record has no posted results. ## Plum and prune extracts in rotenone-exposed neurons

Strathearn and colleagues' February 2014 cell study tested fresh-plum and dried-prune polyphenol extracts in primary rat midbrain cultures. Neither extract clearly mitigated rotenone-associated dopaminergic cell loss under the study conditions. Their phenolic composition differed. The study did not test cooked fruit, levodopa-associated dyskinesia or any behavior.
