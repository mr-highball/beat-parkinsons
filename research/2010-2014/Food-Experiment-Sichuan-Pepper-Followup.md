# Sichuan pepper follow-up

## Abdominal pain in a two-dose constipation series

In a report first published July 20, 2010, Horiuchi and colleagues followed 22 adults with chronic constipation given one of two doses of oral daikenchuto. The higher-dose group’s median abdominal pain score fell from 32 to 9 at four weeks and 7 at both six and eight weeks on a 0–100 scale. The lower-dose group’s within-group comparison reached significance at week four only. There was no placebo group. The intervention combined Japanese *Zanthoxylum piperitum* with ginger, ginseng and maltose. This is indirect pain evidence for 0983 from a distinct population and exposure. ## Perceived tingling on the lower lip

Hagura, Barber and Haggard’s 2013 volunteer experiments applied a preparation of ground commercial Szechuan pepper to the lower lip and measured matching to mechanical fingertip vibration. The perceived tingling corresponded to approximately 50 Hz. The stimulus was applied topically, and the paper does not establish a species-specific culinary intake. Acoustic voice volume, clinically rated hypophonia and pain relief were outside the experiments. The finding supplies oral sensory context for 0137. [Primary report](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2013.1680)

## Mechanical hypersensitivity after locally applied sanshool

Tsunozaki and colleagues tested the isolated pepper constituent hydroxy-α-sanshool in mouse models of mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity in 2013. Local application reduced inflammatory mechanical hypersensitivity and did not reduce heat hypersensitivity. The tested compound, route and model limit use of this finding for 0983, which concerns toasted culinary Sichuan pepper and pain in Parkinson’s. [Primary report](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2013.252106)
