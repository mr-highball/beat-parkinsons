# Nigella seed and anise seed evidence

## Crushed nigella seed capsules and timed attention tasks

Bin Sayeed and colleagues randomized 40 healthy older adults to capsules of crushed *Nigella sativa* seed or psyllium for nine weeks. The preparation was ground and screened through a mesh. Toasting is unreported. Table 2 gives shorter completion times for letter cancellation (165.20 to 159.90 seconds with nigella, 164.85 to 163.70 with placebo, p = .015), Trail Making A (p = .002) and Trail Making B (p < .001). Letter cancellation accuracy was inconclusive (p = .485). The trial measured several cognitive outcomes in a small sample and included no Parkinson cohort. A separate 48-person trial in adolescent boys found a within-arm mood change with seed capsules and no clear between-arm mood difference. [Older-adult paper, Table 2](https://magistralbr.caldic.com/storage/product-files/1058155947.pdf#page=5) · [Older-adult index](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23707331/) · [Adolescent trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24412554/)

## Nigella and the aged rat olfactory system

Eltony and Elgayar examined 15 female rats in three groups. Oral nigella in aged rats was associated with thicker olfactory epithelium and selected cellular differences in the bulb and piriform cortex. The study evaluated tissue structure and supplied no behavioral odor threshold, identification score or Parkinson comparison. [Publisher report](https://rjme.ro/archive/55/2/12/)

## Nigella extract and forced swimming in healthy mice

Shariatifar and colleagues randomized 35 male mice to three oral seed-extract groups, fluoxetine or a control. The extract groups had shorter forced-swim immobility than control. The authors interpreted this together with blood biochemistry as an exercise-fatigue and depression signal. The test did not measure perceived fatigue, Parkinson’s disease or culinary seed. [Licensed original paper](https://www.scirp.org/pdf/cm_2014032514405640.pdf)

## Anise powder and vitality in functional dyspepsia

Ghoshegir and colleagues randomized 107 patients with postprandial distress syndrome to anise powder or cornstarch. The 2014 paper reports higher week-12 SF-36 vitality and bodily pain domain scores with anise. Higher scores indicate better health status. The companion January 2015 paper uses the same registration identifier and identical group sizes of 47 and 60. The comparison measured broad vitality and functional dyspepsia in patients outside Parkinson’s. The historical registration identifier in the two papers is `2013101214980`. A current exact IRCT search did not return that identifier. [Original SF-36 report, Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4333517/#T2) · [Companion paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4354059/)

## Anise odor identification in Parkinson disease

The 2013 study used Sniffin’ Sticks in 148 people with Parkinson’s and 148 healthy controls. Anise was among three stimuli with the largest recognition differences. This was a diagnostic odor exposure, with no ground seed intervention or symptom change estimate. [Licensed primary text](Source-Text/PMC3655992.txt)

## Early Malik report on black seed oil and drug-induced movements

Malik and colleagues’ 2013 conference abstract reports black seed oil and haloperidol exposure in rats over six weeks. It describes oral dyskinesia, other movement observations and astrogliosis. Group sizes, formulation details and numerical motor estimates remain unavailable in this abstract. The same author team and outcomes appear in the 2016 publication PMID 27752803, suggesting one study family. Identical animals remain unverified. The preparation used oil and the outcomes came from drug exposure in rats. [Original source](https://wfneurology.org/public/documents/dF88x/WCN%202013%20Abstracts.pdf) · [Local factual text](Source-Text/2013-Malik-Nigella-Oil-WCN-Abstract-Extract.txt)

## Nigella seed extract and drug-induced catalepsy in mice

Hadipour Jahromy and colleagues published online in August 2014. Fifty male mice were assigned to five groups in a perphenazine model. Oral hydroalcoholic Nigella seed extract was compared with water and a levodopa reference. The two higher extract groups had lower imposed-posture scores over at least some time points, with an inconclusive contrast at the lowest level. The test measures maintenance of an imposed paw position. The preparation and model do not give an estimate of walking speed or clinical rigidity after consuming culinary Nigella.
