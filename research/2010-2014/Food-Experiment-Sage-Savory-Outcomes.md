# Sage and summer savory symptom studies

## Registered sage inhalation trials for obstetric pain

WHO ICTRP lists registration on January 13, 2011 for **IRCT138811192515N1**, against an enrollment date of June 22, 2009 in the source registry. It lists three arms comparing common sage essence, jasmine essence and distilled water in 156 first-time mothers. Kaviani's later publication gives a study period of October 2009 to March 2010, uses an inhaled aqueous distillate and cites the same identifier. The registry enrollment field and article study dates remain separate. [Official registry record](https://trialsearch.who.int/Trial2.aspx?TrialID=IRCT138811192515N1) · [Primary methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4280734/)

WHO ICTRP lists registration on April 18, 2014 for **IRCT201402215912N14**, against an enrollment date of April 5, 2014. The registered study assigned 120 post-cesarean participants to *Salvia officinalis* aroma, bitter-orange aroma or saline inhalation. The registration describes a visual pain scale. The 2017 article's accessible abstract identifies numeric pain ratings. The original tables and earliest registry change history remain open. [Official registry record](https://trialsearch.who.int/Trial2.aspx?TrialID=IRCT201402215912N14) · [Linked 2017 original](../2015-2019/Food-Experiment-Sage-Pain-Comparisons.md#sage-and-bitter-orange-aroma-for-post-cesarean-pain)

## Inhaled sage distillate and labor pain

Kaviani and colleagues first published in November 2014 (PMID **25558267**, PMCID **PMC4280734**). They assigned 52 participants per group to inhaled distillate of dried common-sage flowering branches, jasmine extract or distilled water. In unblinded Table 1, average pain at 30 minutes on the 0–10 scale was 2.46 with sage, 3.19 with jasmine and 3.17 with water (overall p=.001). At 60 minutes the respective scores were 4.00, 4.31 and 3.98 (overall p=.10). The original reports block randomization and separate exposure days. This is short-term obstetric pain after inhalation of a distillate. [Original methods and results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4280734/) · [Original Table 1](https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/0bb8/4280734/cf997c232b49/IJNMR-19-666-g001.jpg)

## Sage tea rinse as an active comparator for oral mucositis

Steinmann and colleagues published in 2012 (PMID **22875590**). Their prospective matched-pair observational comparison examined oral mucositis during radiotherapy or radiochemotherapy in 20 analyzed head and neck cancer patients. A common-sage tea mouth rinse was the active comparator for Traumeel S rinse. Patient diary pain gave a small contrast favoring Traumeel S with no clear between-group difference. The authors note that analgesic use was more frequent in the Traumeel S group. The study yields no comparison of sage with an inert rinse and concerns local contact in mucositis. [Author abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22875590/) · [Original journal issue, pp. 12–18](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230636818_New_Perspectives_on_Complementary_and_Alternative_Medicine_An_Overview_and_Alternative_Therapy)

## Sage extract in a mouse chemotherapy neuropathy model

Abad and colleagues published in September 2011 (DOI **10.3724/SP.J.1009.2011.00354**). The original abstract compares hydroalcoholic common-sage extract in a vincristine mouse model across six groups, with paw licking and biting after formalin as the outcome. It reports an analgesic response associated with the extract. The available abstract does not give a usable arm estimate or the full outcome table. The model and preparation differ from clinical pain after eating fresh sage. [Original author abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1875536411600757)

## Sage extract and hot flash interference in prostate cancer

Vandecasteele and colleagues first published on June 1, 2011. Ten men on androgen deprivation received common sage extract following a pretreatment diary period. The weekly hot flash score declined, while the aggregate interference score showed no clear change. The interference scale includes sleep, concentration and enjoyment of life. Separate estimates for these items are absent. There was no concurrent control. [Primary report, Methods and Results](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdfdirect/10.1002/ptr.3528) · [Official registration](../2005-2009/Food-Experiment-Sage-Savory-Outcomes.md#sage-and-hot-flash-interference-registration)

## Summer savory and amyloid beta in a cell-free screen

Ishigaki and colleagues published their spice screen on August 26, 2013. Ethanolic summer savory extract inhibited amyloid beta aggregation in the fluorescence assay. The authors reported an EC50 of 0.049 ± 0.056 mg/mL and identified rosmarinic acid in activity-guided fractionation. The assay used an Alzheimer-associated protein and gives no behavioral or executive-function measurement for claim 0521. [Primary paper, Figure 8](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0072992) · [Local article text](Source-Text/PMC3753230.txt)

## Salvinorin A and animal pain assays

The 2011 review traces isolated salvinorin A to *Salvia divinorum*. Its animal pain evidence varies by assay and administration route. It supplies no result for fresh common sage leaf ingestion or Parkinson pain. [Original review, section V.B.1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3082450/) · [Local original factual extract](Source-Text/2011-Cunningham-Salvinorin-A-Factual-Extract.txt)

## Two additional sage extract comparisons in animals

Two original abstracts describe lower evoked pain behaviors after sage leaf extracts. The 2010 report compared aqueous and butanol preparations in mice and rats. The report first available in 2011 describes a hydroalcoholic preparation in mice. The original full bodies remain open for group estimates.
