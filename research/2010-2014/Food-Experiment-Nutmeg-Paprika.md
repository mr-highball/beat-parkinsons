# Nutmeg and paprika evidence

## Smoked and unsmoked paprika in healthy young men

Campillo and colleagues published a paired dietary study in July 2010. Healthy university men ate meals containing smoked or unsmoked paprika during separate 30-day periods. The mean supine systolic pressure was 119.9 mmHg at both smoked measurements and 120.2 to 117.9 mmHg during the unsmoked period. Table 4 lists 53 and 50 observations by preparation. The authors measured urinary 1-hydroxypyrene, a smoke-related exposure biomarker, and recorded no voiding-urgency or orthostatic lightheadedness endpoint. [Primary report, methods and Table 4](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/19476330903274286)

## Inhaled nutmeg oil and mouse locomotion

Muchtaridi and colleagues reported that inhaled nutmeg essential oil suppressed wheel-cage activity in mice under the experimental conditions. Activity suppression in this assay is a sedation-related surrogate. The design used inhalation and did not measure daytime sleepiness or Parkinson motor outcomes. [Primary full text](Source-Text/PMC3000115.txt)

## Nutmeg poison-center cases in Illinois

Ehrenpreis and colleagues first published the Illinois series on January 23, 2014. It describes 32 calls from 2001–2011, including 15 intentional and 17 unintentional ingestions. The case narrative with drowsiness, hallucinations and tremor includes several coexposures. The authors also report that a recorder judged a recorded tremor unrelated to exposure. The paper supplies no rest-tremor classification or standardized Parkinson rest-tremor or daytime-sleepiness outcome. These were toxicology calls with incomplete clinical ascertainment. [Primary report](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s13181-013-0379-7.pdf)

## Mood symptoms during high-dose nutmeg misuse

Parthasarathi and colleagues’ December 2013 case describes a man reporting recurrent episodes of dysphoria and psychosis during deliberate high-dose nutmeg misuse. The authors revised a prior bipolar diagnosis and described symptom resolution after the acute episode. Exposure and symptoms were self-reported. The context was intoxication and does not establish a response to ordinary culinary grated nutmeg or Parkinson depression. [Original case report](https://www.mdedge.com/psychiatry/article/78788/addiction-medicine/out-cupboard-and-clinic-nutmeg-induced-mood-disorder) · [Local factual extract](Source-Text/2013-Parthasarathi-Nutmeg-Mood-Factual-Extract.txt)

## Aqueous nutmeg extract and small-intestinal marker travel

The 2012 report gives mean charcoal travel of 85.53% of small-intestinal length in water controls and 67.85%, 58.46% and 51.35% across ascending doses of aqueous nutmeg seed extract, with six mice per group. The Methods for charcoal describe fasting and administration of charcoal and treatment without naming castor oil. The abstract describes the transit experiment as castor-oil induced. The challenge status therefore remains uncertain. Small-intestinal marker travel, ground-spice consumption and clinical constipation are distinct outcomes and exposures. [Local factual extract](Source-Text/2012-Shamkuwar-Pawar-Nutmeg-Transit-Factual-Extract.txt) · [Shamkuwar and Pawar, Methods and Table 3](https://www.scholarsresearchlibrary.com/articles/significance-of-nutmeg-in-diarrhoea.pdf)

## Nutmeg fruit syrup and computerized task measures

Tjondro’s 2010 university thesis abstract describes 30 young women tested before and after nutmeg fruit syrup. The task precision result moved from 51.91 to 48.85 (p < .05); alertness response time from 106.33 to 116.57 was inconclusive (p > .05). There was no reported parallel control. The abstract does not verify exposure to ground nutmeg seed or provide a sleepiness scale. Fruit syrup exposure and task response time differ from the frozen culinary hypothesis.
