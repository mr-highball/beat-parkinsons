Scholey and colleagues report a five-person bioavailability pilot plus two randomized, double-blind, balanced crossovers in healthy young adults. The intervention was manufactured from water-extracted dried leaf. The later food and drink studies used a concentrated extract with more than 6% rosmarinic acid. That preparation differs from a brewed leaf infusion. The paper states 25 recruits for the 480 mL drink study and 21 for the 250 g yoghurt study. The recruit totals are distinct from analyzable totals for individual endpoints.

The drink study's 0.3 g extract plus fruit sweetener was associated with lower state anxiety at one and three hours than the placebo beverage, which used artificial sweeteners. There was no fruit-sweetener-only placebo in that comparison. The 0.6 g extract plus artificial sweeteners was associated with higher state anxiety. The Results place this at one hour and the Discussion at three hours. The placebo used the same blend of artificial sweeteners as this high-dose arm, so the reported difference cannot be assigned to the sweetener alone. The paper reports no other clear drink-study mood effects.

In the yoghurt study, both 0.6 g conditions were associated with higher mental fatigue at one hour. The fruit-sweetened high-dose condition favored lower mental fatigue at three hours. Yoghurt outcomes stay with that comparison. These acute state anxiety and fatigue results do not supply a depressive-symptom score in Parkinson disease.

The article discloses corporate sponsorship, sponsor input on design and selection of outcomes, and use of a proprietary extract. The linked ANZCTR registration is retrospective and describes three planned matrices, including fruit bars. Its reported amounts differ from the later paper's concentrated extract conditions. This article reports the beverage and yoghurt results. The registered bar comparison remains unresolved.
