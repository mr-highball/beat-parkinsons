Title: Anti-diarrhoeal investigation from aqueous extract of Cuminum cyminum Linn. Seed in Albino rats
Authors: Himanshu Bhusan Sahoo, Saroj Kumar Sahoo, Sarada Prasad Sarangi, Rakesh Sagar, and Mohan Lal Kori
Citation: Pharmacognosy Research. July–September 2014;6(3):204–209.
PMID: 25002800
PMCID: PMC4080500
DOI: 10.4103/0974-8490.132596
Source: https://www.phcogres.com/sites/default/files/PharmacognRes-6-3-204.pdf
Text coverage: factual summary of oral extract preparation, selected methods and Tables 1–2.

The investigators dried a water extract of cumin seed to a residue, resuspended it, and administered it by gastric intubation. The reported mean extent of charcoal movement at 30 minutes (percentage of intestinal length) was 84.94 ± 2.78% (vehicle), 49.48 ± 0.65% (atropine), 62.19 ± 3.28% (100 mg/kg extract), 60.09 ± 2.48% (250 mg/kg) and 55.12 ± 2.66% (500 mg/kg), mean ± SEM, n=6 each. Table 2 indicates p < .001 versus vehicle for atropine and each extract arm. The table's inhibition percentage is calculated as the portion of intestinal length that charcoal did not traverse, as evident from the reported component values. The narrative mislabels this complement as distance travelled and calls the highest dose more effective than atropine. Table 2 shows further slowing in the atropine arm. This was acute small-intestinal progression, separate from its castor oil-induced diarrhea and fluid accumulation experiments. There was no levodopa, Parkinson model or clinical OFF-time endpoint.
