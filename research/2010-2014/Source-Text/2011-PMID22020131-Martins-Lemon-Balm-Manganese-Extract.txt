Title: Protective effect of Melissa officinalis aqueous extract against Mn-induced oxidative stress in chronically exposed mice

Reviewed: 2026-11-18 UTC

Martins and colleagues compared prolonged manganese exposure with water in 16 male mice. After the initial three months, the animals were assigned to four groups of four. The second phase included water, Melissa officinalis alone, manganese alone and concurrent manganese plus Melissa. The preparation was a filtered hot-water steep of dried aerial portions, diluted for administration in the drinking water. The paper labels the herb-only and co-treatment exposure 100 mg/kg/day. This small animal comparison does not establish a human tea dose.

Manganese raised the lipid-peroxidation marker TBARS in the hippocampus and striatum and lowered total thiol in the hippocampus. The concurrent herb preparation attenuated those changes. Catalase and superoxide dismutase effects varied by tissue. The paper did not quantify brain manganese, so a change in tissue exposure remains a competing explanation. The early open-field observations preceded the assigned herb comparison. Those activity data were not shown, and the authors report habituation to the apparatus.

This is a metal-toxicity model with Parkinson-like context. Its outcomes describe tissue chemistry. It supplies no estimate for depressive symptoms, treated rest tremor, or gait improvement in Parkinson disease. The hot-water route is closer to the frozen preparation than an ethanolic cell extract, while exposure, population and outcome still limit transfer.
