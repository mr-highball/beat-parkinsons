Text coverage: Full 372-page poster collection, independently deposited at the University of Padua. Printed pages 74 and 445 and selected member folios were visually verified. Raw-order and layout text were scanned, with the pertinent member bodies read individually.

P18.28 (printed 320) surveys phenolic content and antioxidant measurements in plant foods. It mentions strawberry and blackberry, and separately compares or describes banana, barley, broccoli, grape, red onion, tomato and soybean. These are composition observations, without clinical administration or falls. P11.54 (printed 209) cites strawberries as one source of ellagic acid and tests the isolated compound in cell lines. The reported modest p53 and PTEN increases in cancer cell lines were statistically nonsignificant. Neither member isolates a strawberry effect on Parkinson disease or fall frequency.

P01.3 (printed 74) records rice bran, corn, sesame and coconut oils as substrates of a bacterial lipase in an enzyme characterization. No culinary grade, ingestion or symptom was measured. P26.55 (printed 406) uses corn oil as the diazinon solvent in a rat control arm and reports liver oxidative endpoints. P18.29 (printed 320–321) uses injected olive oil as the toxicant vehicle, and P26.48 separately tests hydroxytyrosol in red cells. P19.5 (printed 323) studies soybean-oil emulsion as a pharmaceutical formulation and tests cellular outcomes. P16.8 studies a sesame seed protein allergen. These roles do not identify press or refinement condition or a culinary treatment effect.

Other source-level links include apricot pectinesterase (P18.11), barley germplasm and food proteins (P18.17 and P03.46), egg-white lysozyme (P03.87 and P14.72), grape-seed extract (P10.58 and P20.9), and tomato plant-biochemistry members including P18.19 and P18.26. Sulforaphane is studied in several laboratory contexts including P10.21, P15.15 and P26.8. Cinnamon-related members include a description of cinnamaldehyde as a receptor stimulus (P03.96) and a cinnamon-derived chemical analog tested on inflammatory responses (P16.19). Orange instances in the matched results are dye or assay terminology, and date terms concern chronology.

P26.23 (printed 396) compares circulating oxidative markers in 20 participants with Parkinson disease, 20 with Alzheimer disease, 20 with vascular dementia and 20 matched controls. The Parkinson group is reported to have higher malondialdehyde (p < 0.05) and lower total antioxidant capacity (p < 0.01) than controls. The text reports no significant between-group antioxidant enzyme results in the individual diagnoses, while its pooled patient analysis reports a higher SOD activity. The member did not measure dietary strawberry or falls. The printed material keeps those human biomarker observations, distinct experimental Parkinson abstracts and the strawberry and culinary ingredient entries in separate members.
