Text coverage: Distilled member-level review of all 620 printed pages. The publisher issued a separate erratum with omitted abstracts.

S258, tracking ID 11616. Two web surveys included 260 shift workers and 673 healthcare professionals. Among the workers, 72% reported an effect of shift work on energy. Coffee or tea use to manage symptoms was reported by 35%, and caffeinated soda by 33%. The survey did not isolate coffee versus tea, assign treatment or compare energy scores by remedy. The same 260/673 counts and author group appear in a parallel ISPOR presentation. These are one survey family without a Parkinson fatigue estimate.

S463, tracking ID 11149. A group-home case used afternoon coffee breaks plus social interaction during evaluation of repeated requests for pain relief. Initial improvement was followed by recurrent complaints. The episodes were then linked to the menstrual cycle and improved with a change in analgesic scheduling. There was no controlled coffee intake, and the endpoint was not fatigue.

S475, tracking ID 11407. An iron-deficiency case included a report of heartburn after drinking coffee. She denied fatigue when the history was clarified. This is a report of reflux symptoms and does not establish a fatigue response.

S539–S540, tracking ID 12372. One case describes reported caffeine-pill use before exercise alongside other exposures and later documented anabolic steroid use. The supplement was not brewed coffee, there was no assigned comparison, and fatigue was not measured as an outcome. A separate abstract reports a mixed supplement labeled as containing protein, caffeine and vitamins during a case with rhabdomyolysis and atrial fibrillation. The independent role of caffeine was not established.
