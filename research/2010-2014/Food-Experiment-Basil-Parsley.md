# Basil and parsley evidence

## Basil aroma in the Italian Olfactory Identification Test

Maremmani and colleagues published the validation of the Italian Olfactory Identification Test on April 15, 2012. The instrument presents culturally familiar odorants for forced-choice recognition and was validated with Parkinson’s participants and controls. A 2013 Perugia congress abstract reports that basil was among the odors participants with Parkinson’s most frequently failed to identify, citing the validation work. The congress book was posted online later. The Perugia follow-ups used the same instrument, and cohort overlap between later abstracts and the 2024 clinical paper remains unresolved. [2012 primary validation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22510205/) · [2013 congress abstract P108](https://www.parkinsonlimpedismov.it/web/image/28328/AbstractCongressoRoma2013.pdf) · [Later primary outcome](../2020-2024/Food-Experiment-Basil-Parsley.md#basil-odor-identification-in-a-parkinson-cohort)

## Inhaled basil blend and perceived mental exhaustion

Varney and Buckle first published November 9, 2012. Fourteen adults with self-identified mental exhaustion or moderate burnout were assigned to a personal inhaler containing basil, peppermint and helichrysum oils or rose water. Both groups reported less exhaustion and the authors report a greater reduction for the mixed oils. The study used a self-rating, inhalation and a blend. It provides no dried culinary basil or Parkinson-specific fatigue estimate. [Primary trial abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23140115/)

## Parsley ingestion and near-fatal anaphylaxis

Arslan and colleagues published December 16, 2014. The patient experienced near-fatal anaphylaxis after parsley ingestion. This is an individual allergic reaction with no dried-leaf comparison or measurement of Parkinson dysphagia or orthostatic lightheadedness. [Primary case report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4314155/)

## Dried parsley tea in a randomized sleep crossover

Ngan and Conduit first published February 3, 2011. Forty-one healthy adults participated in a double-blind crossover using dried parsley tea and passionflower tea for seven nights each. Morning refreshment averaged 3.37 with parsley and 3.49 with passionflower, a difference below the study's significance threshold. Subjective sleep quality favored passionflower and the other reported sleep comparisons were unclear. The study included no inert or no-tea comparison, validated daytime sleepiness score, or Parkinson cohort. [Primary methods and Table 1](https://academy.miloa.eu/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Passionflower-subjective-sleep-quality.pdf) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21294203/)

## Parsley sauce meal and bowel symptoms

A 2014 education study identifies this exact article in its description of a Christmas edition that featured invented studies. That characterization adds a serious provenance concern. No independent validation of the reported observations was found. [Original educational study, section 4.2, p. 108](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1067559.pdf#page=6)

Grønbæk and Jensen published December 3, 2012. Ten of 18 healthy football players returned questionnaires, including one who did not eat the study meal. In the preceding and following 24 hours, the median stool frequency went from one to two (p = .02), and median abdominal pain from one to three on a 10-point scale (p = .03). The uncontrolled meal included fried bacon, parsley sauce, potatoes and alcohol. The authors attributed the changes primarily to the high fat intake. The report gives no isolated parsley effect or clinical constipation estimate. [Original Tables 1–2](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/234049608_Effect_of_fried_bacon_and_parsley_sauce_on_gastrointestinal_symptoms_in_healthy_old_boys_footballer) · [Aarhus University record](https://pure.au.dk/portal/en/publications/effekt-af-stegt-fl%C3%A6sk-med-persillesovs-p%C3%A5-mave-tarm-symptomer-hos/)
