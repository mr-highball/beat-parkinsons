# Tart cherry preparations and exercise fatigue

## Cherry skin capsules and resistance exercise recovery

Levers and colleagues randomly assigned 23 resistance-trained men to freeze-dried Montmorency skin powder or placebo. Performance recovery showed no clear group-by-time difference in isokinetic work (p=.75). Overall pressure-provoked soreness was inconclusive (p=.20), with a favorable change at one tested site. Two 2014 conference abstracts strongly match this cohort. The product pooled skins left from juice processing, with no subjective Parkinson fatigue measure. The product supplier supported the work; the article also declares consulting. [Original publication](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4647629/) · [Local factual text](Source-Text/2015-Levers-Cherry-Skin-Strength-Extract.txt)

## Cherry skin capsule and half marathon performance

A separate Levers trial assigned 27 runners or triathletes to powdered freeze-dried cherry skin or placebo. Finish time was reported as 13% faster with cherry (p=.001). The predicted-versus-actual pace comparison was inconclusive (p=.091). Post-race between-group soreness comparisons were inconclusive, and change from prerun at one site was smaller with placebo. The same cohort likely produced two 2014 abstracts with split endpoints. Race performance and pressure-provoked soreness differ from the frozen Parkinson fatigue endpoint. [Original publication](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4880859/) · [Local factual text](Source-Text/2016-Levers-Cherry-Skin-Running-Extract.txt)

## Tart cherry concentrate and exercise tolerance

Keane and colleagues compared a single dose of Montmorency cherry concentrate with a matched placebo in ten trained male cyclists. Time to exhaustion during severe cycling was 772 seconds (SEM 34) with cherry and 733 (SEM 34) with placebo, p=.323. On a separate trial, peak power over the first 20 seconds of a subsequent sprint was 363 versus 330 W (p=.034), and total 60-second sprint work was 21 versus 19 kJ (p=.021). These are acute exercise-performance outcomes, not a subjective Parkinson fatigue assessment. The published abstract says 30 mL, while the accepted manuscript's methods and discussion say 60 mL; the administered dose is unresolved. The intervention was concentrated juice, not dried whole cherries. [Original publication](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.13088) · [Local factual text](Source-Text/2018-Keane-Cherry-Cycling-Extract.txt)

## Montmorency powder and a 15 km cycling time trial

Morgan and colleagues first published January 8, 2019. In a crossover, trained cyclists took capsules of Montmorency cherry powder or dextrose before a 15 km time trial. Completion time was 1,506 seconds (SD 86) with cherry and 1,580 (SD 102) with placebo, p=.004. The outcome was sports performance, with no Parkinson fatigue scale. Capsule preparation differs from eating dried whole cherries. [Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-018-04058-6) · [Local factual text](Source-Text/2019-Morgan-Cherry-Cycling-Extract.txt)

## Freeze dried cherry powder and cycling time to exhaustion

Davis and Bellar reported a double-blind crossover in 12 healthy men. Freeze-dried Montmorency powder capsules and cellulose placebo preceded a cycle test capped at 30 minutes. Time to exhaustion was 21.46 versus 19.50 minutes (both SEM 1.51, paired p=.36). The inconclusive test addresses exercise performance and supplies no estimate of Parkinson fatigue. [Primary source](https://storage.imrpress.com/imr/journal/IJVNR/article/501337/1752833707150.pdf) · [Local factual text](Source-Text/2019-Davis-Bellar-Cherry-Exhaustion-Extract.txt)

## Rugby cherry crossover in the 2019 dissertation

Morehen's thesis was publicly deposited June 3, 2019. Chapter 7 describes the 11-player blinded placebo-controlled Montmorency juice crossover also reported in the 2020 article. The thesis and paper are one trial; see the later Table 1 record for the fatigue ratings.
