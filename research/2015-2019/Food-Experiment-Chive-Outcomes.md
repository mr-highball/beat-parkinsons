# Preclinical evidence related to the chive claims in 2015–2019

## Oral ferulic acid and rotarod latency

Nagarajan and colleagues first published this study on 10 April 2015. They randomized male C57BL/6 mice to five groups of six. Three groups received purified ferulic acid at 20, 40, or 80 mg/kg by intragastric intubation over seven days, starting before MPTP exposure. Figure 2 reports latency to fall from a rotating rod at 5, 10, and 20 rpm. MPTP shortened latency. Ferulic-acid groups generally stayed on the rod longer than the MPTP-only group, with differences among doses and speeds in the figure. The paper used the rod test for motor coordination.

A later composition analysis measured ferulic acid in common chive tissue. The mice received reagent-grade ferulic acid rather than a dried food preparation. A timed apparatus test gives no estimate of spontaneous fall frequency in people with Parkinson's for claim 0249.

## Allium mongolicum extract and intestinal transit

Chen and colleagues first published on 20 December 2019, with a January 2020 issue assignment. They authenticated fresh aerial parts of *Allium mongolicum*, extracted them with 50% ethanol, removed the solvent, and administered the dried extract suspended in saline in a loperamide mouse model. After nine days of oral treatment, Figure 1 and section 3.1 report fecal excretion 96.7% higher with 200 mg/kg and 46.3% higher with 100 mg/kg than in the loperamide control group (p < .01 and p < .05). Carbon-powder transit was 43.7% and 34.2% higher at the same doses (p < .001 and p < .05). Separate cell and isolated intestinal tissue assays used selected constituents.

The effect was directional for that extract and model. *A. mongolicum* is a different species from common chive, *A. schoenoprasum*. The experiment did not evaluate eating fresh common chives or constipation severity in people with Parkinson's. The animal methods and Figure 1 identify mice, despite an isolated reference to rats in the introduction.
