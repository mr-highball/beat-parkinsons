# Cilantro, blood pressure and orthostatic symptoms

## 2015 — PolySPice registry and coriander seed in curry

NCT02599272 was first posted November 6, 2015. The curry meals contained 6 or 12 g of a seven-spice mixture that included dried coriander seed powder. The registry's study description mentions 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure, but the structured outcomes omit it. The published postprandial redox report confirms that the powder came from coriander seeds and evaluates blood samples and reactive hyperemia. It does not report serial blood pressure or posture-provoked symptoms. Separate publications under the same protocol report post-meal glucose, appetite or gut microbiome outcomes. These reports concern mixed curries in healthy volunteers. [Official registry](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02599272) · [Local primary redox report](Source-Text/PMC6333854.txt) · [Primary appetite report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5920412/) · [Primary microbiome report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8163817/)

## 2018 — Two coriander seed powder blood-pressure reports

Zeb and colleagues report assigning 80 participants with hyperlipidemia and cardiovascular disease to garlic powder, coriander seed powder, the combination, or an unsupplemented control. With 2 g/day coriander seed powder, systolic pressure averaged 140.00 at baseline, 117.14 at day 40 and 121.42 mm Hg at day 60. Control means were 137.14, 135.71 and 132.85 mm Hg. The report states a significant treatment-by-time effect on systolic pressure and no significant effect on diastolic pressure. The sphygmomanometer method does not specify a posture protocol, and the paper supplies no standing symptoms. The methods call the design single-blind and the control placebo, although the control received no supplement. [Primary report, Methods and Table 3](https://applications.emro.who.int/imemrf/Pak_J_Pharm_Sci/Pak_J_Pharm_Sci_2018_31_5_1935_1941.pdf) · [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30150192/)

Iqbal and colleagues separately describe 32 participants with both type 2 diabetes and hypertension. The 2 g/day coriander-seed-powder group had mean systolic pressure of 175.63 at baseline and 156.88 mm Hg at day 40, versus 163.75 at both times in placebo. The accompanying diastolic means were 100.00 and 83.75 versus 98.75 and 90.63 mm Hg. The paper describes within-group changes and says the active treatments did not differ significantly from placebo. The unadjusted comparison starts with baseline differences. The two 2018 papers share an author and the same general design template, with distinct reported population criteria, sample sizes and means. Participant overlap cannot be ruled out from the articles. Neither reports Parkinson diagnoses or orthostatic measurements. [Primary report, Methods, Table 2 and abstract](https://juniperpublishers.com/artoaj/pdf/ARTOAJ.MS.ID.555971.pdf)

## 2018 — ACE inhibition by a fraction derived from fresh coriander leaves

Hussain and colleagues bought fresh coriander leaves and extracted multiple fractions. The flavonoid-rich fraction yielded 81.4% ACE inhibition in the assay and an IC50 of 28.91 ± 13.42 μg/mL. The paper measures inhibition of an isolated enzyme. It does not measure blood pressure or determine systemic exposure after eating intact or dried leaves. [Local licensed source, Sections 2.1, 2.2 and Table 1](Source-Text/PMC6276458.txt)

## 2019 — Oral extracts made from dried cilantro leaves increased rat urine output

Thuraisingam and colleagues shade-dried cilantro leaves and prepared solvent extracts from the powder. Wistar rats received the methanol or water extract orally, each at 400 mg/kg. The methanol group had the largest urine response among the two extract groups over six hours. The primary text identifies both leaf form and oral route. It reports urine volume and urinary electrolytes without an arterial pressure, standing or clinical symptom measurement. [Primary report, Sections III–VI and Figure 1](https://irjpms.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/IRJPMS-V2N5P61Y19.pdf)

## 2017 — Registered meal studies and ambulatory blood pressure

NCT03064958, first posted February 27, 2017, preregistered timed endothelial and metabolic measurements after a meal with mixed spices. NCT03064932, first posted the same day, separately registered the four-week mixed-spice diets and included 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure. Its nested meal challenge NCT03063320 was first posted February 24, 2017 and registered flow-mediated dilation and biochemical responses. No registry listed postural lightheadedness or a standing pressure comparison. Outcome papers for these two study families were released in 2020, 2021 and 2026. [Acute pilot registry](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03064958) · [Four-week diet registry](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03064932) · [Nested meal challenge registry](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03063320)

## 2018 — Inhaled coriander essential oil during relaxation

In the Russian-language study by Tonkovtseva and colleagues, 90 adults aged 50–90 with elevated systolic pressure received relaxation with or without linalool-rich coriander essential oil vapor. At ten minutes, systolic pressure was 156.70 to 139.10 mm Hg in 15 oil recipients and 157.10 to 154.50 in 20 controls. The authors also report a significant post-session arm comparison at 20 minutes (oil 143.50 and control 153.00 mm Hg, p < .05). The 30-minute arm comparison was not significant. A 2018 meeting abstract from the same research program combined coriander, clove and rose studies in 240 people. Its participant overlap with the original 90-person report is unresolved. These inhalation studies did not give cilantro orally or measure standing blood pressure.
