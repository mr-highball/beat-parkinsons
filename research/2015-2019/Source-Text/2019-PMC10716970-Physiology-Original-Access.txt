Swallowing and capsicum

S155, 1P-350. In anesthetized rats, acute capsaicin or acid contact provoked reflex swallows and blocking TRPV1 reduced that response. Swallows declined during prolonged chemical contact, and mechanically triggered swallows were fewer after 60 minutes. The electrical-stimulation threshold did not clearly change. A 2018 conference report and the 2020 Yoshihara paper describe the same research line, with closely matching design and results. Treat those reports as one family pending individual animal identifiers. The prolonged laryngeal exposure differs from eating ground cayenne, and the studies did not measure clinical Parkinson swallowing severity.
2020 original journal abstract: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32755305/

S270, 2P-487. A different laboratory compared locally delivered stimuli in anesthetized rats. The poster reports reflex swallows with capsaicin in mineral oil and with extra-virgin olive oil. Plain mineral oil and the separate olive-oil comparator did not trigger the response. This was a provoked reflex, without a Parkinson model, dietary intervention or clinical difficulty score. The authors did not report the oil cultivar or measure its oleocanthal concentration.
Independent official member listing: https://www.nips.ac.jp/faops2019/assets/dl/The_2nd_Poster_Presentation_Day.pdf

S249, 2P-357 reports sensitivity to capsaicin and food preferences in Japanese medical students. Cayenne pepper is named in a preference questionnaire. The authors did not measure clinical anxiety, falls or dysphagia. S147, 1P-297 uses isolated capsaicin in experimental pain pathways. The observed pain behavior was provoked in rats under a laboratory exposure, with no meal or Parkinson pain measurement. Other Capsicum or capsaicinoid matches concern laboratory or non-Parkinson outcomes.

Other genuine ingredient contexts

S117, 1P-115 compares daily dark chocolate with white chocolate in young adults using cognitive tasks. Participant counts and measures match the later 18-participant report. The tests did not measure anxiety. Publication: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6893800/

S188, 1P-547 reports a shorter reaction time after coffee in seven students relative to a control measurement. It did not measure fatigue severity. Other coffee-related records examine isolated caffeine or distinct physiological endpoints.

S121, 1P-135 studies white-skinned sweet potato extract and insulin or glucose outcomes. It does not study boiled sweet potato for fatigue or ordinary roasted potato for sleep. S280, 2P-545 matches a prior paper on 20 aging rats, a nutmeg extract and muscle weight. No tested fatigue, mood, constipation or sleep endpoint appears. Publication: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6311876/

S144, 1P-280 studies isolated rosmarinic acid in a Parkinson-related cell model. It records mitochondrial and protein-aggregation outcomes. It did not compare brewed lemon balm or measure depressive symptoms. S254, 2P-391 adds boiled eggs and other concurrent changes to a monkey feeding regimen for vomiting. S277, 2P-530 varies the speed of eating a fried chicken meal and measures glucose in students. Neither study isolates the corresponding frozen food preparation and Parkinson outcome.

Cherry is a researcher name in the identified records. Tomato is a reporter label. Date refers to timing or an autobiographical event. Potato appears in the sweet-potato botanical name. The source review keeps those contexts distinct from culinary exposures.
