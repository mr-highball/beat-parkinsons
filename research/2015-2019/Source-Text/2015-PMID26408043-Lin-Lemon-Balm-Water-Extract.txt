Title: A medicinal herb, Melissa officinalis L. ameliorates depressive-like behavior of rats in the forced swimming test via regulating the serotonergic neurotransmitter
PMID: 26408043
DOI: 10.1016/j.jep.2015.09.018
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26408043/
Author manuscript: https://academy.miloa.eu/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Lin-S.-H.-Sheen-L.-Y.-al-2015.pdf
First published: 2015-09-25 online. Journal of Ethnopharmacology 175:266–272, December issue.
Coverage: Original accepted manuscript Methods, Results and Figures 1–5.
Reviewed: 2026-11-18 UTC

The authors heated powdered freeze-dried leaves in water for a prolonged extraction, then clarified, filtered and freeze-dried the liquid. The resulting concentrate contained 12.0% rosmarinic acid versus 5.0% in the dried leaves. This preparation is water-derived and more concentrated than a household leaf infusion. The oral rat studies used six to eight animals per group. In the short schedule a dose-equivalent rosmarinic acid group provided a separate constituent comparison.

In the short comparison, water-extract and isolated rosmarinic acid groups showed lower forced-swim immobility versus water. The separate swimming and struggling measures did not show clear differences. In the ten-day comparison all three extract groups and fluoxetine had lower immobility (overall p=.03) and more swimming (overall p=.009), with no clear shift in struggling (overall p=.546). The authors reported no clear group difference in ten-day open-field locomotion and did not show those data. Serotonin-turnover estimates varied by brain region and dose. The highest extract group in Figure 5 shares the water-control letter in frontal cortex, amygdala and hippocampus. These regional data and behavioral comparisons leave the proposed causal pathway unproven.

Shorter swim immobility is a rodent assay outcome. The manuscript contains no diagnosed depression severity outcome and no Parkinson population.
