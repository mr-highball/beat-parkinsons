Title: Poster Sessions, 41st FEBS Congress
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7164006/
PMCID: PMC7164006
DOI: 10.1111/febs.13808
Publication year: 2016
Original published volume: https://www.bingol.edu.tr/documents/2016-The_FEBS_Journal%20%28abstract%20book%29.pdf
Coverage: All 301 published pages, printed pp. 127–427. The poster body contains 894 identified abstracts through p. 417, including miscellaneous and education identifiers. Distilled factual summary.

Measured dietary and experimental exposures are separate across posters. P-03.01.1-023 on p. 145 reports dietary olive-oil intake in a diabetes comparison. P-02.09.1-006 on p. 132 uses olive oil in laboratory medium. Neither supplies an anxiety response to cold-pressed olive oil.

P-02.02.2-022 on p. 214 supplies peanut oil throughout all rat groups and studies a different intervention in an experimental colon model. P-09.04.4-008 and P-09.04.4-017 on pp. 384 and 386 use corn oil as a vehicle in separate toxicology experiments. These reports give no comparison that isolates the shared oil as an intervention for a Parkinson symptom.

P-MIS-037 on p. 175 tests a turmeric constituent with a black pepper preparation in an encapsulation study. Other turmeric-constituent posters use cancer cell, injury, or toxicology models. P-05.01.1-026 on p. 159 tests rosemary in cell culture. P-05.01.1-021 on p. 158 concerns compounds associated with parsley and dill seed. P-02.08.5-032 on p. 333 assays an enzyme isolated from apricot seeds. These preparations and outcomes differ from the mapped culinary claims.

P-02.08.5-061 on p. 341 studies cyanidin chloride in an inflammation model and identifies blackberry, cranberry, red cabbage, onion and grape among reported sources of cyanidin. It does not compare those foods. P-01.02.2-005 on p. 206 tests a grape-skin extract in cells. The tomato studies on pp. 343 and 348 investigate plant or compositional traits. None provides an attributed Parkinson symptom response.

P-MIS-098 on p. 192 reports laboratory testing of the barley preparation Talbina and describes customary use during illness or bereavement as background. It does not measure human anxiety. Other barley posters describe plant responses or seed proteins.

The sage poster P-02.08.5-046 on p. 336 tests Salvia absconditiflora. The volume contains distinct instances of orange as dye or color, kale as a surname or place fragment, and cumin as a PDF line-splitting artifact in curcumin passages. The egg reference on p. 372 concerns developmental polarity. Trout and salmon appear as living research specimens. These contexts do not identify the corresponding culinary exposures. Dates in publication or method text do not identify date fruit.
