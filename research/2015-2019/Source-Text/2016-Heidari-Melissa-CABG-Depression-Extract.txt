Title: The effect of Melissa officinalis on depression in patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting
Source: https://journal.icns.org.ir/article-1-327-fa.html
Original PDF: https://journal.icns.org.ir/article-1-327-fa.pdf
Reference: Heidari and colleagues, Cardiovascular Nursing Journal 4(2):36–43, issue labeled Summer 2015.
First posted by journal: 2016-03-10.
Text coverage: Full original Persian Methods and Tables 1–2, inspected as rendered pages.

The randomized comparison assigned 80 patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting, 40 per group. Methods on printed p38 describe dried Melissa leaf capsules and matched starch placebo during one postoperative week and cite IRCT2014071918531N1. The official registry confirms the HADS depression outcome before and a week after intervention. It was filed retrospectively and has a not-randomized design field, in conflict with the paper. See Trials/IRCT2014071918531N1.txt.

On printed p40, Table 2 gives mean HADS depression scores before and after treatment. The Melissa arm changed from 11.48 (SD 2.23) to 7.28 (SD 2.06). The placebo arm changed from 10.98 (SD 2.28) to 8.73 (SD 2.92). The difference between the displayed changes is 1.95 points in favor of the capsule arm. This subtraction is descriptive. The table reports a post-treatment t value of 2.063 and p=.008. The test statistic and p-value cannot be reconciled as an ordinary two-group comparison. There is no interval for difference in change. Table 1 also reports prior hospitalization in 36 of 40 and 29 of 40, despite baseline similarity asserted in prose.

The authors describe part of a larger graduate study. A later anxiety and sleep paper shares investigators, population, 80-person allocation and dried-leaf formulation. That is a probable companion report. The separate endpoints should not be counted as independent clinical replication. This study uses dried leaf in capsules in a postoperative group outside Parkinson disease.
