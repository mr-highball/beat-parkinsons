## Food intake by swallowing status in the Italian dietary cohort

Cassani et al. published this analysis online June 15, 2017. It used the same 600 Parkinson’s cases and 600 matched controls previously reported by Barichella et al. Within Parkinson’s, Table 3 compares unadjusted intake from a 66-item food-frequency questionnaire for 72 people with self-reported swallowing disturbances and 528 without. The swallowing measure came from a UPDRS Part II item. The subgroup with swallowing disturbances reported 67 ± 83 versus 50 ± 35 g/day of potatoes (p = .002). The separate fried-potato row gives 3 ± 3 versus 3 ± 4 g/day and is marked nonsignificant. There is no baked-potato category. [Primary manuscript, Table 3, PDF pp. 16–17](https://cris.unibo.it/retrieve/handle/11585/670116/898507/CassaniE_JPARKRELDIS_2017_postprint.pdf#page=16) · [Earlier analysis of this cohort](Food-Experiment-Food-Followup.md#coffee-tea-and-constipation-in-an-italian-dietary-survey)

The same subgroup reported more plain unsweetened yogurt (88 ± 65 vs 47 ± 86 g/day, p < .001), sweetened yogurt (127 ± 85 vs 60 ± 63 g/day, p < .001), vegetable soup or purée (185 ± 81 vs 102 ± 82 g/day, p < .001), and milk puddings or custards (24 ± 24 vs 16 ± 18 g/day, p < .001). Reported oatmeal was lower (5 ± 9 vs 13 ± 20 g/day, p < .001). Reported olive oil was 37 ± 14 versus 29 ± 13 mL/day and coffee was 26 ± 43 versus 59 ± 47 mL/day (each p < .001). The olive oil grade and coffee preparation are unspecified. A higher intake in the swallowing-disturbance group may reflect dietary adaptation. The study measures intake according to existing symptoms and supplies no swallowing effect from consuming any specific food. The accessible accepted manuscript is CC BY-NC-ND. [Primary manuscript, Table 3](https://cris.unibo.it/retrieve/handle/11585/670116/898507/CassaniE_JPARKRELDIS_2017_postprint.pdf#page=16) · [Repository terms](https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/670116)

The questionnaire covered the prior 12 months. A 24-hour recall and food atlas helped verify portions. The authors report crude group comparisons for individual foods, separately from the energy-adjusted calculation of their Mediterranean diet score. The ON-GP histories were kept in a password-protected database. The public instrument descriptions leave the historical item coding and recipes unverified. An earlier analysis at the same institute identified disease duration and dementia as correlates of swallowing symptoms without estimating effects of food. [Primary manuscript methods](https://cris.unibo.it/retrieve/handle/11585/670116/898507/CassaniE_JPARKRELDIS_2017_postprint.pdf) · [2009 instrument description](../2005-2009/Food-and-Nutrition.md#2009--food-frequency-instrument-used-in-the-later-milan-parkinson-cohort) · [2014 clinical-factor study](../2010-2014/Food-Experiment-Choking-Followup.md#clinical-factors-associated-with-swallowing-symptoms-in-milan)

## Low-fat and ketogenic meal plans with measured symptom outcomes

Phillips et al. published this eight-week randomized trial on August 11, 2018. Of 47 randomized patients, 44 started and 38 completed the assigned diet. The ketogenic versus low-fat difference in mean change was −3.59 points on MDS-UPDRS Part I (p < .001), including descriptive item differences of −0.50 for fatigue, −0.45 for daytime sleepiness, and −0.27 for cognitive impairment. The Part III change was −6.27 on the ketogenic diet and −8.58 on the low-fat diet (p = .055 for the comparison). Table 3 reports worsened tremor and/or rigidity in 12 of 24 ketogenic versus 3 of 23 low-fat participants in weeks 1–4, and 7 of 24 versus 5 of 23 in weeks 5–8. The adverse event combines tremor and rigidity. The article has a CC BY-NC-ND license. [Primary report, Tables 2–3](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mds.27390)

The original meal plans name several foods without reporting food-specific outcomes. The ketogenic plan includes virgin coconut oil in a tea or coffee preparation, extra virgin olive oil in assorted meals, and coconut oil in several recipes. The low-fat plan includes canola oil of unspecified grade, potatoes, sweet potatoes and blueberries in its shopping lists and recipes. Both plans include cinnamon in recipes and permit optional unsweetened tea or coffee and red wine. The intervention varies multiple foods and nutrients together, and the reported symptom outcomes are by assigned pattern. ## Chinese clinical report on a ketogenic diet

The journal dates 蒋娟’s (Jiang Juan’s) *生酮饮食治疗帕金森病39例临床效果观察* to May 15, 2018, volume 38, issue 3, pp. 235–237. Its primary abstract describes 78 outpatients with Parkinson’s at Haimen People’s Hospital, randomly assigned 39 per group to standard drug treatment with or without a ketogenic diet for six months. The six-month means ± SD for ketogenic plus drug treatment versus drug treatment alone were 38.38 ± 6.35 versus 44.36 ± 5.56 on the total UPDRS, 15.31 ± 3.38 versus 17.41 ± 3.43 on the daily-living component, and 16.38 ± 3.48 versus 19.77 ± 3.26 on motor function. The abstract reports p < .05 for each. Two participants withdrew from the ketogenic arm and four controls changed treatment regimens. The abstract gives no allocation method, analysis denominators, menu, or individual-food result. [Primary journal abstract, publication date and citation](https://ntyx.cbpt.cnki.net/portal/journal/portal/client/paper/fb8e9aa9612ffb5c878179c4d125912a) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.16424/j.cnki.cn32-1807/r.2018.03.023)

## Standardized liquid meal in gastric MRI

The May 3, 2019 Korean MRI study analyzed baseline scans of 38 people with idiopathic Parkinson’s who drank the same 400 mL, 400 kcal Nucare liquid meal. The analysis compares participants with and without dyspeptic symptoms and found slower gastric emptying among those with early satiety or dyspepsia. The primary text mentions pudding and scrambled egg as meals used in cited studies. This cohort received the Nucare meal, and its results estimate no food or ingredient comparison. [Primary article and MRI protocol](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0216396) · [Local CC BY article](Source-Text/PMC6499432.txt)

## Carbohydrate restriction and cognitive outcomes

Krikorian et al. published this eight-week pilot on August 6, 2019. Eighteen people with Parkinson’s and mild cognitive impairment were enrolled, and seven participants per arm completed the randomized comparison. The low-carbohydrate arm targeted 20 g per day of carbohydrates from vegetables and excluded fruit, grains and sweets. The high-carbohydrate arm had no restrictions on carbohydrate sources. Food diaries informed nutrient totals, with no individual food-by-outcome estimate. Compared with the high-carbohydrate group, the low-carbohydrate group improved on lexical access (p = .02, Cohen’s f = 0.76) and memory (p = .01, f = 0.87). The group comparison for memory interference gave p = .06. The authors reported no motor effect on MDS-UPDRS III (34.5 vs 26.5, adjusted p = .42) or either finger-tapping test. The article has a CC BY-NC-ND license. [Primary report, methods and results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8288565/) · [Trial registry cited by the paper](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00777010)
