# Kale options in a Parkinson dietary trial

Phillips et al. published their randomized pilot on 11 August 2018, comparing eight weeks of low-fat and ketogenic dietary patterns in Parkinson's disease. Of 47 randomized (23 low-fat, 24 ketogenic), 44 started and 38 completed; the investigators used regression imputation for missing primary outcomes and blinded examining neurologists to diet. The ketogenic group had a 0.70-point greater decrease in the broad MDS-UPDRS Part I urinary-problems item than the low-fat group. This is the reported *between-arm difference in changes*, not a count of urgency episodes; the paper does not give separate arm-level urinary item changes or an item-specific confidence interval or p value. Table 2 reports Part I totals, not individual urinary values: changes −4.58 ± 2.17 ketogenic versus −0.99 ± 3.63 low-fat, between-group p < .001. The supplement's sensitivity Table S1 concerns aggregate Parts I–IV, not individual urinary items. [Primary article, results and Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6175383/) · [Supplementary appendix](https://pmc-oa-opendata.s3.amazonaws.com/PMC6175383.1/MDS-33-1306-s001.docx)

Both provided meal plans contain kale as an alternative to spinach. The low-fat green smoothie (printed p. 55) lists 60 g spinach or kale leaves blended with fruit and water, without a cooking step; the ketogenic warm kale salad (printed p. 97) lists 60 g kale or spinach leaves and sautés them. Other mixed meals also give the substitution. These options do not establish which leaf a participant chose, nor actual kale consumption or a kale-only contrast. Participants checked off completion of meals instead of recording food diaries; ketones provided indirect pattern adherence. Many ingredients and nutrients differed simultaneously between plans. Therefore the urinary result is **related evidence only** for claim 0797, not evidence that raw kale reduces urinary urgency frequency. It does not test roasted or steamed kale against the other frozen endpoints.
