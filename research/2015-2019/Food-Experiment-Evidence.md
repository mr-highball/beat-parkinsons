# Food experiment evidence for 2015–2019

## Green tea and fatigue in Parkinson’s disease

Chen and colleagues studied daily brewed green tea for three months in a single-arm pilot. Fourteen of 15 people with Parkinson’s completed it. Participants were instructed to steep one tea bag in one cup of boiling water for four minutes and drink three cups daily. Table 1 gives a mean Fatigue Severity Scale score of 28.4 (SD 13.0, range 13–55) at baseline and 32.1 (SD 14.3, range 12–55) at three months. The observed 3.7-point rise is in the direction of [0948](../../experiments/1000_see_what_sticks_food/statements/0948.txt). The table gives no significance marker, exact p-value, or paired-change variance for fatigue. The small uncontrolled cohort leaves the tea effect uncertain. The study gives no separate handwriting or olfactory-impairment estimate. [Chen et al. 2015, methods and Table 1](https://doi.org/10.4236/jbbs.2015.56020) · [Local CC BY full text and Table 1](Source-Text/2015-Chen-Brewed-Green-Tea.txt#L164) · [First public cohort report](../2010-2014/Food-Experiment-Food-Followup.md#first-report-of-the-brewed-green-tea-parkinson-pilot)

### Dissertation report of the same green tea cohort

Chen’s Iowa State dissertation was awarded in 2013. Its repository metadata lists an embargo through July 30, 2015, and an archived December 2017 Iowa State page shows a public PDF download link. Chapter 5 Table 1 adds median Fatigue Severity Scale scores of 27 at baseline and 30.5 at three months, with the same means and ranges as the journal paper and no significance marker. The chapter also reports worse PDQ-39 mobility and UPDRS activities-of-daily-living subscores alongside a favorable motor examination score. These uncontrolled changes address separate outcomes. The dissertation’s copyright page reserves all rights. [Iowa State dissertation, Chapter 5 and Table 1](https://doi.org/10.31274/ETD-180810-3371) · [Repository metadata](https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/server/api/core/items/43d0092e-7567-4f2c-9a6b-51bec8a697aa) · [Archived December 2017 download page](https://web.archive.org/web/20171207011949id_/http://lib.dr.iastate.edu:80/etd/13626/)

## Flaxseed-derived omega-3 plus vitamin E

A 12-week double-blind trial randomized 60 people with Parkinson’s to a combination of flaxseed-derived omega-3 and vitamin E or placebo. The combination group had a more favorable change in the total UPDRS. The trial evaluated two supplements together and did not isolate culinary cold-pressed flaxseed oil or establish an effect on an individual symptom in this experiment. [Trial record and abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28342967/)

A second 12-week randomized trial, IRCT2017061234497N1, enrolled 40 people between April and August 2017 and published online in December 2018. Enrollment began after the earlier 60-person study had been published online. The second study reported favorable changes in total UPDRS and selected inflammatory and metabolic gene-expression measures after the combined supplement. It does not give an isolated cold-pressed flaxseed oil or individual pain result for `0639`. [Second trial methods and results, PMID 30554092](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0303846718304633) · [Earlier trial publication date](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28342967/)

## Sesame oil oral swabs in nursing-home dysphagia

In 2017, Ogawa and Nishio reported a two-week oral-cleaning intervention for 13 tube-fed nursing-home residents with dysphagia, including one person with Parkinson’s disease. Staff used gauze swabs soaked in roasted sesame oil for seven residents and unroasted sesame salad oil for six. Allocation depended on whether oral candidiasis was suspected. Pooled total tongue bacterial counts decreased by 0.56 log units in 12 evaluable residents. The report does not identify which oil the Parkinson’s participant received or measure improvement in swallowing. This was a topical oral-care intervention with microbial and mouth-health outcomes. [Primary report, pp. 387–388 and Tables 2–4](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jsg/32/3/32_386/_pdf)

## Olive and coconut oil for bowel symptoms in hospital staff

A 2017 Japanese conference abstract describes 35 hospital staff assigned to consume olive oil or coconut oil at 15 mL per day for two weeks. About 60% of participants who reported constipation at baseline reported improvement in each group, and 6% of the full sample moved toward normal stool form in the bowel diaries. The authors reported no statistically clear stool-outcome difference. The abstract provides no Parkinson subgroup, oil-free arm, or olive oil grade, which limits its relevance to `0072`. [Primary conference abstract P-1183](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jspen/32/supplement/32_695/_pdf/-char/ja)

## Nanomicelle curcumin and sleep quality

A double-blind randomized study reported a favorable overall sleep-quality result after nanomicelle curcumin, with no clear fatigue benefit. The formulation differs from grated culinary turmeric and the sleep measure does not establish an effect on daytime sleepiness. [Maghbooli et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.15171/icnj.2019.26)

## Dietary variation and nonmotor scores

A multicentre cross-sectional survey compared dietary practices and nonmotor measures across groups with Parkinson’s. It recorded selected spices but did not isolate the specified preparation or identify a clear dietary association with nonmotor scores. The design could not establish the direction of individual culinary effects. [Study report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6312592/)
