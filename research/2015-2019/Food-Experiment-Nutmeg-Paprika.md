# Nutmeg and paprika evidence

## Powdered nutmeg infusion and student insomnia

Qurrota A’yunin’s 2017 Airlangga thesis abstract describes a single-group pre–post study of an oral powdered-seed infusion in students aged 19–24 who reported insomnia. The thesis abstract reports a decline in Insomnia Rating Scale scores after one week (paired p = .001). The study lacked a control arm and provides no Parkinson comparison. [Thesis record and author abstract](https://repository.unair.ac.id/65690/)

## Injected capsaicin and involuntary movements in lesioned rats

Zhao and colleagues first published their 6-OHDA rat study on August 31, 2017. The authors report improved locomotor measures and reduced abnormal involuntary movements after intraperitoneal capsaicin. Clinical levodopa-induced dyskinesia after eating paprika remains unmeasured. [Primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28861768/)

## Oral capsaicin stimulation and saliva output

The controlled experiment in 18 healthy adults reported greater saliva output during brief oral capsaicin stimulation than during distilled water stimulation. Participants spat out the solution and saliva. This was an evoked secretion measurement and the intervention was isolated capsaicin. [Primary report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6388832/)

## Ground nutmeg in milk and one primary insomnia case

Prathibha and colleagues described a woman age 54 with longstanding primary insomnia who took a preparation of 2 g powdered Myristica fragrans boiled in milk and water nightly for 14 days. Regensburg Insomnia Scale scores in Table 1 were 21 at baseline, 18 on day 8, 10 on day 15 and 9 after a month. Daytime drowsiness was reported after one week. There was no comparator and no Parkinson diagnosis. The design cannot separate the powder from the milk preparation, natural variation or expectancy. Original paper: https://ijrap.net/admin/php/uploads/2191_pdf.pdf. ## Accidental ground nutmeg intake and next-morning drowsiness

Roeters van Lennep and colleagues describe a 37-year-old woman with confusion and drowsiness the morning after inadvertently adding substantially more nutmeg than intended to an asparagus dish. She also consumed alcohol. The report describes suspected accidental intoxication, with no parallel comparator or verified Parkinson sample. It documents daytime drowsiness in a food context at excessive exposure and leaves an effect of ordinary use unresolved.
