Title: de Rus Jacquet et al. — Pikuni-Blackfeet traditional medicinal plants for Parkinson-related symptoms
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source kind: Ethnopharmacological field interviews and cellular assay study
DOI: 10.1016/j.jep.2017.01.001
PMID: 28088492
PMCID: PMC6149223
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28088492/
Publication date: 2017-01-11
Date basis: Journal of Ethnopharmacology publication in 2017 documenting contemporary reservation interviews; intergenerational oral tradition age is not dated by document.
Reviewed: 2026-11-25 UTC
Location inspected: PubMed abstract and the displayed PMC author manuscript, including Table 1 and sections 3.1 and 3.2.

Community/context: Interviews with healers and local community members on the Blackfeet Indian Reservation in Western Montana concerning contemporary Pikuni-Blackfeet herbal practices for Parkinson-related and central nervous system symptoms.
Exposure and reported symptom: The PubMed abstract reports 26 plants used for several ailments and the PMC manuscript abstract reports 19. Section 3.1 identifies a more specific set of 10 plants across eight botanical families for Parkinson-related symptoms. The source versions leave the broader inventory count unresolved. During the laboratory phase, nine selected plants supplied ten extracts. Water extracts of Allium sativum (garlic) cloves, Trifolium pratense flowers, and Amelanchier arborea berries protected against rotenone neurotoxicity, while Allium sativum and Amelanchier arborea extracts alleviated paraquat-induced dopaminergic cell death, and 7 of 10 tested extracts activated Nrf2 transcriptional responses in primary cortical astrocytes. Specific individual Parkinson symptoms (e.g., rest tremor vs bradykinesia) and culinary human doses were not clinically measured.
Reported direction: The interviews describe customary uses for symptoms that can overlap Parkinson disease. Cell experiments found neuroprotective activity for separate extracts, including garlic. No clinical change in a Parkinson symptom was recorded.
Evidence and limits: Combines qualitative community interviews with primary astrocyte/dopaminergic cell-culture assays. No human intervention arm, clinical Parkinson symptom rating, or in vivo behavioral trial is included.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed ethnobotanical field study with voucher plant collection and laboratory follow-up (DOI 10.1016/j.jep.2017.01.001).
Efficacy evidence: Neither traditional community attribution nor cell-culture Nrf2/toxin-protection assays establish human clinical efficacy of dietary garlic for Parkinson symptoms.
The main table mentions sage in a peppermint preparation. Section 3.2 traces separate accounts of unspecified Salvia used for pain or paralysis and discusses common sage in a different traditional indication and in cell experiments. These references provide a cultural and experimental trail for sage. They supply no Parkinson pain or rigidity comparison for dietary sage.
Access and follow-up: The PMC manuscript links the original supporting tables at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/instance/6149223/bin/NIHMS878506-supplement-3.doc. Check those tables and the publisher version before reconciling the conflicting inventory totals.
