Title: East Asian (Shi / Kaki / Gam) traditional culinary, cooling-astringent, and persimmon-bezoar cautionary folklore for Diospyros kaki
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4400622/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2015
Date basis: Peer-reviewed publication dates (Iwamuro et al., World J Gastrointest Endosc 2015, PMC4400622; Hibino et al., 2003, PMID:12619669; Jabri et al., 2021, PMID:33872072); East Asian culinary and TCM Shi traditions span over a millennium.
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Dual traditional observation: cooling and astringent (anti-diarrheal/se) fruit properties paired with longstanding East Asian dietary warnings against eating unripe, astringent, unpeeled, or excessive persimmons on an empty stomach due to hard gastric stone (diospyrobezoar) formation and abdominal pain/obstruction.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed clinical gastroenterology and physiological literature inspected during Round 6 review (PMC4400622; PMC7549533; PMID:12619669; PMID:33872072).
Efficacy evidence: Traditional dietary-safety and ethnobotanical record only; strictly distinguishes Diospyros kaki fruit from separate TCM non-fruit parts (Shi Ye leaf extracts and Shi Di calyx).

In Chinese (Shi), Japanese (Kaki), and Korean (Gam) food and traditional medical cultures, Oriental persimmon (Diospyros kaki) occupies a prominent seasonal autumn-winter role as both sweet fully ripe or sun-dried fruit (hoshigaki/gotgam) and a classical cooling, astringent medicinal food (Iwamuro et al., 2015, PMC4400622; Hibino et al., 2003, PMID:12619669). Traditional Chinese materia medica carefully separates plant parts: the calyx (Shi Di) is used for hiccups, the leaves (Shi Ye) are brewed for vascular and heat-clearing applications, the white crystalline sugar frost on dried persimmons (Shi Shuang) is used for sore throats and dry cough, and astringent tannins in the fruit pulp and skin (shibuol) have classical astringent (se) anti-diarrheal properties corroborated in modern rat transit models (Jabri et al., 2021, PMID:33872072). Crucially, East Asian folklore and modern regional gastroenterology alike carry longstanding warnings against eating unripe, astringent, unpeeled, or large quantities of persimmons—especially on an empty stomach or in older adults with slow gastric emptying—because soluble shibuol tannins polymerize with gastric acid into rock-hard concretions (diospyrobezoars) capable of causing severe epigastric pain, gastric ulcers, and mechanical bowel obstruction (Liu et al., 2020, PMC7549533). Traditional usage supplies no clinical evidence of benefit for Parkinson disease symptoms.
