Title: Ayurvedic (Amra) and tropical ethnomedicinal traditions of Mangifera indica fruit pulp versus mango leaf, bark, and kernel uses
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5804368/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2017
Date basis: Peer-reviewed open-access ethnopharmacology review publication year (Ediriweera et al., Evid Based Complement Alternat Med 2017, PMC5804368; fruit phytochemistry contextualized with Maldonado-Celis et al., Front Plant Sci 2019, PMC6807195).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Favorable traditional digestive, mild laxative, and restorative tonic uses of ripe mango pulp; no traditional clinical Parkinson's efficacy claim.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed open-access ethnopharmacology and phytochemistry literature inspected during Round 5 review (PMC5804368, PMID:29456572; PMC6807195, PMID:31681339).
Efficacy evidence: Traditional culinary-medicinal and ethnobotanical context only; strictly separates edible fruit pulp from mango leaf tea/extracts (Zynamite/Stadice), stem bark, and seed kernels, and does not establish Parkinson's disease clinical efficacy.

Across South Asia (classical Ayurveda, where Mangifera indica L. is known as Amra), Unani medicine, Southeast Asia, and tropical regions worldwide, ripe mango fruit pulp (Pakwaphala) has been consumed for millennia both as a staple seasonal fruit and as a restorative food-medicine (Ediriweera et al., 2017, PMC5804368; Maldonado-Celis et al., 2019, PMC6807195). Classical Ayurvedic materia medica classifies ripe mango pulp as sweet, nourishing (balya/brimhana), cooling, stomachic, and mildly laxative, used traditionally for debility, heat exhaustion, dyspepsia, and sluggish bowels, whereas unripe fruit is prepared differently (e.g., spiced cooling drinks such as aam panna). Crucially for modern evidence appraisal, ethnobotanical literature assigns distinct pharmacological roles to non-fruit tree parts: mango leaves are brewed as folk teas for exhaustion, fatigue, and diabetes (the ethnobotanical basis for modern standardized mangiferin-rich leaf extracts such as Zynamite and Stadice), while stem bark and seed kernels are used as astringents for diarrhea and inflammation. Because mango fruit flesh is dominated by gallotannins, gallic acid, carotenoids, and fiber rather than concentrated leaf mangiferin, traditional leaf-tea and bark uses cannot be transferred to culinary raw or dried mango fruit, and no traditional text establishes controlled efficacy in Parkinson's disease.
