Title: Traditional Egyptian, Pakistani, and Chinese Ethnobotanical Uses of Cucumis melo Fruit, Seeds, and Pedicels
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30857984/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2019
Date basis: Publication year of primary peer-reviewed ethnopharmacological study (PMID:30857984), synthesizing companion records PMID:26647620 (2015) and PMID:22543170 (2012).
Reviewed: 2026-11-25 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional cooling, anti-inflammatory, renal/diuretic, and (for non-edible fruit pedicels) prokinetic uses; no traditional Parkinson motor-symptom indication.
Authenticity: Traceable to peer-reviewed ethnopharmacological literature (J Ethnopharmacol 2019; Acta Pol Pharm 2015; J Ethnopharmacol 2012).
Efficacy evidence: Traditional practice and ethnopharmacological context only; strictly separates non-edible pedicel/seed traditions from culinary raw cantaloupe flesh and does not establish clinical PD efficacy.

Peer-reviewed ethnopharmacological accounts document regionally distinct traditional practices involving Cucumis melo varieties. In Egypt, netted muskmelon/cantaloupe (C. melo var. cantalupensis and var. reticulatus) is traditionally regarded as a cooling anti-inflammatory food-medicine for liver 'hot' inflammation, cough, eczema, and urinary tract complaints (Ezzat et al., 2019). In Pakistan, C. melo fruit and especially melon seeds are traditionally used as nutritive diuretics and renal tonics (Ullah et al., 2015). In classical Ming-dynasty Chinese medicine, the bitter non-edible fruit stalk/pedicel (Pedicellus Melo) was formulated into rectal suppositories for abdominal distention and constipation (Gao et al., 2012). Across these traditions, plant part and preparation differ sharply from eating raw cantaloupe flesh, and none provide controlled evidence for Parkinson's motor or non-motor symptoms.
