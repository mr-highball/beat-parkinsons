Title: Classical Chinese culinary heritage of Zizania latifolia seeds (gumi / diaohu) and botanical separation from smut-infected jiaobai stems and leaf extracts
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6100627/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2018
Date basis: Peer-reviewed review publication date (Yan et al., Molecules 2018, PMID:29958396 / PMC6100627); cites classical Chinese historical grain records spanning >3,000 years (e.g., Zhou Li "Six Grains").
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Historical East Asian staple cereal and cooling/satiating traditional food use of Zizania latifolia seeds; strictly separated from fungal-swollen culm vegetable (jiaobai/gaosun) and dried leaf extracts; no Parkinson clinical claim.
Authenticity: Traceable peer-reviewed open-access review (PMC6100627) inspected during Round 7 review.
Efficacy evidence: Historical ethnobotanical and food-taxonomy record only; does not establish clinical efficacy in Parkinson disease.

Historical community context:
In East Asia, seeds of Manchurian/Chinese wild rice (Zizania latifolia, known classically as gumi or diaohu) were harvested and boiled as an esteemed cereal grain for over 3,000 years and listed among the ancient "Six Grains" in classical Chinese dietary records (reviewed in Yan et al., Molecules 2018, PMC6100627). Traditional Chinese dietary texts described cooked gumi as cooling, thirst-quenching, and nourishing for the spleen and stomach.

Critical botanical and culinary boundary:
Over the past millennium, East Asian cultivation shifted predominantly to Z. latifolia plants symbiotically infected by the smut fungus Ustilago esculenta, which suppresses flowering/seed formation and induces a tender swollen aquatic stem vegetable (jiaobai / gaosun in China, makomotake in Japan, "water bamboo"). Separately, modern Korean nutraceutical trials (e.g., Ahn et al. 2024, PMC11171771) use enzyme-treated ethanol extracts of dried Z. latifolia LEAVES standardized for tricin.

Evidence limits:
Both fungal-infected culm vegetables and dried leaf extracts are chemically and botanically distinct from culinary boiled/cooked Zizania seeds (wild rice grain), and neither traditional gumi records nor modern non-grain extracts establish human Parkinson disease symptom efficacy.
