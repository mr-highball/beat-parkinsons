Title: European ethnobotanical traditions for sour cherry (Prunus cerasus) and sweet cherry (Prunus avium)
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4917955/
Source kind: Ethnobotanical field survey and traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2016-06-17
Date basis: Published June 17, 2016 in Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine (Egea et al., PMID:27333772, PMC4917955), cross-linked with Southern Romanian ethnopediatric survey Petran et al., 2024 (PMID:38841372, PMC11150775).
Reviewed: 2026-11-25 UTC
Reported direction: Customary food-medicine and folk-remedy use (digestive/nutraceutical fruit preparations; cherry stalks for urinary ailments); no Parkinson symptom efficacy measurement
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed ethnobotanical field surveys with quantitative Use Value indices (Prunus cerasus UV general = 0.44, top species in Alta Valle del Reno, Italy; n=326 Romanian mothers in Petran 2024)
Efficacy evidence: Documents cultural culinary and herbal familiarity with edible Prunus cerasus and P. avium; supplies no controlled clinical Parkinson symptom evidence

Community/context: Field ethnobotanical interviews in the Alta Valle del Reno mountain borderland between Tuscany and Emilia-Romagna, Italy (Egea et al., 2016, PMID:27333772, PMC4917955) identified sour cherry (Prunus cerasus L.) as the single most culturally important species for traditional plant-based beverages with perceived nutraceutical/digestive properties (UV general 0.44, UV effective 0.413). Separately, a 2024 survey of 326 mothers in Southern Romania (Petran et al., 2024, PMID:38841372, PMC11150775) recorded folk use of Prunus avium (sweet cherry) stalks/stems for pediatric urinary tract ailments.
Botanical boundary: Edible culinary cherries (Prunus cerasus, Prunus avium) are distinct from Himalayan wild cherry (Prunus cerasoides, archived in research/2025-2029/Cultural-and-Experiential/Traditional-Practices/2025-Khadka-Prunus-Cerasoides-Nepal-Use-Survey.txt) and strictly distinct from poisonous cyanogenic cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus, PMID:36355976), winter cherry (Withania somnifera), acerola, and coffee cherry.
Evidence and limits: Customary dietary and stalk-infusion uses provide cultural provenance only and do not measure Parkinson disease symptom changes.
