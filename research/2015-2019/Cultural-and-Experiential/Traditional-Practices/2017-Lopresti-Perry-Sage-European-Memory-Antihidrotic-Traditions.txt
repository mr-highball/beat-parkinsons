Title: Classical European and Mediterranean herbal traditions of Salvia officinalis for memory, alertness, mood, and excess sweating/salivation
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5318325/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2017
Date basis: Peer-reviewed open-access review publication years (Lopresti, Drugs R D 2017, PMC5318325; Perry & Howes, CNS Neurosci Ther 2011, PMC6493900); European herbal texts cited date to the 16th-17th centuries (Gerard 1597, Culpeper 1652).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Favorable traditional reputation for sharpening memory and senses, easing nervous distress, and drying excess perspiration/secretions; accompanied by modern neurotoxic thujone cautions for concentrated oil.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed open-access ethnopharmacology reviews inspected during Round 6 review (PMC5318325; PMC6493900).
Efficacy evidence: Historical ethnobotanical record only; separates traditional folklore from controlled clinical evidence and explicitly distinguishes Salvia officinalis from Spanish sage (Salvia lavandulaefolia).

Common culinary sage (Salvia officinalis L., from Latin salvare, 'to heal/save') holds a centuries-old reputation in Greco-Roman and European herbals—including John Gerard (1597) and Nicholas Culpeper (1652)—as a cognitive and nervine herb believed to 'quicken the senses and memory', strengthen weak nerves, calm low spirits, and reduce excessive sweating and oral/throat secretions (Lopresti, 2017, PMC5318325; Perry & Howes, 2011, PMC6493900). Traditional culinary uses rely on small quantities of fresh, dried, or ground leaf in savory dishes or aqueous teas, whereas modern clinical trials mostly evaluate standardized ethanolic or fresh-tip extracts—and many European cognitive trials actually tested the distinct low-thujone species Salvia lavandulaefolia (Spanish sage). Modern toxicology emphasizes that concentrated S. officinalis essential oil contains convulsant thujone and camphor capable of triggering seizures (Lachenmeier & Uebelacker, 2010, PMID:20727933; Lachenmeier & Walch, 2012, PMID:22353306). Historical tradition does not establish PD clinical efficacy.
