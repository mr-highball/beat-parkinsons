Title: Patagonian Fragaria chiloensis foodways and Alpine Fragaria vesca traditional leaf/fruit distinctions
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6722795/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2019-07-26
Date basis: Published open-access review date (Foods 2019 Jul 26; PMC6722795; cross-referencing Alpine ethnobotany PMC9693256 published 2022-11-03).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Contextual culinary and ethnobotanical record: documents traditional culinary and functional consumption of Chilean wild strawberry fruit (Fragaria chiloensis, maternal progenitor of modern garden strawberry F. x ananassa) in Patagonia/Chile alongside European Alpine use of woodland strawberry (F. vesca) leaf/root infusions as astringents, with no historical Parkinson symptom-treatment record.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed open-access ethnobotanical and food-science reviews (PMC6722795; PMC9693256) verified via NCBI PMC during Round 7 review.
Efficacy evidence: Historical foodway and botanical-lineage context only; separates edible ripe receptacle fruit from traditional astringent leaf/root herbal infusions and establishes no clinical efficacy for PD.

Peer-reviewed ethnobotanical literature (Foods 2019; PMC6722795) documents the traditional gathering and culinary use of Patagonian berries including white Chilean strawberry (Fragaria chiloensis, one of the two octoploid parent species hybridized in 18th-century Europe to produce modern culinary garden strawberry, Fragaria x ananassa) as seasonal fresh, dried, and fermented foods valued for refreshment and general vitality. By contrast, European Alpine ethnobotanical surveys (Pharmaceutics 2022; PMC9693256) record traditional medicinal preparations of woodland strawberry (Fragaria vesca) relying primarily on dried leaves and rhizomes/roots as astringent teas for mild diarrhea, oral inflammation, or metabolic support, whereas the ripe fruit was eaten mainly as a seasonal culinary food. Distinguishing ripe culinary F. x ananassa fruit from non-fruit leaf/root herbal decoctions is essential for evidence attribution. Neither cultural lineage documents controlled treatment of Parkinson disease symptoms.
