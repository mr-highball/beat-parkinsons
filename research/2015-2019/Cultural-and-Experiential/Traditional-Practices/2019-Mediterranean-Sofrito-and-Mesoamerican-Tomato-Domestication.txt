Title: Mesoamerican tomato domestication and Mediterranean olive-oil sofrito cooking enhancing carotenoid bioaccessibility
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6514867/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2019-04-19
Date basis: Published open-access study date (Molecules 2019 Apr 19; PMC6514867; complemented by tomato glycoalkaloid domestication study Nat Commun 2024-04-03, PMC10991328).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Contextual culinary-chemistry and foodway record: documents traditional Mediterranean slow-cooking of tomatoes with extra-virgin olive oil (sofrito) increasing carotenoid/lycopene isomerization and extractability, alongside Mesoamerican domestication selecting away from toxic steroidal glycoalkaloids in ripe Solanum lycopersicum fruit; no traditional Parkinson treatment claim.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed open-access food-science and plant-domestication articles (Molecules 2019 PMC6514867; Nat Commun 2024 PMC10991328) verified via NCBI PMC during Round 7 review.
Efficacy evidence: Culinary matrix and domestication context explaining why cooked/oil-processed tomato delivers markedly higher bioavailable cis-lycopene than raw tomato while ripe domesticated fruit has minimal tomatine; does not establish clinical PD efficacy.

Peer-reviewed food-chemistry research on traditional Mediterranean culinary technique (Molecules 2019; PMC6514867) demonstrates that home-style sofrito — slow-simmering tomatoes with extra-virgin olive oil, onion, and garlic — disrupts plant cell walls, transfers polyphenols and carotenoids into the lipid phase, and increases Z-isomer (cis) lycopene bioaccessibility compared with uncooked tomato. Complementary genomic work on Mesoamerican tomato domestication (Nat Commun 2024; PMC10991328) documents how indigenous selection acted on distal regulatory elements to dramatically suppress bitter, toxic steroidal glycoalkaloids (such as alpha-tomatine) in ripe red Solanum lycopersicum fruit, distinguishing edible ripe culinary tomatoes from toxic green foliage and unripe wild nightshades. This culinary context helps interpret differences between raw, roasted, and cooked-paste tomato exposures (e.g., MITOS concentrated tomato paste) without implying traditional efficacy for Parkinson disease.
