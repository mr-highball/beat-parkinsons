Title: Indigenous Maori kutai (Perna canaliculus) kaimoana heritage and global ethnomedicinal mollusc anti-inflammatory traditions
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28830818/
Source kind: Traditional-Practice
Publication date: 2018-01-30
Date basis: Publication date of peer-reviewed ethnopharmacological review cataloging global traditional mollusc preparations (J Ethnopharmacol 2018, PMID:28830818), corroborated by Aotearoa New Zealand kaimoana/mahinga kai studies (PMID:33506813; PMID:37442321).
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional coastal dietary staple (kaimoana / mahinga kai) among Maori in Aotearoa New Zealand and source of 104 cataloged traditional anti-inflammatory mollusc preparations across TCM (70), European, Siddha, and Mediterranean traditions; motivated modern clinical joint-pain trials of Perna canaliculus and Mytilus edulis.
Authenticity: Peer-reviewed ethnopharmacology review (Ahmad et al., J Ethnopharmacol 2018, PMID:28830818) and Aotearoa kaimoana harvesting literature (Howells et al. 2021, PMID:33506813; van Hamelsveld et al. 2023, PMID:37442321).
Efficacy evidence: Documents historical cultural use and hypothesis generation for modern arthritis trials; traditional consumption does not establish Parkinson disease efficacy, and wild coastal harvesting requires water-quality monitoring for fecal pathogens and marine biotoxins.

For Indigenous Maori communities in Aotearoa New Zealand, the endemic green-lipped mussel (Perna canaliculus, known as kutai or kuku) has been a vital coastal kaimoana (seafood) and mahinga kai (traditional wild-harvested food) resource for centuries, harvested from intertidal rocks and exchanged inland (Howells et al. 2021, PMID:33506813; van Hamelsveld et al. 2023, PMID:37442321). Globally, an ethnopharmacological review of Materia Medica (Ahmad et al., J Ethnopharmacol 2018, PMID:28830818) cataloged 104 traditional anti-inflammatory mollusc preparations across Traditional Chinese Medicine (70 preparations), European, Siddha, Mediterranean, and African traditions, noting how Maori coastal consumption of P. canaliculus spurred modern development of freeze-dried whole mussel powders and lipid extracts (such as Lyprinol and Biolane). Contemporary European coastal gastronomy similarly relies heavily on farmed steamed Mytilus edulis and M. galloprovincialis (Magnani et al. 2026, PMC13564763). Environmental studies also emphasize that wild-harvested bivalves rapidly bioaccumulate fecal bacteria and heat-stable algal biotoxins if gathered from contaminated or bloom-affected waters.
