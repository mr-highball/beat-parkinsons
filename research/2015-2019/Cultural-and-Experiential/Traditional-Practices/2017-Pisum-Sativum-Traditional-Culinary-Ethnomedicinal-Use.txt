Title: Traditional South Asian and Old World culinary and folk health uses of Pisum sativum (green peas)
Collection: Cultural and experiential
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5368943/
Source kind: Traditional-practice record
Publication date: 2017
Date basis: Peer-reviewed ethnomedicinal study publication year.
Reviewed: 2026-11-26 UTC
Reported direction: Traditional staple legume valued across Eurasia and South Asia for digestible plant nourishment, glycemic moderation, and general vitality; no traditional Parkinson-specific efficacy claim.
Authenticity: Traceable peer-reviewed publications (Zilani et al. 2017, BMC Complement Altern Med, PMC5368943; Rijntjes 2019, Parkinsons Dis, PMC6875167; Jaggi et al. 2025, Sci Adv, PMC12356257).
Efficacy evidence: Traditional culinary and ethnomedicinal background only; does not establish clinical Parkinson symptom effects.

Pisum sativum L. (garden/green pea) is among the oldest domesticated Old World founder crops, historically serving alongside broad beans and lentils as a primary winter and lean-day plant protein across medieval Europe (Rijntjes 2019, PMC6875167) and remaining a widespread culinary staple across Europe, Asia, and the Americas. In South Asian traditional herbal and culinary contexts, edible aerial parts and seeds of Pisum sativum subsp. sativum are traditionally eaten for general nourishment and described in folk medicine as supportive for blood-sugar balance and cardiovascular wellness (Zilani et al. 2017, PMC5368943). Botanical note: Culinary Pisum sativum (green peas) must be strictly separated from Lathyrus sativus (grass pea / khesari dal), a distinct species historically associated with neurolathyrism from beta-ODAP during famines.
