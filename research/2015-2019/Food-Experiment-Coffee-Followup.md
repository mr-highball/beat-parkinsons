# Brewed espresso and fatigue in Parkinson’s disease

Ferreira and colleagues conducted blinded randomized single-person crossover trials in four people with Parkinson’s disease and moderate-to-severe daytime sleepiness. Participants drank regular or decaffeinated espresso two to four times daily during randomized five-day periods, with up to three paired comparisons per participant. Regular Delta Platina supplied 100 mg caffeine per cup and decaffeinated Novadelta Light supplied 2 mg per cup. Daytime sleepiness was the primary outcome. A fatigue visual analog scale was a separate secondary outcome. The 2009 abstract documents that fatigue was already a secondary outcome at that stage. An earlier 2002 abstract likely describes the same trial family and gives sleepiness results only. [Primary report, PMID 27014181](https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2016.00027) · [Local NLM plain text](Source-Text/PMC4782159.txt#L105) · [2009 abstract](../2005-2009/Food-Experiment-Food-Followup.md#brewed-espresso-and-a-secondary-fatigue-scale-in-the-lisbon-trials) · [2002 abstract](../2000-2004/Food-Experiment-Portuguese-Primary-Followup.md#early-lisbon-report-of-regular-and-decaffeinated-espresso)

Mean fatigue scores for regular versus decaffeinated espresso were 26.7 versus 43.7, 26.7 versus 31.7, 48.5 versus 94.0, and 43.0 versus 32.3. One participant had lower scores with regular espresso in all three paired comparisons and a one-sided p value of 0.03. The remaining three comparisons had one-sided p values of 0.19 to 0.26, including one participant with higher mean fatigue on regular espresso. One participant contributed only two complete pairs. The study reported no pooled fatigue effect. The short treatment periods, secondary outcome, participant selection, and potential caffeine withdrawal limit generalization beyond this espresso comparison. [Local source, Table 4 and discussion](Source-Text/PMC4782159.txt#L179)

The experiment’s [claim 0121](../../experiments/1000_see_what_sticks_food/statements/0121.txt) predicts worse fatigue from brewed coffee. The crossover measured fatigue under two brewed espresso regimens. Average fatigue was lower on regular espresso for three participants and higher for one. Both regimens included brewed coffee. The differences concern the two products and their caffeine levels, leaving the effect of brewed-coffee consumption unresolved. The run-in used regular espresso and the washouts substituted decaf. The authors considered caffeine withdrawal a potential influence. A separate survey found that some people used coffee or caffeine to alleviate fatigue, and a caffeine-capsule trial measured fatigue without establishing a clear effect. A later caffeine-capsule trial assessed motor function and adverse events. ## Coffee intake and the presence of levodopa-induced dyskinesia

The FRAGAMP investigators examined self-reported coffee intake and levodopa-induced dyskinesia in an Italian multicenter case-control study. In the investigators’ 2015 conference report, 128 of 485 participants with Parkinson’s had dyskinesia on UPDRS IV item 32. Coffee intake was associated with lower odds of dyskinesia after adjustment (OR 0.50, 95% CI 0.26–0.97). This estimate comes from the investigator conference report, poster PP25. The University of Catania lists the subsequent journal letter, with access to the full PDF restricted. The observational comparison concerns the presence of dyskinesia, with the coffee preparation unspecified. It supplies no fatigue result for [0121](../../experiments/1000_see_what_sticks_food/statements/0121.txt). [Investigators’ conference report, page S199](https://www.accademialimpedismov.it/web/image/28330/AbstractCongressoTorino2015.pdf#page=19) · [Journal letter, PMID 26769461](https://doi.org/10.1002/mds.26459) · [University of Catania record](https://www.iris.unict.it/handle/20.500.11769/28187)

The later randomized Café-PD study used caffeine capsules and reported slightly higher dyskinesia scores in secondary analyses. Its drug formulation and score-based analysis differ from FRAGAMP’s coffee history and binary dyskinesia outcome. [Café-PD primary report, PMID 28954882](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5664303/) · [Earlier CALM-PD cohort analysis](../2010-2014/Food-Experiment-Coffee-Followup.md)

## Dietary caffeine and a composite nonmotor questionnaire

Moccia and colleagues followed 79 newly diagnosed patients over four years. Higher reported total caffeine intake was associated with lower composite Non-Motor Symptoms Questionnaire scores. The abstract expresses the intake increment in espresso-cup equivalents and supplies no fatigue item estimate or beverage-specific coefficient. The university repository restricts the accepted manuscript. The result leaves [0121, brewed coffee and fatigue](../../experiments/1000_see_what_sticks_food/statements/0121.txt) unresolved. [Primary abstract, PMID 27622969](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27622969/) · [University of Naples record](https://www.iris.unina.it/handle/11588/758381)

## First-recorded Parkinson diagnosis in UK Biobank

UK Biobank dates field 131022 to July 2019. It records the date of the first source record mapped to ICD-10 G20. Companion field 131023 gives the source. The date can support an analysis of whether coffee intake and fatigue responses follow a recorded diagnosis. The first coding date can differ from clinical onset, and special values encode missing or invalid event dates. Public metadata show individual field counts. Participant-level coverage across all measures remains unknown. ## Caffeine and creatine in NET-PD LS-1

Simon and colleagues analyzed 1,549 participants with caffeine data from a randomized creatine trial. In the creatine group, higher reported caffeine intake was associated with faster worsening on the total UPDRS (p = 0.002). The placebo group showed no clear progression association. Caffeine intake was reported once and was outside the randomized assignment. The result concerns a possible interaction with creatine on a composite progression scale. Frozen claim `0121` asks about brewed coffee and fatigue. A subsequent genetic analysis reused the LS-1 cohort. [Primary LS-1 analysis, PMID 26366971, Figure 2 and Table 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573899/) · [Genetic reanalysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5386398/)

## Coffee listed as management for infusion-associated hypotension

In a 2017 levodopa–carbidopa intestinal gel study, the adverse-event table lists coffee alongside dose adjustment, salt, and fludrocortisone as management for significant hypotension. In a separate infusion substudy, mean fatigue scores changed from 39.4 to 37.4 (p = .465). The paper does not identify which participants drank coffee, its preparation, or how fatigue changed with coffee exposure. The results give no effect estimate for [0121, brewed coffee and fatigue](../../experiments/1000_see_what_sticks_food/statements/0121.txt). [Primary report, Tables 1 and 3, PMID 28828219](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5561319/) · [Local full text](Source-Text/PMC5561319.txt#L236)

## Coffee history in the Abidjan neurology clinic

Kouassi and colleagues’ 2015 journal report studied 134 neurology attendees in Abidjan, of whom 91 reported drinking coffee. The two Parkinson diagnoses occurred among the 86 participants in the low-intake group. Table III reports p = 0.34 for the comparison with 43 nonconsumers. The study gives no fatigue measure or other within-Parkinson symptom outcome. Two university records dated the underlying work to 2014 and registered their DOIs in November 2025. Their matching design and counts link them to the same study. The 2015 journal issue is the earliest public full report verified here. ## Coffee and yerba mate history in Montevideo

Amestoy and colleagues compared recalled exposure among 128 people with Parkinson’s and 256 controls. Reported coffee exposure was associated with higher odds of Parkinson’s case status (unadjusted OR 2.04, 95% CI 1.21–3.44), while yerba mate was associated with lower odds (OR 0.56, 95% CI 0.33–0.95). The paper found no clear gradient by coffee intensity. The 2016 university monograph and 2017 journal report describe the same sample and estimates. Retrospective exposure, selection, and differences between cases and controls limit interpretation. The measured outcome was case status. There was no fatigue measurement or brewed-coffee comparison for [0121](../../experiments/1000_see_what_sticks_food/statements/0121.txt). [2016 university monograph](https://hdl.handle.net/20.500.12008/32971) · [2017 journal report, Tables 5, 8 and 9](https://www.colibri.udelar.edu.uy/jspui/handle/20.500.12008/30579)

## Coffee-derived chlorogenic acids and morning fatigue

Ochiai and colleagues randomized 16 healthy daytime-working men in a crossover, with 15 included in analysis. The active drink contained 300 mg of chlorogenic acids extracted from green Coffea canephora beans in 100 mL. Each period lasted 13 days and a one-week washout separated periods. Figure 1 reports no clear difference in fatigue on awakening early in week one and lower ratings with the active drink late in week two. The selected outcome concerns waking fatigue after an extract beverage in healthy workers. The 2026 Umeda trial used a distinct parallel-group cohort and dose.
