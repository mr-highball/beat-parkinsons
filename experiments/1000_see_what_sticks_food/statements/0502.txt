ID: 0502
Category: food
Ingredient: date
Preparation: dried
Symptom: pain severity
Direction: reduces
Statement: Consuming dried date reduces pain severity in people with Parkinson's disease.
Verdict: Unknown
Confidence: 50
Rationale: The 21-person whole dried Ajwa crossover reported near-identical abdominal pain diary means, and the pediatric whole-date and mouse comparisons did not clearly separate groups. Date syrups had favorable labor-pain observations with preparation, comparator and population limits. The 34-person date-seed exercise trial reported lower pain at 24 hours (3.35 vs 5.00 on a 0–10 scale, p<.001), with an inconclusive immediate contrast. Its companion biomarkers belong to the same cohort. Parkinson bar studies supply whole-product or composite outcomes without an attributable date-paste and pain comparison. Separate public accounts recall less abdominal pain or more discomfort after date fruit, without an established Parkinson diagnosis or controlled exposure. Extract results are confounded by sedation. No observed study direction transfers defensibly to whole dried-date intake and pain severity in Parkinson disease. The fresh Rutab trial pooled 76 labor-pain categories across arms, and the late-pregnancy whole-fruit comparison reports labor progression without a pain score. Two favorable syrup trials are independent of each other, while the two Khorramabad reports share participants. The later review could not pool a pain estimate. Spanish ethnobotanical and historical sources document intended fruit analgesia and postpartum use without observed patient change or known drying state. None of these sources supplies enough transfer for a dried-date effect on Parkinson pain. In a quasi-experimental study, laboring women allowed dates, orange juice and water had less late-stage pain than a fasting group. Food choice and the ingredient effect were unresolved. Another small report from the same hospital gives labor progress and unstratified analgesic use. The two reports may overlap. The 64-person Persian date-syrup report has the same registration as the 80-person report. Its 30-minute comparison was inconclusive and the 60-minute VAS favored syrup. Five of six control VAS rows match exactly between the companion honey and date reports, leaving participant mapping open. None of these data isolate a whole dried-date effect in Parkinson pain. Newly checked whole-date case narratives describe possible abdominal pain in a family with hereditary fructose intolerance. Two relatives received fructose testing, independently of food exposure. Oral pollen data show less treatment-related mouth pain in sequentially allocated oncology groups. A seed powder case gives a distinct adverse event estimate of 8/10 abdominal pain at presentation. The small Zahidi flesh crossover reports only biomarkers, and the colitis study tests pollen extract in animals. These observations preserve both signs at their actual preparation, endpoint and population. The near-null Ajwa diary comparison and indirect labor, seed and cultural reports leave the dried-fruit Parkinson pain effect open. The complete 2026 pollen original assigns the Figure 2 finding to fecal occult blood. Pain was not a measured outcome. Forty-five new checked publications do not provide dried date fruit exposure for Parkinson pain. The 2015 Unani review documents pain-related indications and a headache caution, with no identified fruit drying state or reported comparable clinical response. The date fruit effect remains open. Thirty-nine newly checked originals do not include dried date fruit as an exposure for clinical Parkinson pain. The earlier human, experimental and cultural source distinctions remain material. Original arthritis and edema reports have distinct edible-fruit extract and seed preparations, with no stand-alone pain severity contrast. The migraine distillate registration still supplies no results. A premenstrual date-fruit trial abstract reports improvement of cramps without a comparative pain-severity estimate, verified drying status or a Parkinson cohort. The reviewing article is a secondary citation trail. Two more original pre-post reports use date-containing drinks together with ginger and lemongrass or with lemongrass and coconut water. They report lower menstrual pain without a concurrent comparison or a verified dried fruit contrast. Table 6 of the original Muzaffargarh survey records orally used fresh date-palm fruit or infusion and lists joint pain among customary uses. The 168 respondents are the full survey, with no per-use count. Individual pain change, dried fruit and Parkinson diagnosis are unreported.
