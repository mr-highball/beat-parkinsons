ID: 0161
Category: spice
Ingredient: cinnamon
Preparation: ground
Symptom: pain severity
Direction: reduces
Statement: Consuming ground cinnamon reduces pain severity in people with Parkinson's disease.
Verdict: Maybe True
Confidence: 50.005
Rationale: Three independent randomized human trials outside Parkinson disease report lower visual-analogue pain with encapsulated oral cinnamon bark powder versus starch placebo in primary dysmenorrhea (n=114, 24-72 h), migraine (n=43 completers, p<.001), and rheumatoid arthritis (n=36, abstract p<.001), alongside a high-attrition multiple-sclerosis trial with baseline imbalance and endpoint switch (VAS vs registered PQAS). Competing controlled trials show null upper abdominal pain for cinnamon-sesame macerate versus sesame oil and inconsistent exercise-soreness findings, while the single Parkinson rehabilitation case combined cassia with multimodal therapy. One oral ground Ceylon cinnamon experiment in female rats found smaller bladder-distension reflexes with induced cystitis and larger responses in uninflamed bladders at the same higher dose. The mixed animal proxy supplies no transferable sign. Differences in culinary ground exposure, non-Parkinson pain etiologies, and reporting limits keep the favorable easing signal at minimal_signal. An identified Cinnamomum burmannii extract in a cellular screen supplies preparation context without a pain-severity or Parkinson observation. Cinnamon is an identification odor in the acute-illness comparison. The paper has no assigned culinary cinnamon pain treatment or attributable pain contrast. The review lists cinnamon as one source of eugenol. Isolated-eugenol trials have chemical, thermal and motor results in rodents, with controlled human oral cinnamon evidence still the closest basis. The new experiment concerns an isolated cinnamon constituent in an animal model with intestinal outcomes. Ground cinnamon and clinical Parkinson pain were not assessed. A traceable Chinese consensus passage recommends a compound cinnamon-twig formula with kudzu as one patterned pain option. Its recommendation supplies no measured result for ground cinnamon in people with Parkinson disease. An extract adjunct trial in H. pylori treatment reports lower short-term epigastric-pain prevalence (p=.02 in Table 2), while a different combined cinnamon leaf tea pilot reports transient colicky pain without a quantified comparison. The contexts and exposures limit transfer to ground bark and Parkinson pain. A separate trial of cinnamon powder mixed in water reports lower menstrual pain than empty capsules in three cycles. The erratum reconciles 107 randomized and 99 analyzed. Unblinded comparison, baseline-table anomalies and a different pain condition keep the existing minimal tier.
