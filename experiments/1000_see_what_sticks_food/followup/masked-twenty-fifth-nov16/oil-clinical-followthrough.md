# P25 clinical follow-through: safflower oil / bowel outcomes; coconut oil / speech

Date checked: 2026-11-16. Scope: official P25 ClinicalTrials.gov query 4 plus full primary human sources closest to the two assigned oil/outcome pairs. No frozen wording or canonical files changed.

## Registry review

P25 `registry/4/registry-query.tsv` records the official API query for safflower oil with constipation/stool terms and related P25 expressions: 7 studies reported and retrieved, one page, one exposure candidate. The only candidate is **NCT06834984**, first posted 2025-02-19: “Ritual Synbiotic+, a Dietary Supplement Designed to Impact Gastrointestinal Health, Mood, and Behavior in Women.” Official current ClinicalTrials.gov v2 record checked directly; source copy retained as `NCT06834984-api.json`.

The record is COMPLETED (actual completion 2026-09-03; record last posted 2026-09-10), but `hasResults=false`; no results tables or appendices are posted. The active treatment is a pre/pro/postbiotic combination (LGG and BB-12 probiotics, phage prebiotic, tributyrin). **Hi-oleic safflower oil is listed only as an ingredient in the placebo capsule** along with the capsule shell, cellulose, ascorbyl palmitate, and silica. This is a concomitant placebo ingredient, not an oil-only active arm.

The registered primary bowel outcomes are:

- Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale at baseline, acute 3 days, 6 weeks, and 12 weeks, including loose/hard stools, increased/decreased stool passage, urgency, and incomplete evacuation.
- Total number and types of bowel movements, recorded daily for 12 weeks; description specifies defecation frequency and abnormal/normal stool proportions using the Bristol Stool Form Scale (1–7; 3–5 stated as healthier).

Planned population is about 100 adult women, age 25–65, BMI <30, with moderate gastrointestinal symptoms; planned treatment for 12 weeks after a one-week lead-in. Actual results are not available, and any future placebo-arm effect would not isolate safflower oil from a capsule placebo. Direct registration: [NCT06834984](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06834984). The P25 registry query 3 for refined coconut oil with speech/dysarthria terms returned 0 studies (0 reported/retrieved); no coconut/speech candidate occurs in the batch’s registry candidate files.

## Primary human source: safflower oil taken orally

Heagerty et al., “Influence of dietary linoleic acid on leucocyte sodium transport and blood pressure,” *BMJ* 1986;293:295–297, DOI [10.1136/bmj.293.6542.295](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.293.6542.295), is a double-blind randomized crossover in 22 healthy normotensive volunteers. Participants took eight 500-mg safflower seed oil capsules daily (4 g oil/day; capsules contained 72% linoleic acid) for 28 days, crossed over after a 28-day washout to identical paraffin capsules. Main outcomes were erythrocyte membrane fatty acids, leukocyte sodium transport, and lying/standing blood pressure; it was not a constipation trial and did not record stool frequency, Bristol type, or constipation severity.

The full two-page BMJ report says some participants felt bloated while taking safflower oil and states that no participant noticed bowel-habit change during the placebo period. It does not give a count or definition of constipation, report an active-arm bowel-frequency estimate, or specify oil refining. Therefore this is oral oil-only versus named paraffin comparator with a nonspecific GI adverse-event observation, not evidence of a constipation effect. The publisher full text was inspected in its official PDF rendering through the web source; direct PowerShell download returned HTTP 403. The complete PDF text is available in the official BMJ record at the DOI link above. No appendix/supplement is attached.

## Primary human sources: coconut oil and speech

**No direct oil-plus-speech intervention was located.** The closest direct dietary-exposure source is Mischley et al., “Role of Diet and Nutritional Supplements in Parkinson’s Disease Progression,” *Parkinson’s Disease* 2017, PMCID PMC5610862, PMID 29081890. It is a pragmatic, prospective observational survey analyzed cross-sectionally at baseline in 1,053 participants with self-reported idiopathic PD. Participants reported recent food intake on a frequency scale; the modeled coconut-oil serving is 1 teaspoon per unit increase in reported frequency. Primary outcome is total PRO-PD composite severity (33 symptoms), adjusted for years since diagnosis, age, gender, and then income—not a voice or speech measure. Table 2 gives coconut-oil-associated predicted PRO-PD change of −18.6 (SE 5.5), p=.001, 95% CI −29.3 to −7.8; adjusted change −20.2 (SE 5.5), p<.000, CI −31.0 to −9.4. Refining grade, precise oil composition, and an administered dose are unspecified. Paper gives no symptom-specific coconut-oil estimates, no item-level voice/speech or constipation result, and no appendices/supplement (four tables in the body). Complete primary PMC XML is retained as `PMC5610862-primary.xml`; official record: [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5610862/).

Koyuncu et al., “Effect of ketogenic diet versus regular diet on voice quality of patients with Parkinson’s disease,” *Acta Neurologica Belgica* 2021;121:1729–1732, DOI [10.1007/s13760-020-01486-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13760-020-01486-0), is the closest PD trial with a voice endpoint, but the exposure is a whole ketogenic diet, not coconut oil/MCT. Seventy-four patients reporting PD-related voice disorder were randomized to ketogenic versus regular diet for three months; 68 completed. The trial used the patient-reported VHI-10, not acoustic, perceptual, articulation, or intelligibility testing. Mean VHI total was 21.2 ± 4.9 to 5.3 ± 1.2 in KD and 22.2 ± 5.1 to 20.9 ± 4.8 in RD; the ten VHI-10 item means improved within the KD group (reported p<.01) while RD scores did not change significantly. The full four-page article was accessible in author-shared text; publisher displays subscription preview. The full text does not mention coconut oil or MCT, provide a specific oil recipe, or include an appendix/supplement. Direct publisher: [Springer article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13760-020-01486-0); full accessible text copy: [author-shared article](https://www.academia.edu/114148434/Effect_of_ketogenic_diet_versus_regular_diet_on_voice_quality_of_patients_with_Parkinson_s_disease).

A related UMIN registry record (not P25 ClinicalTrials.gov exact hit) helps bound MCT/oil specificity: **UMIN000033447 / MCT SMILE** described MCT-oil jelly (15 g oil/package, 6 g MCT) escalated to 18 g MCT/day, compared with jelly in which MCT is replaced by canola oil; 3-month primary outcome was ADAS-Jcog in older adults/MCI, with cognitive/PET/MRI outcomes. It states MCT is included in coconut oil but does not identify coconut as the oil source, and it has no PD voice or speech outcome. The registry says results unpublished. Official record: [UMIN000033447](https://center6.umin.ac.jp/cgi-open-bin/ctr_e/ctr_view.cgi?recptno=R000038140).

## Scope conclusion for Dexter

- Safflower oil appears in an official human bowel-tracking registration only as high-oleic placebo ingredient, with stool outcomes planned and no posted results. An older oil-alone oral supplement trial notes bloating, not a constipation or stool-frequency result.
- Coconut oil has one observational PD association with broad total symptom severity; no published item-level speech/voice outcome is reported. The voice-quality RCT measures VHI-10 under a ketogenic diet, with no coconut/MCT assignment. Neither trial directly tests refined coconut oil or speech intelligibility.
- No primary human source found that isolates either claimed oil and measures the named endpoint.
