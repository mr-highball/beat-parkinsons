## Compound honey syrup

Review Table 5 (local line 184) summarizes the Sadr/Choopani randomized pediatric asthma trial. The original reports 80 randomized and 72 analyzed. Both arms received fluticasone with rescue salbutamol as needed. One arm additionally received a multi-extract honey syrup. The tested plant components were ginger root, cinnamon bark, saffron stigma, cardamom fruit and galangal root. The study measured waking due to asthma, respiratory symptoms, activity limitations due to asthma, rescue medication use and FEV1. The ACQ night item is a reason to link 0218 as very low fit. The experiment did not compare saffron itself, investigate insomnia severity or enroll people with Parkinson disease. The original article does not supply fatigue, general pain, depression, anxiety or falls from the spices. The current publisher identifies the trial as DOI 10.31661/gmj.v6i4.884. Its older manuscript and reference metadata show 10.22086/gmj.v6i3.884 for the same 2017 volume 6 issue 4 article. [Original publisher](https://journals.salviapub.com/index.php/gmj/article/view/884) · [ACQ pediatric validation](https://publications.ersnet.org/content/erj/36/6/1410.full).

## Nigella seed and cumin

The review’s section 12 (lines 244–264) covers `Nigella sativa`, called black cumin. Table 6 (lines 219–222, ref 37) names Javanese turmeric and black cumin blended with honey in a DMBA-induced breast carcinogenesis model. Hidayati’s original names black cumin as `Nigella sativa` and measures tumor and immune outcomes. Ordinary cumin is `Cuminum cyminum`. The other primary Nigella preparation in Table 8 (lines 256–264, ref 45) is topically applied seed oil, including a mixed honey arm and wound-area endpoint. Neither study assesses mood, insomnia or fatigue. The general traditional herbal-honey insomnia statement (line 79) does not specify Nigella. Broad ref 43 discusses CNS observations separately from the examined honey mixtures, including oil/maze rodent anxiety and thymoquinone in nonmatching models. No claim-specific depression, insomnia or fatigue finding was attributed to this review. [Curcuma/Nigella cancer study](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/15/2/371) · [Nigella wound study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30020313/) · [Cited Nigella review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3642442/).

## Sesame oil

Section 14 and Table 10 (lines 296–311, refs 50–52) describe phytochemistry and a sesame oil/camphor/honey treatment in experimental burns. Vaghardoost’s original tracked burn healing, including wound area, epithelialization and vascularization. The wound endpoint does not establish analgesia. The sesame review cited at ref 50 separately traces two primary topical analgesia experiments. Those studies supply a citation route for 0144. Related corroboration includes a topical tahini-oil trial and an oral capsule trial in postpartum pain with unreported processing. These exposure and population differences leave the frozen oral toasted-oil Parkinson pain outcome unresolved. [Original burn study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5890368/) · [Pain trial proposals](../../../../../research/2015-2019/Food-Experiment-Sesame-Oil-Pain-Origins.md).

## Turmeric

Table 2 (line 92) pairs curcumin and several traditional uses, including arthritis. The line supplies no pain measurement. Section 10 and Table 6 (lines 209–222, refs 35–37) focus on `Curcuma xanthorrhiza`/Javanese turmeric and the rat cancer study. This is a species distinct from culinary `C. longa`. The broad ethnobotanical source describes Javanese turmeric for rheumatic pain. A separately located original assessed a standardized ethanolic Javanese turmeric extract and found formalin nocifensive responses lowered but no significant hot-plate or tail-flick effect. That species/form and induced rodent endpoint do not make the honey cancer study a ground-turmeric pain experiment and give no falls data. [Javanese turmeric review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1155/2021/9960813) · [Original Javanese turmeric nociception study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20428088/).

## Ginger

Table 2 (line 93) lists ginger and pain relief, and the Zingiber section (lines 365–376; ref 66) cites Ali and colleagues’ broad pharmacology review for analgesic properties. The honey review itself describes ginger-containing asthma syrup and a distinct multi-ingredient rat study with lipid outcomes. Its general anxiety text and the lavender entry do not describe ginger anxiety results.

The corroborating oral pain trail includes osteoarthritis extracts and repeated oral encapsulated powdered rhizome in experimental muscle pain. These primary studies are documented separately. The related record 0725 routes to that evidence without treating the honey review as an independent tested cohort. The original extraction status, dose and pain contrasts are distinguishable by source. [Pain trial proposals](../../../../../research/2010-2014/Food-Experiment-Ginger-Pain-Origins.md).

## Thyme

The line 396 conclusion names thyme among possible herbs for infusion alongside ginger, turmeric and garlic. There is no thyme intervention in the review's experimental tables. In Table 2, anxiety and sleep uses are assigned to lavender constituents. No experimental anxiety measurement is tied to thyme here.
