## Methods and frailty proxies

The review gives PubMed terms and a ten-year English-language window, with no runnable search, last PubMed date, screening flow, extraction procedure or risk-of-bias scheme. The cited registry URL records one trial. This gives a narrative sampling frame rather than reproducible review coverage. Section 1 (local line 70) says five criteria are required for the frailty phenotype. Fried’s primary study requires at least three of five. Its self-reported exhaustion and slowness are separate components, scored alongside weight loss, weakness and physical activity. Frailty predicts falls in that cohort. A frailty diagnosis and a fall-risk model cannot replace observed falls or establish separate efficacy directions for fatigue, walking slowness, depressive symptoms or rigidity. The Edmonton mood/performance domains and composite MDS-UPDRS scores have a similar attribution limit. [Original frailty phenotype](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11253156/).

The trial anticipated in section 6 (line 228) has now completed. The current NCT06352099 record lists TMT as primary and SPPB, the Hunova Silver Index, HADS, EQ-5D and Modified Fatigue Impact Scale among secondary outcomes. The Silver Index combines gait speed with six other functional measures into a fall-risk score. In the linked 36-person randomized pilot, the abstract reports differences in fall risk and cognition relative to control and no clear difference between doses. It gives no fall-event estimate or fatigue effect in the abstract. Altemor names a different supplement containing micronized diosmin, hesperidin and botanical extracts/proanthocyanidins. Current marketed product metadata lack any of the four frozen spices, and the exact administered lot and full paper outcome tables are unverified. The registration and publication concern older adults and do not identify a Parkinson cohort. [Registry](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06352099) · [Original results](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40694049/) · [Product manufacturer](https://hopeditibra.com/shop/prodotto/altemor/).

## Cinnamon essential oil

At line 214, section 5.1 associates cinnamon and peppermint extract with IL-1 and IL-6, respectively. References 271 and 274 are general book chapters. The verified CRC chapter (ref. 274) offers a general description of essential-oil uses for pain, sleep disorders and tremor, including inhalation. The accessible abstract gives no cinnamon-specific symptom data, and its deposited metadata have no references. The publisher chapter link gave metadata and an abstract. The offered PDF request did not provide the chapter. Preserve 0161 for a specific cinnamon-pain citation and 0889 for a check on whether the chapter references the already catalogued oral cinnamon powder animal motor studies. The other six pair decisions reflect the review’s identified cytokine exposure and the distinct patient or diagnostic contexts, with no implication of efficacy. ## Saffron and oxidative stress

The saffron paragraph (line 220) describes antioxidant properties and references Nassiri-Asl and Hosseinzadeh’s 2020 chapter. Its DOI and all 78 deposited citations were recovered through Crossref. Publisher full text returned HTTP 403. The chapter references the 40-person active-comparator depression trial after coronary intervention and an isolated crocetin rat stress experiment. The former compared saffron capsules and fluoxetine, with an inconclusive between-arm difference and no placebo group. The latter used depression-like animal behavior. This confirms the related citation route for 0063 with no additional evidence direction for ground saffron in Parkinson disease. The checked parkinsonism references concern animal locomotor measures such as induced rotation or fly climbing, and cell/neuronal responses. Retinal/visual studies do not measure olfactory function. The evidence here supplies no fatigue or fall-event data from saffron. The existing 2025 Parkinson-specific Sargol paper postdates this review and remains a separate direct evidence source. [Chapter bibliography](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/B978-0-12-817780-8.00023-2) · [Primary post-PCI trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24289892/) · [Primary crocetin study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29766828/) · [Existing Parkinson trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41439298/).

## Ginger in Parkinson models

Sections 5.1 and 5.2 (lines 214 and 218) cite Angelopoulou et al. (ref. 272). The cited review and primary abstract verify the oral-zingerone experiment co-administered with injected levodopa after 6-OHDA injury, with no evident improvement in levodopa-induced abnormal behaviors and less favorable striatal dopamine outcomes. In a different timing experiment using pretreatment and a different endpoint, preservation of striatal dopamine was reported. The timing and outcome differences preclude assigning a direction to ground ginger and clinical dyskinesia. The cited 6-shogaol model measured bradykinesia-related and coordination behavior, without a separately verified rigidity assessment. An enteric study gave ginger and 6-shogaol in separate gavage groups and measured tight junction, inflammation and enteric dopaminergic outcomes. Its accessible primary abstract gives no stool or transit measure. Newer co-treatment work in a distinct experimental protocol can be assessed under its original source key. These mechanistic routes do not connect the review to separate gastric, pain or olfactory outcomes. [Cited ginger review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9578130/) · [Primary zingerone and levodopa](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11064-011-0548-5) · [Primary zingerone pretreatment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16018576/) · [Primary enteric barrier study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30230979/) · [Primary 6-shogaol motor study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23811724/).

## Curcumin and motor outcomes

Section 5.1 (line 214) cites Turer and Sanlier, whose deposited bibliography explicitly lists Ghodsi et al. The 60-person randomized pilot compared nanomicellar curcumin with placebo alongside dopaminergic treatment. Its aggregate Part III group-by-time comparison was p=.04, with no clearly different individual visits in reported post hoc results or overall clinical/QoL effect. MDS-UPDRS Part III contains rigidity and gait items, but no item-specific results were recovered in the original access trail. The current exact roles 0359 and 0642 are related via that scale and formulation. Donadio’s separate nonrandomized cohort using phospholipid curcuminoids is corroborating aggregate evidence with participant selection and medication differences. It is absent from the retrieved Turer deposited reference list and should not be attributed to this route. Rotarod latency, drug-induced rotation and inflammation cannot be equated with observed falls, Parkinson gait speed, or smell function. [Turer bibliography](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1093/nutrit/nuae079) · [Ghodsi primary report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35636380/) · [Donadio primary report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9210322/) · [Cited rotarod study](https://doi.org/10.1111/cns.13805).

## Curcumin and pain

Section 5.1 (line 214, ref. 238) directly cites Chandran and Goel’s small randomized eight-week rheumatoid-arthritis trial. It assessed visual-analog pain as a secondary outcome using enhanced-bioavailability BCM-95 curcuminoids alone, with diclofenac, or diclofenac alone. The trial had no inactive placebo arm. The pain scores and main inflammatory endpoint belong to rheumatoid arthritis and the tested formulations. This is low-fit related evidence for 0812, not an estimate for the frozen preparation. [Primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22407780/) · [Original paper and Table 5](https://rheumaterx.com/assets/pdfs/cs-curcumin.pdf).
