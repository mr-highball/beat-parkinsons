# Green tea, olfactory outcomes and original-source reviews

Twenty-two verified main bodies supplied 58 exact original-search dispositions. An olfactory-test study improved scores by changing response choices, including cinnamon recognition from 59% to 88%. The tea item was in a familiarity survey and was absent from the final test. The parosmia study used green-tea scent in its final multiscent phase. These are examples of why original exposure and endpoint checks matter even when a record contains both keywords. ## Brewed green tea and smell

Three Europe PMC expressions reconciled 11 appearances over three pages. The ClinicalTrials.gov expression reconciled all 23 records across three pages. The earlier claim screen had 14 pending appearances. Eleven of those were rechecked against original patent texts. They describe formulations, product aroma, or other uses without a clinical comparison of drinking green tea and smell. The patent review leaves the other three appearances at their previous access depth. PENSA's supplementary Table 6 gives the exploratory 12-month smell result. Both arms received the same lifestyle program. The isolated EGCG versus placebo contrast was +0.1 points on UPSIT (95% CI −1.3 to +1.5, p=.853). The population was older adults with subjective cognitive decline. The interval leaves benefit and harm open, with no brewed-tea or Parkinson comparison. The oolong study measured the immediate strength of the beverage's own retronasal aroma after successive sips. Two training trials included a green-tea scent among multiple odors. In the retrospective parosmia study, improvement had already occurred before the green-tea phase. Japanese odor-card studies and Mind Your Nose add measurement context. The latter reports conflicting follow-up coefficients between its narrative and table and leaves the tea variety unresolved. A perioperative registry contains a multi-odor enrichment arm that uses green-tea scent, with results still pending.
