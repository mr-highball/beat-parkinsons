# Historical ginger and constipation transit follow-through

The two 1820 general index objects contain 108 continuously numbered primary page scans. The four cited historical originals add 31 more pages. Page checks separate ginger accounts about gout or rheumatic pain from the neighboring constipation and shaking palsy references. Nineteen exact pairs have full-text outcomes. Five other BMJ records have verified administrative metadata and unresolved page access, leaving 54 pairs open in this group. [First general index](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5613495/) · [Supplemental index](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5613502/)

One historical physician reported ginger easing gout pain while weakness persisted. A second described worse abdominal pain followed by relief after powdered ginger with sago and brandy. An 1817 editorial described an initial worsening and later relief after an infusion in rheumatic illness. The plant described in the 1804 parsley paper is common garden parsley under an older scientific name. Its reported concern is fits, with no measured Parkinson rigidity. [1804 gout letter](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5674314/) · [1808 letter](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5689329/) · [1817 editorial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5611059/) · [1804 botanical description](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5674414/)

The 60-person healthy-adult Daikenchuto trial reports faster emptying in part of the colon with one dose and inconclusive stool outcomes. The registry and W1413 match that study. Horiuchi’s 22-person study and T1829 report pain changes during an add-on formula comparison and no clear change in bowel frequency. A chronic constipation registry fits the site and schedule but has different allocation and enrollment. Its link to that paper remains tentative.
